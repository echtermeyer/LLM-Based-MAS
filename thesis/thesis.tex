\begin{abstract}

A popular way to improve how language models reason is multi-agent debate, in which
several agents discuss a problem over multiple rounds. As such systems move into real
applications, it matters not only whether they reach good answers but also whether
the way they reach them can be trusted. Recent studies raise doubts on both counts,
showing that debate often does no better than a simple vote, as groups agree too
early on what they already share and agents abandon correct answers to follow
confident peers. These failures arise from how the discussion unfolds, yet debate is
mostly judged by outcome measures such as final accuracy. Such measures cannot tell
apart two debates that reach the same answer, one because the group corrected an
early mistake and the other because it simply amplified the answer most of its agents
started with. Studying the discussion itself calls for a theory of how interacting
parts produce collective behaviour, which is exactly what complex systems theory
offers. If debate behaves like other complex systems, its failures should leave
recognisable patterns that explain why it goes wrong and could signal problems early.
We connect this theory to multi-agent debate and introduce Self-Organization Motif
Analysis (\suitename{}), a method that detects four such patterns in debates, from
self-reinforcing positions to emerging leaders.

Applied to $30{,}000$ debates, these patterns reveal a single mechanism, as debate
mostly amplifies the answers agents hold at the start rather than producing new ones.
When we change only how many agents start with the correct answer, for instance, the
group ends correctly more often with each additional correct agent, while it rarely
reaches a correct answer that none of its agents started with. Diverse starting
answers, in turn, make it more likely that the group follows an agent who was right
from the beginning. Since \suitename{} largely works with information that many
debate systems already record, the path to an answer becomes something practitioners
can measure rather than merely assume. Thus, this thesis connects complex systems
theory with multi-agent debate and turns the resulting insights into concrete design
guidance. 

\end{abstract}
