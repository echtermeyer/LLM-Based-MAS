This section reports the empirical results of applying the \suitename{} detectors
to 21{,}000 debates across two benchmarks.
We first characterise overall system performance (\autoref{sec:results:perf}),
then measure each motif in turn: self-reinforcement (\autoref{sec:results:sr}),
bistability (\autoref{sec:results:bistability}), dominance (\autoref{sec:results:dominance}),
and limit cycles (\autoref{sec:results:lc}).
Section~\ref{sec:results:evid} follows with outcome-distribution evidence and a
causal test for the amplifier reading; Section~\ref{sec:results:linguistic} closes
with the linguistic channel.
Each motif section reports detection statistics, structural drivers, and
relationship to outcomes; null and near-null results are reported as findings.

\subsection{System Performance}
\label{sec:results:perf}
This section reports overall system accuracy and efficiency across all
420 task--configuration combinations (35 tasks $\times$ 2 datasets $\times$
3 memory windows $\times$ 2 topologies, $R = 50$ repetitions each,
$N = 21{,}000$ total).
Two metrics are reported throughout:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy}: fraction of repetitions in which the majority vote
        at the final round matches the ground-truth answer.
  \item \textbf{Efficiency}: mean number of rounds until the early-stopping
        criterion fires or the hard cap $T = 15$ is reached.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Single-agent baseline and debate gain.}

To isolate the contribution of debate, we compare the multi-agent outcome
against two baselines defined at round~0, before any discussion takes place:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-agent accuracy}: the fraction of individual agent answers
        that are correct at round~0, averaged across all agents and all
        repetitions.
  \item \textbf{Majority-vote baseline}: the accuracy of the plurality vote
        over all agents at round~0 (ties broken arbitrarily).
        This is the best a voting ensemble can do without any interaction
        and represents the fair single-agent comparison point.
\end{itemize}


\autoref{tab:perf:baseline} summarises the three accuracy levels together
with an oracle upper bound (fraction of repetitions in which at least one
agent holds the correct answer at round~0).

The two datasets tell qualitatively different stories:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GPQA.}
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $36.7\,\%$, and majority voting
        raises this modestly to $39.4\,\%$ by aggregating four independent
        guesses.
        Debate adds a further $\mathbf{+1.3}$\,percentage points (pp),
        reaching $40.7\,\%$.
        This marginal gain is consistent with findings across the multi-agent
        debate literature, where homogeneous agents on knowledge-intensive
        tasks tend to show small absolute accuracy improvements over single-agent
        baselines \citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, liu2025stopovervaluing}.
        The model has reasonable prior knowledge on GPQA questions; debate
        refines but does not transform the outcome.
        Notably, the oracle bound of $68.8\,\%$ reveals that the correct answer
        is present among the agents in roughly two thirds of repetitions: the limiting
        factor is not knowledge but the group's ability to identify and amplify
        the correct voice.

  \item \textbf{HiddenBench.}
        Here the dynamics are strikingly different.
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $15.5\,\%$, already well below
        random.
        Majority voting does not help, it \emph{reduces} accuracy further
        to $8.3\,\%$.
        This is a direct consequence of correlated errors: agents do not fail
        randomly across the options but share a systematic bias, all
        gravitating towards the same wrong answer.
        When three agents independently commit to the same wrong option,
        majority voting amplifies that shared error rather than cancelling it
        out, an LLM analogue of the biased information sampling documented
        in human hidden-profile experiments \citep{stasser1985pooling}.
        Debate reverses this partial collapse: final accuracy reaches
        $29.4\,\%$, a gain of $\mathbf{+21.1}$\,pp over the majority baseline.
        This gain is the substantive effect; in absolute terms $29.4\,\%$ is
        still \emph{at or slightly below} the chance level, because most
        HiddenBench tasks have three options rather than four (31 of 35 tasks,
        $M = 3$, so the repetition-weighted chance level is $\approx 32\,\%$
        rather than $25\,\%$; see below).
        The oracle bound of $46.6\,\%$ confirms that the correct option is
        present among the agents in roughly half of repetitions; debate recovers
        roughly $\frac{29.4 - 8.3}{46.6 - 8.3} \approx 55\,\%$ of the
        accuracy that is in principle recoverable from the majority baseline.
\end{itemize}

Because the chance level differs by option count, we report HiddenBench accuracy
split by $M$.
The $31$ three-option tasks reach $30.3\,\%$ (chance $33.3\,\%$) and the $4$
four-option tasks reach $22.7\,\%$ (chance $25\,\%$); both sit just below their
respective chance lines.
The correct reading is therefore not that debate lifts HiddenBench above chance,
but that it recovers most of the ground lost by the majority-biased starting
point: the starting majority vote is far below chance ($8.3\,\%$), and debate
returns the group roughly to the chance line.

\paragraph{Correlated errors.}
The oracle bounds locate the difference between the benchmarks. On HiddenBench the
oracle ($46.6\,\%$) is close to the independence prediction for four agents at the
observed per-agent accuracy, so the collapse is not a failure to cover the correct
answer but a correlation in \emph{which wrong option} the agents choose, which is
what drives the majority vote down to $8.3\,\%$.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The contrast between GPQA and HiddenBench is not simply a difficulty
difference.
It reflects a qualitative shift in the role of debate.
On GPQA, debate acts as a \emph{refinement}: agents start mostly correct,
and the system consolidates.
On HiddenBench, debate acts as a partial \emph{correction}: agents share a
systematic bias that drives the majority vote well below chance, and structured
discussion recovers most of that deficit without pushing the group clearly above
chance.
The self-reinforcement, bistability, dominance, and limit-cycle analyses in
the following sections explain when and why this correction succeeds or fails.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Topology has no detectable effect on accuracy.}
        Aggregating each task to its mean accuracy and pairing fully connected
        against star across the $35$ tasks, the difference is negligible and its
        $95\,\%$ confidence interval contains zero for both datasets
        (GPQA $\Delta \approx 0.0$\,pp, $p = 0.96$; HiddenBench
        $\Delta = +1.6$\,pp, $p = 0.16$). We find no evidence that the choice of
        topology determines whether the system reaches the correct answer.

  \item \textbf{Topology has a large and significant effect on efficiency.}
        Star topology requires $0.47$ more rounds on GPQA and $1.84$ more
        rounds on HiddenBench compared to fully connected (both $p < 0.001$).
        The hub bottleneck in the star slows convergence: every spoke must
        route information through a single central node, which aggregates
        conflicting signals and commits more slowly.

  \item \textbf{The efficiency cost of star is larger on HiddenBench.}
        On questions where agents disagree more persistently, the hub receives
        more conflicting input per round and takes longer to commit.
        On GPQA, where agents converge quickly regardless of topology, the
        hub bottleneck is less consequential.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Effect of memory window.}
The memory window has essentially no effect on accuracy: task-blocked Friedman
tests are non-significant in three of the four dataset/topology cells, and the lone
exception (HiddenBench/fc, $p = 0.019$) does not survive Benjamini--Hochberg
correction ($q = 0.076$). It does affect convergence speed on HiddenBench, where
larger $W$ converges faster (the smallest window produces the most rounds); on
GPQA, where agents converge in very few rounds regardless of $W$, additional
history provides no useful signal.

\paragraph{Round distributions.}
GPQA converges fast (mode at rounds 2--3; the hard cap is reached in
$\leq 1.6\,\%$ of repetitions), whereas HiddenBench converges slowly and often
fails to converge, with star topologies reaching the cap in up to $14.3\,\%$ of
repetitions against $1.2$--$3.3\,\%$ for fully connected. Star shifts the
distribution right in both datasets, consistent with the mean-rounds differences in
\autoref{tab:perf:topology}.

\paragraph{Rounds and accuracy.}
The relationship between rounds to consensus and accuracy reveals a
striking asymmetry (full plot in Appendix~\ref{app:perf:rounds_acc}).
On GPQA, accuracy peaks at rounds~3--4 ($47.1\,\%$) and is lower both
for the earliest stops at round~2 ($36.1\,\%$) and for longer debates
(around $40\,\%$ or below at most later stopping points, though the
individual late-round buckets are sparse): neither very fast nor very slow
consensus is reliably correct.
On HiddenBench the pattern is opposite: repetitions stopping at round~2
achieve only $6.4\,\%$ accuracy, far below random, because early
consensus means the group has locked in on the majority-biased wrong answer
before any corrective exchange.
Accuracy then rises sharply over the next few rounds ($23.8\,\%$ at round~3,
$31.7\,\%$ at round~4, $33.1\,\%$ at round~5) before flattening and declining at
higher round counts, so the overall trend is non-monotone rather than steadily
increasing (overall Spearman $\rho \approx +0.02$).

This asymmetry is fundamental to understanding the dynamics reported in the
following sections.
On GPQA, self-reinforcement of an early consensus is often adaptive.
On HiddenBench, it is the primary failure mode: agents self-reinforce a
shared bias, and the system reaches confident unanimity on the wrong answer.
Full per-configuration accuracy, efficiency, cap rates, and vote-flip counts
are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:perf:configs}.


\subsection{Self-Reinforcement}
\label{sec:results:sr}

Self-reinforcement (SR) is measured as the OLS slope $\beta_{\mathrm{seg}}$
of agent confidence on round number within each \emph{vote-stable segment}
(a maximal block of $\geq 3$ consecutive rounds where an agent holds the same
vote).
The prevalence $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ is the fraction of segments with
$\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} > 0$; the null is 0.5 (random walk).
Across the full dataset (21\,000 repetitions, both benchmarks, all 6 configs)
the length filter yields 94\,173 segment records.
Of the 84\,000 agent trajectories, 43.5\,\% (36\,531) never flip their vote
across the entire debate and form the extreme SR group; because a debate is
recorded only once it has reached at least three rounds, every one of these
never-flipping trajectories yields a vote-stable segment of length $\geq 3$ and is
retained.\footnote{This agent-level count should not be confused with the ``never flipped'' subgroup
of \autoref{tab:sr:subgroups} ($n = 12{,}748$ segments), which is defined at the
\emph{repetition} level: it collects the segments that belong to the subset of
repetitions in which \emph{no} agent ever flipped (a fully static debate), a
stricter condition than an individual agent holding its vote while others move.
The two numbers therefore measure different things, individual never-flipping
agents versus segments inside fully static repetitions, and are not expected to match.}

\paragraph{Prevalence.}
\autoref{tab:sr:overview} shows $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ and mean $\bar\beta$ for all 12
(dataset, topology, $W$) cells.
$p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ ranges from 0.742 to 0.875 across all cells, well above the null
of 0.5, and in every cell all 35 tasks individually exceed 0.5 (binomial sign test
$p < 10^{-4}$; cluster-bootstrapped 95\,\% CIs exclude 0.5 for $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ and
0 for $\bar\beta$ in all 12 cells).
\textbf{Self-reinforcement is a universal property of this system: it is
present in every configuration and on both benchmarks.}

\paragraph{Configuration effects.}
Configuration effects on $\bar\beta$ are second-order relative to the structural
drivers below. On GPQA $\bar\beta$ \emph{decreases} as $W$ increases (Page trend
test \citep{page1963trend} $p < 10^{-5}$ for both topologies), refuting the
predicted strength-scaling order and pointing to peer re-exposure rather than self
re-exposure as the driver of confidence accumulation. Fully-connected consistently
produces higher $\bar\beta$ than star (effect size 0.04--0.10 confidence points per
round), and on the star topology GPQA exceeds HiddenBench. All these effects are at
most 0.10 in magnitude.

The initial vote distribution is the largest single driver of SR intensity.
Fully unanimous starting configurations (4-0-0-0) produce $\bar\beta = 0.669$;
the fully split case (1-1-1-1) drops to 0.290, a gap of 0.38 confidence
points per round.
Because these segment counts pool repetitions within tasks, we confirm the effect
at the task level: comparing each GPQA task's mean slope under fully unanimous
(4-0-0-0) versus fully split (1-1-1-1) starts, where both occur
(task-paired Wilcoxon, $n = 21$), gives a median difference of $+0.42$
($p < 10^{-4}$). This driver is substantially larger than the topology or
$W$ effect (at most 0.10).
Segments inside fully static repetitions, where no agent ever flips, accumulate the
highest slopes of any subgroup
($\bar\beta = 0.698$, $p_{\mathrm{SR}} = 0.910$) and represent the extreme
case where SR runs unchecked for the full debate.

\paragraph{Efficiency.}
\autoref{fig:sr:efficiency} shows task-level mean rounds plotted against
mean $\bar\beta$ for all (dataset, topology) panels.
The Spearman correlation between $\bar\beta$ and mean rounds to consensus is
negative in all 12 cells; computed at the task level ($n = 35$ tasks per cell)
it ranges from $\rho = -0.77$ to $-0.91$ ($p < 10^{-6}$ throughout).
Higher SR is consistently associated with \emph{fewer} rounds across all
configurations.
This finding is the clearest and most consistent result in the SR analysis:
\textbf{self-reinforcement is the convergence mechanism of the debate.}

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
\autoref{fig:sr:accuracy} shows $\bar\beta$ against task accuracy.
\emph{Marginally}, the point-biserial correlation between SR and correctness
is near zero in all 12 cells: $r_{pb}$ ranges from $-0.007$ to $+0.052$
at the repetition level, and none of the GPQA cells reach significance
($p > 0.3$ in all six).

The marginal flatness is, however, a consequence of conditioning on the full
dataset, not a substantive null result.
When repetitions are split by whether the ground-truth option appeared in the
initial vote distribution, the picture reverses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GT absent from initial votes} (31\,\% of GPQA reps,
        53\,\% of HiddenBench reps): SR is \emph{negatively} associated with
        accuracy ($r_{pb} = -0.11$ on GPQA, $-0.04$ on HiddenBench).
        At the task level the GPQA effect survives (cluster-bootstrap $95\,\%$
        CI $[-0.16, -0.06]$), whereas the HiddenBench effect does not
        (CI $[-0.08, +0.01]$, which includes zero). When no agent starts on
        the correct answer, faster lock-in means the system converges on a
        wrong answer sooner.

  \item \textbf{GT present in initial votes}: SR is \emph{positively}
        associated with accuracy ($r_{pb} = +0.20$ on GPQA, $+0.18$ on
        HiddenBench; both $p < 10^{-10}$).  When the correct answer is
        already represented, stronger reinforcement amplifies the correct
        initial position.
\end{itemize}

These two effects are of similar magnitude and opposite sign, cancelling in the
marginal to produce the near-zero $r_{pb}$ observed across the full sample; a
linear probability model confirms a significant SR\,$\times$\,GT-presence
interaction of the same sign in both datasets ($p < 10^{-37}$).

A further analysis shows that high-SR repetitions amplify the initial vote
distribution more strongly: the Pearson correlation between initial GT
fraction and accuracy is $r = 0.86$ in the high-SR half versus $r = 0.54$
in the low-SR half (GPQA; HiddenBench: $0.59$ vs.\ $0.42$).
\textbf{SR does not select for correct answers; it amplifies the initial
vote composition.}
Whether this helps accuracy depends entirely on whether the initial
distribution already favours the correct option, a point the high-repetition
analysis also establishes independently
(\autoref{sec:results:evid}).

Also notable: confidence gain is essentially independent of correctness.
Mean confidence rise from round 0 to the final round is 1.66 points (correct)
vs.\ 1.64 points (incorrect) on GPQA and 1.51 vs.\ 1.48 on HiddenBench.
At the task level the correct-versus-incorrect difference is not significant
on GPQA (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.10$, with an unstable sign) and only marginal
on HiddenBench ($p = 0.004$, magnitude $\approx 0.05$ points).
The system grows more confident regardless of whether its final answer is
right or wrong.

\subsection{Bistability}
\label{sec:results:bistability}

Bistability is measured per (task, config) cell using two complementary
signatures: $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (inverse Simpson diversity over endpoint
attractors, measuring coexistence) and Cramér's $V$ with a permutation
$p$-value (measuring whether the initial-vote distribution selects the
attractor, i.e.\ basin sensitivity).
A repetition is eligible if it settles (trailing-constant $k \geq 3$) on a
unanimous endpoint; non-unanimous-split endings are excluded
(2.12\,\% of reps).
Cells are labelled \emph{monostable} ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} < 1.5$),
\emph{multistable} ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5$ and $p_{\mathrm{basin}} < 0.05$),
or \emph{stochastic} ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5$ but $p_{\mathrm{basin}} \geq 0.05$).

\paragraph{Convergence accounting.}
Of 21\,000 repetitions, 98.6\,\% settle within the hard round cap and
96.5\,\% converge to a unanimous attractor.
No cell has fewer than two converged repetitions, so all 420 cells are
classified.

\paragraph{Prevalence.}
\autoref{tab:bistab:overview} summarises the label distribution.
Of the 420 cells, 90 (21.4\,\%) are monostable, 176 (41.9\,\%) are
multistable, and 154 (36.7\,\%) are stochastic.
$N_{\mathrm{eff}} > 1.5$ in 330 of 420 cells (78.6\,\%);
the overall mean is 2.06 (median 2.01).

The stochastic class deserves comment: its mean $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is 2.31, so
these are not marginal cases but exhibit clear multi-attractor coexistence.
Stochastic cells are \emph{not} monostable, they exhibit genuine
multi-attractor coexistence at roughly the same $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ level as
multistable cells (mean 2.27), but the final attractor does not depend
deterministically on the initial-vote composition.
The distinction between multistable and stochastic is therefore about
\emph{basin sensitivity}, not about the number of attractors.

\paragraph{Configuration effects.}
Neither topology nor memory window produces a significant or consistent effect on
either metric: at the task level all topology comparisons have confidence intervals
containing zero (smallest Mann--Whitney $p = 0.16$, none surviving correction) and
the memory-$W$ effect is non-significant in all eight strata (per-configuration
heatmaps in Appendix~\ref{app:bistab}).
\textbf{Bistability is, to the precision we can measure, topology- and
memory-invariant in this system.}


\paragraph{Task-level consistency.}
The question of whether bistability is a property of the task or a config
artefact is answered by the pairwise Spearman correlation of per-task
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ across the six configurations.
For GPQA the mean pairwise $\rho = 0.913$ (min 0.869, max 0.941);
for HiddenBench $\rho = 0.782$ (min 0.597, max 0.880).
Of the 35 GPQA tasks, 17 are labelled multistable in $\geq 4$ of the 6
configs, and 9 HiddenBench tasks meet this criterion.
\textbf{Bistability is a stable property of specific tasks, not a config
artefact.}

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ does not predict accuracy in either dataset: the task-level
Spearman is non-significant on both GPQA ($\rho = +0.04$, $p = 0.81$) and
HiddenBench ($\rho = +0.14$, $p = 0.43$), as is the partial correlation controlling
for initial-vote diversity. Label-wise, HiddenBench stochastic cells have the lowest
mean accuracy and multistable the highest, but a task-clustered bootstrap of the
difference straddles zero ($p \approx 0.06$), so the apparent penalty on
random-convergence cells is at most a marginal trend.


\paragraph{Correct-basin depth.}
A notable finding concerns the association between attractor correctness and basin
selection strength. Restricting to a fixed option count to keep Cramér's $V$
comparable, among the HiddenBench three-option ($M = 3$) cells those whose dominant
attractor is the correct answer have substantially higher $V$ than those whose
dominant attractor is wrong ($0.435$ vs.\ $0.333$); aggregating to the task level, a
cluster bootstrap over the $31$ tasks gives a $V$ difference of $0.102$ ($95\,\%$ CI
$[0.03,\ 0.18]$, $p = 0.005$), so the effect survives task clustering. On GPQA
($M = 4$) the difference is negligible. Correct attractors therefore exhibit
stronger basin selection on HiddenBench: the initial-vote composition predicts the
outcome more reliably when that outcome is correct. The ordering is unchanged under
bias-corrected $V$.

\paragraph{Cross-motif: SR and bistability.}
Monostable cells have the highest mean SR slope (0.555), followed by
multistable (0.412) and stochastic (0.370); this ordering is consistent
across configurations.
At the task level ($n = 35$) the association between $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and
mean SR slope is strong and significant on GPQA ($\rho = -0.79$,
$p \approx 0$) but does not reach significance on HiddenBench
($\rho = -0.26$, $p = 0.13$).
This negative relationship has a natural interpretation: SR is the
within-basin amplification mechanism.
In cells where one attractor dominates (monostable), agents lock in early
and accumulate steep confidence ramps.
In multistable cells the system splits across attractors, different
agent subgroups lock into different options, so the SR signal is
diluted across multiple trajectories.
Bistability and self-reinforcement are therefore complementary at the
system level: strong SR $\to$ fast within-basin convergence;
high $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ $\to$ the system visits multiple basins across
repetitions.

\subsection{Dominance}
\label{sec:results:dominance}

Dominance is measured via the Herfindahl index $D$ over the
confidence-weighted influence graph: how concentrated is the total
conversion credit among agents?
$D = 0$ means influence is distributed equally; $D = 1$ means one agent
causes all opinion changes.
The surrogate null randomises the source identity within each neighbourhood,
testing whether concentration exceeds what the topology alone would produce.
The star topology's null is degenerate for 73--87\,\% of repetitions
(leaf conversions are forced onto the centre), so \emph{emergent excess}
($z$-score and flagged fraction) is only identified for the
fully-connected topology; raw $D$ is reported for both.

\paragraph{Accounting.}
Of 21\,000 repetitions, 17\,801 (84.8\,\%) involve at least one vote
change and have a well-defined $D$.
The 3\,199 excluded reps (15.2\,\%) converged unanimously from round 0
or had no opinion changes; they carry no dominance information.
Of the valid fc repetitions, 72\,\% (GPQA) and 83\,\% (HiddenBench) have
a non-degenerate null and contribute to the $z$ analysis.

\paragraph{Topology dominates raw D.}
\autoref{tab:dom:overview} shows the $D$ distributions.
Star topology produces 3.1--4.1$\times$ higher $D$ than fc across all
(dataset, $W$) combinations (paired Wilcoxon, all $p < 10^{-10}$).
This is partly mechanical: the star's centre is the sole eligible conversion source
for every leaf, so its influence share is structurally inflated and should not be
over-interpreted as a behavioural finding.

Even without a structural hub, fc debates produce influence concentration
above the topology-keyed null.

($\bar{z} \approx 0.80$), suggesting that longer context windows allow
a de-facto leader to emerge even when all agents are topologically
equivalent.

a structural consequence of the star topology.}

\paragraph{Memory window effect.} $W$ has a small significant effect on $D$ for HiddenBench only ($\Delta D \approx +0.02$--$0.03$, paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.003$ for both topologies); GPQA is flat. We read this as a modest entrenchment effect at larger context windows, not a robust design lever.

\paragraph{Hub concentration and correctness.}
In fc the hub (highest-influence agent) holds on average 42--45\,\% of total
influence share (GPQA $0.42$, HiddenBench $0.45$); in star it holds 76--79\,\%.
Because the group converges onto the dominant agent's position, the rate at which
the hub holds the correct answer tracks the group's final accuracy; the substantive
question is whether that rate beats the $1/M$ chance baseline. On GPQA it does
(task-level, one-sided $p = 0.003$); on HiddenBench it is at or below the $1/3$
baseline ($31.7\,\%$, not significant), so the dominant agent on HiddenBench is not
systematically the better-informed one.

\paragraph{Hub identity stability.}
The hub role is not permanently attached to a specific agent but shifts across
repetitions (dominant agent wins 33--49\,\% of reps). Yet the dominant hub, when it
recurs, is more likely to hold the correct answer on GPQA (40.5\,\% vs 25\,\%
baseline; task-level $p \approx 0.01$) and at chance on HiddenBench, suggesting a
question-specific expertise signal partially determines the hub on GPQA while hub
assignment is random with respect to correctness on HiddenBench.

\paragraph{Dominance and accuracy.}
Tested at the task level, higher $D$ predicts \emph{higher} accuracy on
HiddenBench/fc ($\rho = +0.44$, $p = 0.008$, survives correction) but \emph{lower}
accuracy on HiddenBench/star ($d = -0.051$, $p = 0.010$, survives correction); GPQA
is at most marginal and does not survive correction. Dominance is therefore not
uniformly benign: a decisive leader helps when every agent sees every other (fc) but
hurts when influence is forced through a single hub (star), where concentration
entrenches the shared initial bias among equally uninformed agents.

\paragraph{Conversion structure.}
$D$ correlates strongly with the fraction of total conversion weight attributed to the single most influential source agent (task-level Spearman $\rho = 0.85$ on GPQA, $0.84$ on HiddenBench). Notably, $D$ is \emph{negatively} correlated with total conversion volume ($\rho = -0.34$ to $-0.56$): high-$D$ repetitions involve fewer total opinion changes, concentrated on one agent---a single persuasive cascade, not convergence by attrition.

\paragraph{Independence from bistability.}
$D$ is uncorrelated with $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in both datasets
($\rho = -0.07$ to $+0.01$, all $p > 0.3$) and does not differ
significantly across bistability labels (monostable/multistable/stochastic).
Dominance and bistability operate on orthogonal dimensions: one describes
\emph{who} drives opinion change within a repetition; the other describes
\emph{how many attractors} the system visits across repetitions.




\subsection{Limit Cycles}
\label{sec:results:lc}

Limit cycles are the rarest motif in the dataset.
The cooperative debate design strongly suppresses sustained oscillation:
99.4\,\% of agent vote sequences and 98.6\,\% of system composition sequences
converge to fixed points and are excluded before any test is run.
\autoref{tab:lc:funnel} shows the full detection funnel.
Of the small fraction that fail to converge (candidates), 6.3\,\% (agent)
and 7.9\,\% (system) carry a statistically significant periodic signature,
yielding \textbf{29 agent-level} and \textbf{23 system-level} limit cycles
across the dataset.
Both counts are well above the null floor. Assessed at the repetition level
(the unit that is independent under the null, since star spokes driven by a
common hub are not independent successes), the agent-level excess gives
$p \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-7}$ (17 flagged repetitions spanning 15 tasks) and
the system-level excess $p \approx 2 \times 10^{-16}$: the flagged cases are
genuine periodic structure, not test artefacts.

\paragraph{Topology.}
The star topology dominates: 27 of 29 agent-level and 18 of 23 system-level
flagged cases occur in star configurations.
All 52 flagged cases (both topologies) were inspected under the same fixed
criteria used in the oscillation taxonomy below, a case counts as a genuine
period-2 oscillation only if it shows a clean alternation with $\hat{P} = 2$,
$DET \geq 0.70$, and no dominant long hold, so the classification is not applied
selectively to the fc cases. Under these criteria the 7 fc cases (2 agent + 5
system) are all stable-revisit or near-convergent artefacts (large uniform blocks
from vote persistence, no periodic diagonal stripes), whereas the majority of star
cases pass.
\textbf{Only the star topology produces genuine limit cycles}, the same
conclusion as in the qualitative analysis of the mechanism described below.

\paragraph{Dataset.}
Flagged cases concentrate in HiddenBench: 24 of 29 agent-level, 21 of 23
system-level.
GPQA contributes a notable cluster: 5 agent flags all arise from two questions
(qid~90 and qid~98, both star/$W{=}2$).
The qid~90 / rep~43 case is the clearest collective cycle in the new dataset:
all four agents flag as genuine period-2 oscillation simultaneously
(DET ranging from 0.86 to 1.00), indicating full group synchrony.

\paragraph{Oscillation taxonomy.} Among the 29 flagged agent cases, 20~(69\,\%) are genuine period-2 oscillations ($DET \geq 0.70$, $\hat{P} = 2$, no dominant long hold); the remaining 9 are near-convergent or higher-period artefacts. At the system level, 7 of 23 cases are genuine period-2 oscillations.

most informative cases.

all four agents alternate between votes B and C in near-perfect synchrony
for all 15 rounds.
The right panel (HiddenBench/star/$W{=}5$/q20/rep~45) shows a hub-driven
cycle: the group starts fragmented, the hub (agent~0) locks into an

\paragraph{Cross-level co-occurrence.} Six repetitions are flagged simultaneously at both levels; five are HiddenBench/star and the sixth is the GPQA/star qid~90/rep~43 collective cycle, consistent with hub-driven cycles cascading to the system level within one or two rounds.

\paragraph{Mechanism.}
Inspection of the flagged cases suggests a consistent causal picture, stated as a
hypothesis rather than a certified mechanism. Every limit cycle runs to the hard
round cap: they are a pure efficiency failure in which early stopping never
triggers. The proposed chain has five interacting conditions: (1) unconditional
social deference (agents abandon their own \arguephase{} argument in the
\updatephase{} phase upon observing a peer majority); (2) the hub's broadcast power
reconstructs the majority for the opposite option after each flip; (3) arguments
neither escalate nor resolve the evidence ambiguity across rounds; (4) the question
offers two independently defensible evidence threads; (5) the hub begins in a
minority or tie. When all five hold, each flip recreates the conditions that caused
it. The absence of genuine limit cycles in fc follows from condition~(2): no single
node aggregates all peer signals at once, so the inversion loop cannot form.

\paragraph{Accuracy and design implications.}
Because every LC repetition runs to the hard cap without reaching consensus,
the group's final majority vote is a low-confidence artefact of wherever
the oscillation happens to land on round~15.
LC prevalence is too low for reliable inferential comparison, but the
structural implication is clear: star topology is the only configuration in
this system capable of producing genuine limit cycles, and a hard round cap
is the only reliable stopping condition for those cases.
Flagging repetitions that reach $T$ as unresolved, rather than returning a
spurious majority, is the recommended system response.

\subsection{Outcome Distributions and Causal Evidence}
\label{sec:results:evid}

The four motif analyses characterise the \emph{dynamics} of debate.
This section asks a complementary question: does the opening vote composition
causally determine the outcome, or is that association confounded?
Two experiments address it in order: a within-task distribution analysis at
$R = 1{,}000$ repetitions that establishes the observational pattern, followed
by a seeded-initialisation experiment that confirms the causal interpretation.

For the observational analysis, three individual GPQA questions (q84, q125, q144),
selected for mid-range accuracy and high initial-vote entropy to ensure genuinely
uncertain outcomes, were each run for $R = 1{,}000$ repetitions at $W = 2$,
fully-connected, $N = 4$. The aim is to characterise the outcome distribution across
many repetitions of the same task and identify which properties of the initial vote
composition predict accuracy and convergence speed.

For each repetition we extract \emph{initial-state features} observable before any
debate (initial vote entropy $H_0$, initial plurality size, and binary indicators
for whether the ground-truth option is present in any initial vote and whether it
holds the initial plurality) and \emph{trajectory signatures} recorded post-hoc
(total vote flips and confidence change).

\paragraph{Ground-truth presence in the initial vote distribution.}
The dominant finding is stark: whether the ground-truth option appears at all in
the initial vote distribution is by far the strongest predictor of final accuracy,
consistent across all three tasks
(\autoref{fig:highrep:init_votes_split}, \autoref{tab:highrep:init_votes}).

When the correct answer is absent from all initial votes, the system almost never
reaches it: accuracy is $0.4$\,\%, $3.1$\,\%, and $6.9$\,\% for q84, q125, and
q144 respectively ($\chi^2 > 120$, $p < 10^{-27}$ in all cases).
The rare correct answers in the absent condition are consistent with stochastic
noise rather than genuine recovery.
Within the present condition, accuracy is roughly $2\times$ higher when the correct
option also holds the initial plurality than when it is present but not the
plurality (e.g.\ $0.620$ vs $0.257$ for q84, the latter being the present-but-not-plurality
subgroup, i.e.\ the $743 - 213 = 530$ repetitions where the correct option is in the
opening profile but does not lead it).
These findings suggest that the system operates primarily as a vote amplifier:
debate selectively strengthens options already represented in the initial
distribution and overwhelmingly reinforces whichever option holds the initial
plurality.
The capacity to surface a correct answer that no agent endorsed at the outset is
negligible under the present design.

\paragraph{Initial vote entropy and efficiency.} Higher initial-vote entropy $H_0$ is associated with longer debates in all three tasks. Accuracy is not monotone in $H_0$: it is largely a confound with the probability that the ground truth is present in the initial votes. Within the GT-present subset, higher entropy places the correct option in a weaker competitive position. The full per-$H_0$ breakdown is in Appendix~\ref{app:highrep}.

\paragraph{Debate length, turbulence, and confidence change.}
Longer debates do not reliably produce more accurate outcomes: accuracy is
negatively correlated with rounds for q144 ($r = -0.19$), near zero for q84, and
only slightly positive for q125. Vote flips and rounds are tightly coupled
($r = 0.83$--$0.92$): turbulent trajectories with many opinion changes are the same
repetitions that fail to reach fast consensus. Confidence increases monotonically
across rounds regardless of outcome (mean gain $\approx 1.6$--$1.9$ points) and
carries at most a negligible predictive signal for correctness ($|r| < 0.09$); the
confidence-change distributions for correct and incorrect repetitions overlap almost
perfectly, confirming that confidence gain is a property of the debate process
itself rather than a signal of outcome quality.
Full feature-by-outcome correlations are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:highrep}.

\paragraph{Causal test: seeded initial conditions.}
\label{sec:results:seeded}

The preceding analysis is observational: whether the ground truth is present at
$t=0$ co-varies with task difficulty, agent confidence, and initial entropy, so its
apparent effect on accuracy could be confounded.
To isolate the causal contribution of the initial vote composition, we ran a
controlled intervention on $34$ GPQA tasks (the same $W=2$,
fully-connected, $N=4$ configuration, $R=50$ repetitions each).
These are GPQA Tier-3 tasks from the same task population as the main study (the
three high-repetition tasks are among them); one of the $35$ could not supply a
response pool rich enough to construct both conditions and is excluded, leaving $34$.
For each task we first collected a pool of genuine, independent single-agent
responses, then constructed the initial four-agent vote profile of each repetition by
sampling from that pool under one of two constraints:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{init\_0} (ground truth absent): exactly zero of the four agents
        begin on the correct option;
  \item \textbf{init\_1} (ground truth seeded): exactly one of the four agents
        begins on the correct option, i.e.\ the ground truth is present but as a
        $1/4$ minority.
\end{itemize}
Only the \emph{composition} of the starting votes is manipulated; every initial
vote is a real agent response, and the debate that follows is unmodified.
The contrast between the two conditions therefore measures the causal effect of
placing the ground truth into the initial distribution, and the init\_0 arm
separately measures the system's capacity to \emph{generate} the correct answer de
novo when no agent starts with it.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig4_seeded_causal.png}
  \caption[Causal effect of seeding the ground truth]{Causal effect of seeding the ground truth into the initial vote
           distribution.
           (a) Per-task accuracy for all $34$ tasks, paired: grey = init\_0
           (ground truth absent), blue = init\_1 (ground truth seeded as a $1/4$

The effect is large and unambiguous (\autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_causal}).
Pooled over $1{,}700$ repetitions per condition, seeding a single correct agent raises
accuracy from $1.8\,\%$ (init\_0) to $23.7\,\%$ (init\_1), a roughly $13\times$
increase (pooled two-proportion $\chi^2 = 364$; we treat the task-paired test
below as the primary one).
The lift holds in \emph{every} task: init\_1 exceeds init\_0 in all $34$ of $34$
tasks (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank $p = 3.6\times10^{-7}$, median per-task lift
$0.18$).
This confirms causally what the $R=1{,}000$ analysis showed observationally: the
presence of the correct option in the opening vote profile is the single largest
determinant of whether the system arrives at it.
At the same time, init\_0 accuracy is not exactly zero.
The correct answer was recovered de novo in $15$ of $34$ tasks
($31$ repetitions in total), so the debate does possess a weak generative channel rather
than acting as a pure amplifier.

\paragraph{De-novo recovery is bounded by task difficulty.}
That generative channel is not a general reasoning capacity: it fires only on tasks
that are already easy, and vanishes on the hard ones.
We measure difficulty independently of the debate by the task's single-agent
accuracy $\mathrm{acc}_1 = n_\text{correct}/200$, the correct fraction of the fixed
pool of $200$ independent single-agent responses from which the initial votes are
drawn (identical across both conditions).
This index measures intrinsic difficulty rather than an artefact of the seeding
procedure: for the three $R=1{,}000$ tasks it matches the naturally observed $t=0$
per-agent accuracy almost exactly.
De-novo recovery rises monotonically with $\mathrm{acc}_1$ across the $34$ tasks
(Spearman $\rho = 0.51$, $p = 2.3\times10^{-3}$;
\autoref{fig:highrep:recovery_difficulty}), and the dependence is stark at the
hard end: the hardest quartile of tasks (single-agent accuracy $0.5$--$12.5\,\%$)
recovers the ground truth in \emph{zero} of its $400$ init\_0 repetitions.
Concretely, on the biased, low-accuracy tasks, exactly those where independent
reasoning would be most valuable, the system never manufactures the correct answer
that no agent initially held; it only rediscovers answers that are intrinsically
defensible to begin with.

\paragraph{Convergence dynamics: convert-all or capitulate-all.}
Both conditions reach fully unanimous final states in $100\,\%$ of repetitions, so the
seeded correct agent has a strictly binary fate: it either converts the entire group
($23.7\,\%$ of init\_1 repetitions) or capitulates completely, with no repetition
ending on the ground truth as a surviving minority. When the minority does win it
does so early, climbing to a plurality within the first one to two rounds, and
de-novo recoveries in init\_0 appear equally early (mean first-appearance round
$1.45$). Persuasion in this system is front-loaded: the opening exchange, not
protracted deliberation, decides the outcome, echoing the earlier finding that
longer debates do not produce better answers.

\paragraph{Does the intervention match observation?}
The intervention is broadly consistent with the observational data: for the three
$R=1{,}000$ tasks, seeded init\_1 accuracy is directionally consistent with the
naturally-occurring ``exactly one correct minority vote'' subset, though it runs
somewhat below the observed value in all three, consistent with the seeded pool
enforcing an exact $1/4$ minority rather than sampling the natural minority
configurations. Within init\_1 the correct agent wins more often against a split
opposition than against a unified wrong bloc ($32.3\,\%$ vs.\ $19.5\,\%$ pooled),
but this difference is not significant at the task level.


Taken together, the seeded experiment upgrades the central claim of this section
from association to intervention: the multi-agent system functions predominantly as
a \emph{selective amplifier} of its initial vote distribution.
Seeding the correct answer as a lone minority multiplies accuracy roughly $13$-fold,
yet even then the correct agent is out-voted three times out of four, and the system
almost never manufactures a correct answer that no agent initially held.
The composition of the opening votes, not the length or intensity of the debate, is
the dominant lever on whether collective reasoning succeeds.
This intervention was run on GPQA only; the observational ground-truth-presence
result holds on both benchmarks (\autoref{sec:results:sr}), but the causal claim
itself is established here for GPQA, and its extension to HiddenBench is discussed as
a limitation in \autoref{sec:discussion:limitations}.

\subsection{Linguistic Dimensions}
\label{sec:results:linguistic}

The four motifs above are read from the discrete vote and the numeric
confidence.
This section examines the complementary textual channel: each public message is
scored on three ordinal $1$--$5$ dimensions, \emph{conviction} (how certain
the message sounds), \emph{anchoring} (whether the stated belief source is the
agent's own reasoning or deference to peers), and \emph{dialogic orientation}
(engagement with a named peer), as defined in
\autoref{sec:method:linguistic}.
Because the scorer is an instance of the same model family as the debate agents,
these scores are used only for \emph{relative} comparisons and never as an
absolute measure of argument quality; the associations below are reported at the
task-cell level (420 cells) with rank statistics throughout.

\paragraph{Level effects are weak; diversity is the signal.}
The mean level of each dimension carries little information about system behaviour:
across all 420 cells the Spearman correlations between a cell's mean dimension score
and its MAS metrics are uniformly small (for accuracy all three $|\rho| \leq 0.11$;
the largest, conviction vs.\ mean rounds, is $\rho = -0.30$). Dataset differences in
level are modest and the hub and leaf roles in the star topology are nearly
indistinguishable. The informative quantity is not how the agents talk on average
but how \emph{differently} they talk from one another within a repetition.

\paragraph{Stylistic diversity predicts faster convergence.}
For each repetition we compute the between-agent standard deviation of each
dimension, a measure of within-repetition stylistic diversity, and correlate it with
outcomes at the cell level (\autoref{tab:ling:diversity}).
All three diversity measures are strongly and negatively associated with mean rounds
to consensus ($\rho = -0.51$ to $-0.55$, all $p < 10^{-10}$): repetitions in which
agents adopt visibly different argumentative styles resolve \emph{faster} than
repetitions in which they sound alike. The effect holds within each dataset
separately, so it is not an artefact of pooling two benchmarks with different base
rates.

\paragraph{Interpretation and its limits.}
The efficiency association connects to the self-reinforcement finding: when agents
argue in a uniform style there is little friction to resolve, and the group locks
into whichever position it already leans toward, the textual shadow of within-basin
self-reinforcement (fast but not necessarily correct convergence); stylistically
heterogeneous groups instead sustain genuine back-and-forth that lengthens the
debate. The accuracy side does not generalise: diversity is mildly beneficial on
GPQA but mildly harmful on HiddenBench, buying deliberation time without a
consistent accuracy payoff. Two caveats bound the result: the scorer shares a model
family with the agents (so only relative, rank-based associations should be
trusted), and these are cell-level correlations, not interventions, so the direction
of causation is not established. We therefore report stylistic diversity as a weaker
diagnostic correlate of convergence dynamics, not an independent design lever.
