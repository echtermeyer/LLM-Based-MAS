\subsection{System Performance}
\label{sec:results:perf}

This section reports overall system accuracy and efficiency across all
420 task--configuration combinations (35 tasks $\times$ 2 datasets $\times$
3 memory windows $\times$ 2 topologies, $R = 50$ repetitions each,
$N = 21{,}000$ total).
Two metrics are reported throughout:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy}: fraction of repetitions in which the majority vote
        at the final round matches the ground-truth answer.
  \item \textbf{Efficiency}: mean number of rounds until the early-stopping
        criterion fires or the hard cap $T = 15$ is reached.
\end{itemize}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Single-Agent Baseline and Debate Gain}
\label{sec:results:perf:single_vs_multi}

To isolate the contribution of debate, we compare the multi-agent outcome
against two baselines defined at round~0, before any discussion takes place:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-agent accuracy}: the fraction of individual agent answers
        that are correct at round~0, averaged across all four agents and all
        repetitions.
  \item \textbf{Majority-vote baseline}: the accuracy of the plurality vote
        over all four agents at round~0 (ties broken arbitrarily).
        This is the best a voting ensemble can do without any interaction
        and represents the fair single-agent comparison point.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{plots/general/fig1_single_vs_multi.png}
  \caption{Accuracy decomposition for GPQA (left) and HiddenBench (right):
           per-agent accuracy at round~0, majority-vote accuracy at round~0,
           and multi-agent accuracy after debate.
           Arrow and annotation show the debate gain (majority $\to$ final).
           Dashed line: random chance at $25\,\%$ (four-choice questions).
           All configurations pooled ($N = 10{,}500$ per dataset).}
  \label{fig:perf:single_vs_multi}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:perf:baseline} summarises the three accuracy levels together
with an oracle upper bound (fraction of repetitions in which at least one
agent holds the correct answer at round~0).

The two datasets tell qualitatively different stories
(Figure~\ref{fig:perf:single_vs_multi}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GPQA.}
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $36.7\,\%$, and majority voting
        raises this modestly to $39.4\,\%$ by aggregating four independent
        guesses.
        Debate adds a further $\mathbf{+1.4}$\,percentage points (pp),
        reaching $40.7\,\%$.
        This marginal gain is consistent with findings across the multi-agent
        debate literature, where homogeneous agents on knowledge-intensive
        tasks tend to show small absolute accuracy improvements over single-agent
        baselines \citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, liu2025stopovervaluing}.
        The model has reasonable prior knowledge on GPQA questions; debate
        refines but does not transform the outcome.
        Notably, the oracle bound of $68.8\,\%$ reveals that the correct answer
        is present among the agents in roughly two thirds of runs: the limiting
        factor is not knowledge but the group's ability to identify and amplify
        the correct voice.

  \item \textbf{HiddenBench.}
        Here the dynamics are strikingly different.
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $15.5\,\%$, already well below
        random.
        Majority voting does not help --- it \emph{reduces} accuracy further
        to $8.3\,\%$.
        This is a direct consequence of correlated errors: agents do not fail
        randomly across the four options but share a systematic bias, all
        gravitating towards the same wrong answer.
        When four agents independently commit to the same wrong option,
        majority voting amplifies that shared error rather than cancelling it
        out --- an LLM analogue of the biased information sampling documented
        in human hidden-profile experiments \citep{stasser1985pooling}.
        Debate reverses this: final accuracy reaches $29.4\,\%$, a gain of
        $\mathbf{+21.1}$\,pp over the majority baseline and above the random
        chance level of $25\,\%$.
        The oracle bound of $46.6\,\%$ confirms that the correct option is
        present among the agents in roughly half of runs; debate recovers
        roughly $\frac{29.4 - 8.3}{46.6 - 8.3} \approx 55\,\%$ of the
        theoretically recoverable accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The contrast between GPQA and HiddenBench is not simply a difficulty
difference.
It reflects a qualitative shift in the role of debate.
On GPQA, debate acts as a \emph{refinement}: agents start mostly correct,
and the system consolidates.
On HiddenBench, debate acts as a \emph{correction}: agents share a
systematic bias, and structured discussion partially overcomes it.
The self-reinforcement, limit-cycle, bistability, and dominance analyses in
the following sections explain when and why this correction succeeds or fails.

\begin{itemize}

        in any of the six per-$W$ cells or in either pooled comparison.
        The choice of topology does not determine whether the system reaches
        the correct answer.

  \item \textbf{Topology has a large and significant effect on efficiency.}
        Star topology requires $0.47$ more rounds on GPQA and $1.84$ more
        rounds on HiddenBench compared to fully connected (both $p < 0.001$).
        The hub bottleneck in the star slows convergence: every spoke must
        route information through a single central node, which aggregates
        conflicting signals and commits more slowly.

  \item \textbf{The efficiency cost of star is larger on HiddenBench.}
        On questions where agents disagree more persistently, the hub receives
        more conflicting input per round and takes longer to commit.
        On GPQA, where agents converge quickly regardless of topology, the
        hub bottleneck is less consequential.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Effect of Memory Window}
\label{sec:results:perf:window}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{plots/general/fig3_memory_window.png}
  \caption{Accuracy (left column) and mean rounds (right column) for
           $W \in \{1, 2, 5\}$ across both datasets and topologies.
           Blue: fully connected; red: star.
           Kruskal-Wallis tests for $W$ effect on accuracy are
           non-significant across all 12 cells (all $p \geq 0.10$).}
  \label{fig:perf:memory_window}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Memory window has no effect on accuracy} in any of the
        12 (dataset, topology, $W$) cells.
        Kruskal-Wallis tests are non-significant across all combinations
        (all $p \geq 0.10$).
        Whether agents receive only the previous round ($W=1$) or the last
        five rounds ($W=5$) does not change how often the system reaches
        the correct answer.

  \item \textbf{Memory window affects convergence speed on HiddenBench.}
        Larger $W$ is associated with faster convergence: $W=1$ produces
        the most rounds (fc: $5.47$, star: $7.56$), while $W=5$ is the
        fastest (fc: $4.99$, star: $6.54$), with $W=2$ intermediate
        (Kruskal-Wallis $p < 0.001$ for both HiddenBench topologies).
        A longer context window may allow agents to track the trajectory
        of the debate and resolve disagreements more efficiently.
        On GPQA, rounds are not significantly affected by $W$
        (all $p \geq 0.21$).

  \item \textbf{The expected monotonic ordering is observed on HiddenBench}
        but absent on GPQA.
        Since GPQA agents converge in very few rounds regardless of $W$,
        additional history provides no useful signal.
        On HiddenBench, where convergence is slow and contested, more context
        appears to help agents distinguish a genuine emerging consensus from
        transient fluctuations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Round Distributions and the Rounds--Accuracy Asymmetry}
\label{sec:results:perf:rounds}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/general/fig4_round_distribution.png}
  \caption{Fraction of repetitions stopping at each round count,
           for all four (dataset, topology) panels and all three memory
           windows (colours).
           The hard cap at $T = 15$ is marked by the dashed red line.
           The minimum possible value is $T_r = 2$: with early-stopping
           threshold $u = 3$, three consecutive unanimous rounds
           (rounds 0, 1, 2) trigger an early stop.}
  \label{fig:perf:round_dist}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GPQA converges fast.}
        The distribution is strongly right-skewed with a mode at
        rounds 2--3.
        Very few repetitions reach the hard cap:
        $p_{\mathrm{cap}} \leq 1.6\,\%$ across all GPQA configurations.

  \item \textbf{HiddenBench converges slowly and often fails to converge.}
        The distribution is substantially flatter and shifted right.
        The hard cap is reached in $1.2$--$14.3\,\%$ of repetitions
        depending on configuration, with star topologies accounting for
        the highest failure rates ($11.0$--$14.3\,\%$).
        This spike represents pure efficiency failures: the group never
        reached unanimous consensus within the round budget.
        Fully-connected topologies show dramatically lower cap rates
        ($1.2$--$3.3\,\%$), reflecting their faster convergence.

  \item \textbf{Star shifts the distribution right across both datasets.}
        Both topology panels in each dataset row show that star has a
        heavier tail and a larger cap spike than fully connected,
        consistent with the mean-rounds differences in
        Table~\ref{tab:perf:topology}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Rounds and accuracy.}
The relationship between rounds to consensus and accuracy reveals a
striking asymmetry (full plot in Appendix~\ref{app:perf:rounds_acc}).
On GPQA, accuracy peaks at rounds~3--4 ($47.1\,\%$) and is lower both
for the earliest stops at round~2 ($36.1\,\%$) and for longer debates
(${\leq}\,40\,\%$ from round~5 onwards): neither very fast nor very slow
consensus is reliably correct.
On HiddenBench the pattern is opposite: repetitions stopping at round~2
achieve only $6.4\,\%$ accuracy --- far below random --- because early
consensus means the group has locked in on the majority-biased wrong answer
before any corrective exchange.
Accuracy rises steadily with more rounds: $23.8\,\%$ at round~3,
$31.7\,\%$ at round~4, $33.1\,\%$ at round~5.

This asymmetry is fundamental to understanding the dynamics reported in the
following sections.
On GPQA, self-reinforcement of an early consensus is often adaptive.
On HiddenBench, it is the primary failure mode: agents self-reinforce a
shared bias, and the system reaches confident unanimity on the wrong answer.
Full per-configuration accuracy, efficiency, cap rates, and vote-flip counts
are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:perf:configs}.

% [Mediation analysis -- existing text below]

The subgroup analysis showed that more diverse initial vote configurations
are associated with both lower SR and more rounds.
To test whether the diversity--rounds relationship runs \emph{through} SR,
or whether diversity affects rounds independently of it, we conducted a
Baron--Kenny mediation analysis~\citep{baron1986moderator} with a
bootstrapped indirect effect.

\subparagraph{Variables.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Treatment $X$: initial diversity.}
        Measured as the Herfindahl diversity index
        $D = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2$, where $p_k$ is the fraction of agents
        on option $k$ at round 0.
        $D = 0$ for a unanimous start (4-0-0-0);
        $D \approx 0.75$ for a fully split start (1-1-1-1).

  \item \textbf{Mediator $M$: self-reinforcement.}
        The repetition-level mean run slope $\bar\beta$.

  \item \textbf{Outcome $Y$: number of rounds.}
        Rounds to consensus or early stopping ($T_r$).
\end{itemize}

All three variables were z-scored \emph{within} each
(dataset, topology, $W$) cell before regression, so that
config-level mean differences are partialled out and only
within-config variation is modelled.
This is equivalent to including a full set of config fixed effects.
The sample is all repetitions with a valid $\bar\beta$
($n = 12{,}595$).

\subparagraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:sr:mediation} reports the three Baron--Kenny steps.

\centering

         diversity ($X$) $\to$ SR ($M$) $\to$ rounds ($Y$).
         All coefficients are in standardised units (z-scores within config).
         $n = 12{,}595$ repetitions.}

  & Proportion mediated               & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$63\,\%$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Total effect (Step 1).}
        More diverse initial configurations lead to more rounds
        ($b = +0.344$, $p < 0.001$). \checkmark

  \item \textbf{Diversity suppresses SR (Step 2).}
        More diverse starts produce lower $\bar\beta$
        ($b = -0.377$, $p < 0.001$). \checkmark

  \item \textbf{SR predicts rounds after controlling for diversity (Step 3).}
        Lower $\bar\beta$ is associated with more rounds even when diversity
        is held constant ($b = -0.575$, $p < 0.001$).
        The direct effect of diversity on rounds is positive but substantially
        reduced ($b = +0.128$), indicating partial mediation.

  \item \textbf{Indirect effect.}
        The bootstrapped indirect effect is $0.217$
        (10{,}000 draws, percentile method),
        with a 95\,\% confidence interval of $[0.205,\ 0.229]$
        that excludes zero.
        Approximately \textbf{63\,\%} of the total effect of diversity on
        rounds is transmitted through SR.

  \item \textbf{By dataset.}
        The mediated proportion differs between datasets:
        for HiddenBench the indirect path accounts for approximately 82\,\%
        of the total effect (indirect $= 0.190$, direct $= 0.041$),
        while for GPQA the split is closer to equal
        (indirect $= 0.205$, direct $= 0.253$).
        On HiddenBench, SR is nearly the entire explanation for why
        diverse starts produce longer debates.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Interpretation.}
The diversity--rounds relationship is a \emph{partial mediation}:
roughly two-thirds of the effect of initial vote dispersion on debate
length runs through SR, with the remaining third being a direct path
(diverse starts add rounds independently of confidence dynamics,
likely through richer argument exchange leading to more vote flips).
The chain is therefore supported: aiming for diverse initial configurations
is a plausible way to induce slower convergence, and SR is the primary
mechanism by which this operates.

The findings above support the following practical observations.
These are descriptive summaries of the data and should not be taken as
causal claims.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{SR is the convergence mechanism.}  The strong negative
        correlation between SR and rounds ($\rho \approx -0.7$) holds
        universally.  Systems that exhibit more SR finish faster.

  \item \textbf{SR is not uniformly beneficial for accuracy.}
        On GPQA it correlates positively with accuracy; on HiddenBench it
        does not.  Whether SR helps depends on whether the initial vote
        distribution is reliable.

  \item \textbf{Initial vote distribution is the largest driver of SR.}
        Unanimous starting configurations (4-0-0-0) yield $\bar\beta = 0.706$;
        fully split configurations (1-1-1-1) yield 0.349.  The topology and
        memory window together shift $\bar\beta$ by at most 0.05--0.17;
        the initial distribution shifts it by up to 0.37.

  \item \textbf{Topology is the most accessible lever.}
        Switching from fc to star reduces $\bar\beta$ by roughly
        0.04--0.08 per round and is significant at $W{=}1$ for both datasets.
        This is the clearest within-data design choice.

  \item \textbf{Memory window has a weak, consistent effect.}
        Smaller $W$ is associated with slightly higher SR (opposite to the
        strength-scaling prediction).  The effect is not statistically
        significant in most cells and should be treated as suggestive only.

  \item \textbf{To amplify SR} (prioritise speed, accept faster convergence):
        use fc topology; use $W{=}1$.

  \item \textbf{To attenuate SR} (prioritise deliberation):
        use star topology; use larger $W$;
        consider a minimum-round floor to prevent very early stopping
        driven by never-flipping agents. Use a diverse starting position. However, more debate does not necessarily mean better accuracy.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Limit Cycles}
\label{sec:results:lc}

\subsubsection{Detection Funnel and Topology Contrast}
\label{sec:results:lc:prevalence}
Limit cycles are the rarest motif in the dataset.
Table~\ref{tab:lc_funnel} shows the detection funnel across all sequences.
The cooperative debate design suppresses sustained oscillation to near-zero:
99.0\,\% of agent sequences and 97.6\,\% of system sequences converge to fixed
points and are excluded before any test is run.
Of the small fraction that fail to converge (candidates), only 2.8\,\% and
5.4\,\% carry a statistically significant periodic signature, yielding
\textbf{14 agent-level} and \textbf{16 system-level} limit cycles across the
entire dataset.

      & $p_{\mathrm{LC}}$ (cond.) \\
\midrule
Agent-level  & 50{,}400 & 49{,}896~(99.0\,\%) &  500~(1.0\,\%) & 14 & 0.028 \\

Flagged cases concentrate strongly in HiddenBench (12 of 14 agent-level,
15 of 16 system-level), consistent with that benchmark's greater ambiguity
keeping agents uncertain for longer.
Every single flagged case runs the full $T = 15$ rounds: limit cycles are a
\textbf{pure efficiency failure} in which early stopping never triggers and
the group never reaches unanimity.

\paragraph{Fully-connected topology: no genuine oscillations.}
Figure~\ref{fig:lc_rec_fc} shows the recurrence matrices for all flagged
agent-level cases in the fully-connected topology.
None display the equally-spaced diagonal stripe pattern that is the
unambiguous signature of periodic recurrence.
Instead, each matrix is dominated by a large block produced by a long stable
run of the majority vote, with one or two isolated stray votes.
The formally detected period (e.g.\ $\hat{P} = 8$) merely reflects the
distance between those stray votes, not a genuine oscillation.
We conclude that the fully-connected topology produces \textbf{no genuine limit
cycles}: the flagged cases are artefacts of near-convergent trajectories whose
trailing run is $k = 1$ or $k = 2$, just below the convergence threshold
$u = 3$.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/lc/lc_rec_fc.png}
  \caption{Recurrence matrices for all agent-level flagged cases in the

           is a large uniform block from vote persistence, not periodic
           diagonal stripes. These are near-fixed-point artefacts.}
  \label{fig:lc_rec_fc}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:lc_rec_star} shows the recurrence matrices for the star-topology
cases. In contrast to the fully-connected matrices, these display clean
equally-spaced diagonal stripes with DET $\geq 0.98$ and shuffle $p$-values
of 0.001, the unambiguous signature of a genuine periodic orbit.

and when.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/lc/lc_timelines.png}
  \caption{Vote sequence timelines for the two most informative star-topology
           limit cycles. Each row corresponds to one agent; colour encodes
           the vote held in that round. Left: the hub-driven case ($q{=}86$,
           GPQA) in which the hub (agent~0) alternates perfectly and the
           spokes follow. Right: the collective case ($q{=}51$, HiddenBench)
           in which all four agents, including the hub (agent~3), are locked
           into a synchronised two-option alternation for the entire debate.}
  \label{fig:lc_timelines}
\end{figure}

Two structural patterns are present across all genuine star-topology cases.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hub-driven cycle} ($q{=}86$, GPQA; $q{=}40$, HiddenBench).
        The hub (degree-3 node, connected to all spokes) alternates between
        two votes every round and broadcasts this alternation to all spokes
        simultaneously.
        In $q{=}86$ the hub sequence is \texttt{BABABABABABABABA}
        (DET = 1.00, $\hat{P} = 2$, $p = 0.001$); all three spokes follow
        with minor noise (visible in the timeline).
        The mechanism is a positive feedback loop: the hub aggregates
        conflicting signals from three spokes each round, cannot commit,
        flips, and then re-broadcasts that flip to all spokes, who in turn
        flip back.

  \item \textbf{Collective synchronised cycle} ($q{=}51$, rep~23,
        HiddenBench/star/$W{=}5$).
        All four agents cycle in synchrony: hub (agent~3) sequence is
        \texttt{CBABABABABABABAB}; all three spokes carry DET $\geq 0.82$
        with the same $\hat{P} = 2$ ($p = 0.001$ for three of four agents).
        This is the only repetition in the entire dataset where both an
        agent-level and a system-level limit-cycle flag coincide.
        The group composition oscillates between $(1, 3, 0, 0)$ and
        $(3, 1, 0, 0)$ for twelve consecutive rounds: a one-versus-three
        majority that flips every round and cannot be broken.
\end{enumerate}

In both patterns the hub is the critical node. This contrasts with converged
star-topology repetitions, in which the hub typically has the fewest vote
flips of any agent, settling first due to its aggregator role. On genuinely
ambiguous questions the same aggregator role becomes a liability: the hub
receives conflicting signals, becomes the most uncertain node, and its
broadcast power then propagates that uncertainty to the entire group.

\paragraph{System-level perspective.}
The 16 system-level flagged cases tell the same story at the composition level.
The most informative case ($q{=}51$, system DET = 0.944, $p = 0.001$,
$\hat{P} = 4$) is the collective cycle described above, in which the
composition of votes alternates between two states for the majority of the
debate.
A second pattern present in several cases is a \emph{stable split}: the
composition locks into a constant two-way division (e.g.\ $(2, 0, 2, 0)$
for ten consecutive rounds) rather than oscillating. This is a distinct
failure mode from periodic oscillation but is correctly detected by the RQA
machinery because the repeated identical composition state produces diagonal
structure in the recurrence matrix.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
The effects of limit cycles on accuracy cannot be reliably reported.
The sample size is too small (14 and 16 cases) for any inferential comparison
across configurations, and the raw accuracy difference between flagged and
non-flagged repetitions (0.60 vs.\ 0.40 at the agent level) is a selection
artefact: limit cycles arise precisely when the group is genuinely split,
which is also when a correct-answer minority was present and resisting
throughout.

\paragraph{System design implications.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Topology is a relevant design axis for worst-case efficiency.}
        Star topology can produce genuine limit cycles; fully-connected topology
        cannot, at least in this dataset. Because every limit cycle runs to the
        hard round cap, topology choice directly determines whether the system
        has a bounded worst-case runtime. This effect is rare but
        structurally guaranteed when it occurs, not random noise.

  \item \textbf{A hard round cap is a necessary stopping condition.}
        Without it, star-topology debates on genuinely ambiguous questions
        have no self-termination mechanism. Reaching $T$ should be treated
        as a detectable failure signal and handled explicitly by the
        system (e.g.\ by flagging the output as unresolved rather than
        reporting a potentially spurious majority vote).

  \item \textbf{The memory window $W$ is not a relevant design axis for limit
        cycles.}
        No effect of $W$ is observed across $W \in \{1, 2, 5\}$.
        However, the total number of flagged cases is too small to draw
        firm conclusions; future work with larger datasets and a wider range
        of $W$ should revisit this.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Mechanism: Log Analysis}
\label{sec:results:lc:mechanism}

\paragraph{Why limit cycles occur: evidence from log analysis.}

To understand the causal structure behind the flagged star-topology cases, we
extracted and analysed the full Phase~A and Phase~B text logs of all 14
flagged agent-level trajectories (129 flip events total), comparing flip
events against stable rounds and against non-flagged control repetitions on
the same questions.

\begin{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Social deference.}
        For each flip event, we searched the agent's Phase~B reasoning for
        deference language (\emph{"after reviewing"}, \emph{"I am updating
        my vote"}, \emph{"strong defenses presented by"}) and self-conviction
        language (\emph{"I independently concluded"}, \emph{"my own
        analysis"}, \emph{"I realized"}).
        Deference phrases appear in 64\,\% of all 129 flip events;
        self-conviction phrases appear in 2\,\%.
        The key observation is that in every recorded flip, the agent's own
        Phase~A defense in the \emph{same} round argued for the position it
        then abandoned in Phase~B.
        In qid\,=\,86 (GPQA, round~1), agent~0 writes in Phase~A:
        \emph{"Option~B presents a 1H~NMR spectrum with chemical shifts
        consistent with a para-substituted benzene ring and a methyl group
        attached to a carbonyl"}\ --- then immediately writes in Phase~B of
        the same round:
        \emph{"After reviewing the Phase~A drafts, I'm reconsidering my vote.
        Agent2 and Agent4 have raised valid points about Option~A.
        The peak at 9.9\,ppm in Option~A is indeed characteristic of an
        aldehyde proton"} and switches to A.
        The same structure repeats at every subsequent flip across all 15
        rounds of that repetition.
        \textbf{The agent's own argument has no observable causal weight on
        the flip decision.}

  \item \textbf{Peer consensus at flip time.}
        For each flip event we recorded the fraction of peers holding the
        new vote at round $t{-}1$.
        In period-2 cases 35\,\% of flips are preceded by unanimous peer
        consensus (3/3 peers on the new vote); 65\,\% by a 2/3 majority.
        The self-sustaining mechanism is visible in the round-by-round
        vote matrix of qid\,=\,86:

        \smallskip
        \begin{center}
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{ccccc}
        \toprule
        Round & Hub (a0) & Spoke (a1) & Spoke (a2) & Spoke (a3) \\
        \midrule
        0 & B & B & A & A \\
        1 & \textbf{A} & B & A & A \\
        2 & \textbf{B} & A & A & A \\
        3 & \textbf{A} & B & B & B \\
        4 & \textbf{B} & A & A & A \\
        $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ \\
        \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
        \end{center}
        \smallskip

        At round~1 the hub (agent~0, out-degree~3) sees 2/3 peers on~A,
        flips to~A. It now broadcasts an A-defending Phase~A to all three
        spokes. The spokes, who were on~A, read the hub's challenge and flip
        to~B at round~2, restoring 3/3 pressure on~B for the hub. The hub
        then flips back to~B at round~3, and so on.
        In qid\,=\,51 (HiddenBench, hub agent~3) the pattern is even
        cleaner: from round~3 onward the hub receives 3/3 unanimous pressure
        at every step and the cycle runs without noise for twelve consecutive
        rounds.
        \textbf{Each flip recreates the conditions that caused it.}

  \item \textbf{Argument recycling.}
        We computed SequenceMatcher similarity between all pairs of Phase~A
        defenses an agent produced for the same option across non-consecutive
        rounds.
        For qid\,=\,86 the agent defends option~B at rounds~1,\,3,\,5,\,7,\,9,\,11
        and option~A at rounds~2,\,4,\,6,\,8,\,10,\,12,\,14.
        The B-defense at round~1 reads:
        \emph{"Option~B presents a 1H~NMR spectrum with chemical shifts
        consistent with a para-substituted benzene ring and a methyl group
        attached to a carbonyl. The chemical shifts at 7.8\,ppm and
        7.6\,ppm\ldots"}
        The B-defense at round~3 is word-for-word identical for the first
        220 characters (similarity\,=\,0.85 averaged over all B-defense pairs).
        The A-defenses are similarly repetitive (similarity\,=\,0.85):
        \emph{"Option~A's peak at 9.9\,ppm is highly indicative of an
        aldehyde proton, strongly aligning with the carbonyl group indicated
        by the FTIR spectrum. The peaks at 7.8 and 7.6\,ppm suggest aromatic
        protons\ldots"} appears with minor wording variation at rounds
        4,\,6,\,8,\,10,\,12,\,14.
        Neither side ever introduces a new piece of evidence, a
        numerical comparison, or an argument the other side has not already
        countered.
        The same pattern holds in qid\,=\,117 (GPQA, agent~2 defending
        option~D across ten rounds, mean similarity\,=\,0.92).
        \textbf{Arguments do not accumulate or escalate across rounds:
        the debate is effectively stateless with respect to argument quality.}

  \item \textbf{Question structure.}
        All genuine LC cases involve questions where exactly two options are
        each supported by a distinct, independently defensible evidence
        thread with no formal contradiction between them.
        In qid\,=\,86 the competing options are:

        \smallskip
        \begin{center}
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{ll}
        \toprule
        Option & 1H NMR peaks \\
        \midrule
        A & 9.9\,(1H,\,s),\ 7.8\,(2H,\,d),\ 7.6\,(2H,\,d),\ 3.7\,(2H,\,s) \\
        B & 7.8\,(2H,\,d),\ 7.6\,(2H,\,d),\ 2.3\,(3H,\,s) \\
        \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
        \end{center}
        \smallskip

        Option~A anchors on the 9.9\,ppm aldehyde peak as evidence for a
        carbonyl group; option~B anchors on the 2.3\,ppm singlet as evidence
        for a methyl-carbonyl.
        Both interpretations are chemically valid and neither refutes the
        other, so the argument can cycle indefinitely.
        In qid\,=\,51 (HiddenBench) the cycling pair is Green Valley
        Outpost~(A) vs.\ Red Cliff Lodge~(B): one defended on the grounds of
        southerly location reducing wind exposure, the other on solid stone
        construction; both are independently valid survival heuristics.
        In qid\,=\,40 the pair is Mars Colony~(A) vs.\ Orion Station~(C):
        one defended via confirmed shielding, the other via proximity
        (2--3\,hour travel vs.\ 3\,hours).
        The symmetry is quantified by round-0 vote distributions: in
        qid\,=\,40 the two cycling options start at 35\,\% and 37\,\% across
        all 30 repetitions; in qid\,=\,86 the minority cycling option
        captures 23\,\% of round-0 votes.
        \textbf{Limit cycles only arise when the question provides no
        argument that dominates across all agents simultaneously.}

  \item \textbf{Initial vote assignment.}
        In every LC case the cycling agent begins in a minority or tie,
        creating adversarial pressure from round~0.
        The causal role of the initial draw is directly confirmed for
        qid\,=\,86 by comparing the three representative repetitions:

        \smallskip
        \begin{center}
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{cccccc}
        \toprule
        Rep & Hub (out-deg\,3) & Spoke a1 & Spoke a2 & Spoke a3
            & Outcome \\
        \midrule
        0  & A & A & A & B & converges r4 \\
        1  & B & A & B & A$^\dagger$ & converges r7 \\
        11 & B & B & A & A & \textbf{limit cycle} \\
        \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
        \end{center}
        \footnotesize{$^\dagger$ hub in rep~1 is agent~3 (out-degree~3).}
        \smallskip

        \normalsize
        In rep~0 the hub starts on the majority option~A and immediately
        reinforces convergence.
        In rep~1 the hub starts on~B but is paired with a 3:1 majority for~A,
        collapsing within two rounds.
        In rep~11 the hub starts on~B while the three spokes are split 2A:2B,
        creating the symmetric adversarial pressure that initiates and sustains
        the oscillation.
        The question, model, temperature, and system prompt are identical
        across all 30 repetitions of qid\,=\,86.
        \textbf{The differentiating factor between an LC repetition and a
        converging repetition on the same question is the random initial
        vote draw, not argument quality.}

\end{itemize}

The five conditions interact as follows.
Conditions~1--2 produce the cycle structure: unconditional deference to the
perceived majority means that every flip immediately reconstructs the
majority for the opposite option.
Conditions~3--4 prevent the cycle from being broken: because arguments
neither escalate nor resolve the underlying evidence ambiguity, no single
round produces a decisive intervention.
Condition~5 determines which repetition out of 30 on the same question
enters the attractor: the cycle can only start if the hub begins with a
split or isolated initial vote.
The absence of genuine limit cycles in the fully-connected topology follows
from conditions~1--2: in a fully-connected graph no single node aggregates
all peer signals simultaneously, so the hub-driven inversion loop cannot
form.

\subsection{High-Repetition Distribution Analysis}
\label{sec:results:highrep}

The preceding sections characterise system behaviour at the task-population level,
aggregating over 35 tasks per dataset at $R = 30$ repetitions each.
This section takes a complementary, within-task perspective: three individual GPQA
questions (q84, q125, q144) were each run for $R = 1{,}000$ repetitions under the
fixed configuration $W = 2$, fully-connected topology, $N = 4$ agents.
The aim is to characterise the distribution of outcomes across many runs of the same
task and to identify which properties of the initial vote composition predict
accuracy and convergence speed.
The three tasks were selected on the basis of mid-range accuracy ($0.27$--$0.51$
under $R = 30$) and high initial-vote entropy, ensuring genuinely uncertain outcomes
and substantial run-to-run variability.

For each run the following quantities are extracted.
\emph{Initial-state features} (causal, observable before any debate):
initial vote entropy $H_0$ (Shannon entropy in bits; with $N = 4$ agents and $M = 4$
options $H_0$ takes five discrete values $\{0, 0.81, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0\}$
corresponding to the vote patterns $(4,0,0,0)$ through $(1,1,1,1)$); initial
plurality size (count of agents on the most popular option; a deterministic function
of $H_0$); and two binary indicators for whether the ground-truth option is present
in any initial vote and whether it holds the initial plurality.
\emph{Trajectory signatures} (post-hoc): total vote flips (count of individual
opinion changes across all rounds) and confidence change (mean final confidence
minus mean initial confidence).

\paragraph{Ground-truth presence in the initial vote distribution.}
The dominant finding is stark: whether the ground-truth option appears at all in
the initial vote distribution is by far the strongest predictor of final accuracy,
consistent across all three tasks
(Figure~\ref{fig:highrep:init_votes_split}, Table~\ref{tab:highrep:init_votes}).

When the correct answer is absent from all initial votes, the system almost never
reaches it: accuracy is $0.4$\,\%, $3.1$\,\%, and $6.9$\,\% for q84, q125, and
q144 respectively ($\chi^2 > 120$, $p < 10^{-27}$ in all cases).
The rare correct answers in the absent condition are consistent with stochastic
noise rather than genuine recovery.
Within the present condition, accuracy is roughly $2\times$ higher when the correct
option also holds the initial plurality (e.g.\ $0.620$ vs $0.257$ for q84).
These findings suggest that the system operates primarily as a vote amplifier:
debate selectively strengthens options already represented in the initial
distribution and overwhelmingly reinforces whichever option holds the initial
plurality.
The capacity to surface a correct answer that no agent endorsed at the outset is
negligible under the present design.

\paragraph{Initial vote entropy and efficiency.}
Figure~\ref{fig:highrep:h0} shows accuracy and mean rounds to consensus as a
function of $H_0$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig2_h0_accuracy_efficiency.png}
  \caption{(a) System accuracy and (b) mean rounds to consensus by initial vote
           entropy $H_0$.  X-axis labels show the vote pattern.
           Wilson 95\,\% CI for accuracy; $\pm$SEM for rounds.
           $R=1{,}000$ per task, $W=2$, fully-connected.}
  \label{fig:highrep:h0}
\end{figure}

Higher $H_0$ is associated with longer debates in all three tasks.
The effect on accuracy is not monotone: it is largely a confound of $H_0$ with the
probability that the ground truth is present in the initial votes.
At $H_0 = 0$ all agents agree on one option, which is the ground truth only by
chance; at $H_0 = 2$ every option gets one vote so the ground truth is always
present but as a minority.
Within the GT-present subset, $H_0$ negatively predicts accuracy ($r = -0.23$ for
q84, $r = -0.29$ for q144): a more spread-out start places the correct option in a
weaker competitive position.
$H_0$ therefore encodes a tradeoff: higher entropy increases the probability of the
correct option being present but decreases its initial plurality and thus its chance
of winning.

\paragraph{Debate length, trajectory turbulence, and confidence change.}
Figure~\ref{fig:highrep:rounds} shows the relationship between rounds to consensus
and accuracy, and between vote-flip count and mean rounds.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig3_rounds_accuracy_flips.png}
  \caption{(a) P(correct) as a function of rounds to consensus
           (Wilson 95\,\% CI, $n \geq 15$ per point).
           (b) Mean rounds to consensus as a function of total vote flips
           ($\pm$SEM, $n \geq 10$ per bin).
           $R=1{,}000$ per task, $W=2$, fully-connected.}
  \label{fig:highrep:rounds}
\end{figure}

Longer debates do not reliably produce more accurate outcomes.
For q144 accuracy is negatively correlated with rounds ($r = -0.19$,
$p < 10^{-9}$); for q84 the correlation is near zero ($r = -0.03$);
only q125 shows a slight positive trend ($r = +0.12$, $p < 0.001$), which reflects
task-specific structure rather than a general deliberation benefit.
Vote flips and rounds are tightly coupled ($r = 0.83$--$0.92$, all $p \approx 0$):
turbulent trajectories with many opinion changes are the same runs that fail to
reach fast consensus.
Confidence increases monotonically across rounds regardless of outcome (mean gain
$\approx 1.6$--$1.9$ points on the 1--10 scale) and carries no predictive signal for
final correctness ($|r| < 0.09$ across all tasks and all confidence features).
The distributions of confidence change for correct and incorrect runs overlap
almost perfectly across all three tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:app:highrep:distributions}
in Appendix~\ref{app:highrep}), confirming that confidence gain is a property of
the debate process itself rather than a signal of outcome quality.
Full feature-by-outcome correlations are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:highrep}.
