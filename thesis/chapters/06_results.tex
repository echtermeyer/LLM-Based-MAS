This section reports the empirical results of applying the \suitename{} detectors
to 30{,}000 debates across two benchmarks, GPQA and HiddenBench.
The two benchmarks behave so differently that every result below is reported per
benchmark rather than pooled: as \autoref{sec:results:perf} shows, debate plays a
fundamentally different role on each, and the motif analyses inherit that split
throughout.
We first characterise overall accuracy and efficiency (\autoref{sec:results:perf}),
then measure each motif in turn: self-reinforcement (\autoref{sec:results:sr}),
bistability (\autoref{sec:results:bistability}), dominance (\autoref{sec:results:dominance}),
and limit cycles (\autoref{sec:results:lc}).
Outcome-distribution evidence and a causal test for the amplifier reading follow
(\autoref{sec:results:evid}), and we close by extracting the effective influence
topology that emerges within fully-connected runs
(\autoref{sec:results:eff_topology}).
Each section reports detection statistics, structural drivers, and relationship
to outcomes; null and near-null results are reported as findings.

\subsection{Accuracy and Efficiency}
\label{sec:results:perf}
This section reports overall system accuracy and efficiency across all
600 task--configuration combinations (50 tasks $\times$ 2 datasets $\times$
3 memory windows $\times$ 2 topologies, $R = 50$ repetitions each,
$n = 30{,}000$ total).
Two metrics are reported throughout:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy}: fraction of repetitions in which the final answer
        (the plurality of the agents' votes at the final round; see
        \autoref{sec:setup:protocol}) matches the ground-truth answer.
  \item \textbf{Efficiency}: mean number of rounds until the early-stopping
        criterion fires or the hard cap $T = 15$ is reached.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Single-agent baseline and debate gain.}

To isolate the contribution of debate, we compare the multi-agent outcome
against two baselines defined at round~0, before any discussion takes place:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-agent accuracy}: the fraction of individual agent answers
        that are correct at round~0, averaged across all agents and all
        repetitions.
  \item \textbf{Majority-vote baseline}: the accuracy of the plurality vote
        over all agents at round~0 (ties broken arbitrarily).
        This is the best a voting ensemble can do without any interaction
        and represents the fair single-agent comparison point.
\end{itemize}


\autoref{tab:perf:baseline} summarises the three accuracy levels together
with an oracle upper bound (fraction of repetitions in which at least one
agent holds the correct answer at round~0).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Dataset & Per-agent & Majority vote (r\,=\,0) & After debate & Oracle (r\,=\,0) \\
\midrule
GPQA        & $38.2\,\%$ & $40.5\,\%$ & $\mathbf{42.2\,\%}$ & $70.6\,\%$ \\
HiddenBench & $14.9\,\%$ & $\phantom{0}7.6\,\%$ & $\mathbf{26.1\,\%}$ & $46.3\,\%$ \\
\bottomrule

         All configurations pooled ($n = 15{,}000$ per dataset).
         Oracle: fraction of repetitions where at least one agent is correct
         at round~0.}
\label{tab:perf:baseline}
\end{table}

The two datasets are different in kind, not merely in difficulty, and cannot be
pooled; we therefore analyse them separately throughout. They tell qualitatively
different stories:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GPQA.}
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $38.2\,\%$, and majority voting
        raises this modestly to $40.5\,\%$ by aggregating four independent
        guesses.
        Debate adds a further $\mathbf{+1.8}$\,percentage points (pp),
        reaching $42.2\,\%$.
        This marginal gain is consistent with findings across the multi-agent
        debate literature, where homogeneous agents on knowledge-intensive
        tasks tend to show small absolute accuracy improvements over single-agent
        baselines \citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, liu2025stopovervaluing}.
        The model has reasonable prior knowledge on GPQA questions; debate
        refines but does not transform the outcome.
        Notably, the oracle bound of $70.6\,\%$ reveals that the correct answer
        is present among the agents in roughly two thirds of repetitions: the limiting
        factor is not knowledge but the group's ability to identify and amplify
        the correct voice.
        The discrepancy between oracle and achieved accuracy, which we call the
        \emph{oracle gap} following \citet{bertalanic2026costofconsensus}, is a
        documented property of plurality aggregation under peer influence rather
        than an artefact of our setting.

  \item \textbf{HiddenBench.}
        Here the dynamics are strikingly different.
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $14.9\,\%$, already well below
        random. This is expected rather than surprising: HiddenBench is
        constructed to exploit a shared bias in the underlying model, so
        individual agents are drawn towards the same attractive-but-wrong option
        instead of erring independently.
        Majority voting does not help, it \emph{reduces} accuracy further
        to $7.6\,\%$.
        This is a direct consequence of correlated errors: agents do not fail
        randomly across the options but share a systematic bias, all gravitating
        towards the same wrong answer. When agents are drawn from a single model,
        response similarity produces static debate dynamics that converge to the
        shared misconception rather than cancelling it out
        \citep{estornell2024multillm}, an LLM analogue of the biased information
        sampling documented in human hidden-profile experiments
        \citep{stasser1985pooling}.
        Debate reverses this partial collapse: final accuracy reaches
        $26.1\,\%$, a gain of $\mathbf{+18.5}$\,pp over the majority baseline.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The contrast between GPQA and HiddenBench is not simply a difficulty
difference.
It reflects a qualitative shift in the role of debate.
On GPQA, debate acts as a \emph{refinement}: agents start mostly correct,
and the system consolidates.
On HiddenBench, debate acts as a partial \emph{correction}: agents share a
systematic bias that drives the majority vote well below chance, and structured
discussion recovers most of that deficit without pushing the group clearly above
chance.
The self-reinforcement, bistability, dominance, and limit-cycle analyses in
the following sections explain when and why this correction succeeds or fails.

\paragraph{Effect of memory window.}

\autoref{fig:perf:memory_window} shows accuracy and mean rounds to consensus
broken down by memory window $W \in \{1,2,5\}$ for each dataset and topology.

  \centering

  \caption[Accuracy and rounds by memory window]{Accuracy (left column) and mean rounds to consensus, i.e.\ efficiency
           (right column), as a function of memory window $W$, for GPQA (top row)
           and HiddenBench (bottom row).
           Lines connect the two topologies.}
  \label{fig:perf:memory_window}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Memory window shapes accuracy; $W = 2$ consistently ranks first.}
        Across both datasets and both topologies, $W = 2$ produces the highest
        per-task accuracy, outperforming $W = 1$ by up to $3.0$\,pp and $W = 5$
        by up to $2.4$\,pp (full per-configuration breakdown in
        Appendix~\ref{app:perf:configs}).
        We report this as a consistent ordering rather than an established
        effect. At the task level the differences between memory windows are
        small and do not hold up under multiple-comparison correction
        (Section~\ref{sec:results:synthesis}), so the ordering is suggestive at
        best.
        A window of one round gives agents only the most recent exchange,
        which is too little context to detect persisting disagreement.
        A window of five carries older, less relevant rounds that dilute the
        signal from the current debate state.
        $W = 2$ strikes the balance: enough history to read the trajectory
        of opinions without burying the most recent exchange.
\end{itemize}

        against star across the $50$ tasks, the difference is negligible for
        both datasets (GPQA $\Delta \approx 0.0$\,pp; HiddenBench
        $\Delta = +0.6$\,pp). We find no evidence that the choice of
        topology determines whether the system reaches the correct answer.
        Two independent studies report the same null. In a networked
        convention-formation task, the network type an agent sits in has no
        significant effect on how well the group finally coordinates
        \citep{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory}; and in a debate task, giving each
        agent more visible peers leaves accuracy flat and never above the
        non-communicative baseline \citep{bertalanic2026costofconsensus}.

  \item \textbf{Topology has a large effect on efficiency.}
        Star topology requires $0.50$ more rounds on GPQA and $1.74$ more
        rounds on HiddenBench compared to fully connected.
        The hub bottleneck in the star slows convergence: every spoke must
        route information through a single central node, which aggregates
        conflicting signals and commits more slowly.
        This is a genuine trade-off rather than a pure cost, however: the star
        carries only $2(N-1) = 6$ messages per round against the $N(N-1) = 12$ of
        the fully-connected topology (\autoref{sec:setup:notation}), so each of
        those extra rounds is roughly half as expensive in tokens, and the two
        topologies are closer in total communication cost than the round counts
        alone suggest.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Rounds and accuracy.}
The relationship between rounds to consensus and accuracy reveals a
striking asymmetry (full plot in Appendix~\ref{app:perf:rounds_acc}).
On GPQA, accuracy peaks at rounds~3--4 and is lower both for the earliest
stops and for longer debates: neither very fast nor very slow consensus is
reliably correct.
On HiddenBench the pattern is opposite: repetitions stopping at round~2
achieve only $2.5\,\%$ accuracy, far below random, because early
consensus means the group has locked in on the majority-biased wrong answer
before any corrective exchange; accuracy then rises sharply over the next
few rounds before flattening.

This asymmetry is one instance of a broader point that governs everything that follows.
The two benchmarks are so different in kind, GPQA a knowledge task where debate
refines a mostly-correct start and HiddenBench a bias task where it must correct a
systematically wrong one, that the motif analyses in the following sections must be
read per benchmark rather than pooled. The same measurement routinely points in
opposite directions on the two datasets, so the dataset is the first variable to
condition on before interpreting any result.


\subsection{Self-Reinforcement}
\label{sec:results:sr}

Self-reinforcement asks whether an agent that keeps its vote grows steadily
more confident in it, measuring the debate's tendency to entrench a position rather
than hold it open to revision. We read it from the slope $\beta_{\mathrm{seg}}$ of
confidence against round number within each \emph{vote-stable segment} (a run of at
least three consecutive rounds on the same vote), and summarise a cell by the
prevalence $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$, the fraction of segments with a positive slope,
against a random-walk null of $0.5$; the full construction is given in
\autoref{sec:method:sr}. Pooled across both benchmarks and all configurations, the
segment filter leaves $133{,}696$ segments to analyse. Three findings follow, moving
from how often self-reinforcement occurs, to what it does to the debate, to what it
does to accuracy.

\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
  \item \textbf{It is universal.} $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ never approaches its null,
        ranging from $0.733$ to $0.881$ across the 12 (dataset, topology, $W$)
        cells, with all 50 tasks individually above $0.5$ in every cell
        (per-configuration values in Appendix~\ref{app:sr:supp}). Confidence climbs
        fastest exactly where the debate has least to resolve, in repetitions that
        never change their votes and in unanimous starts, while topology and memory
        move the slope an order of magnitude less than the initial vote composition
        does. The pattern is entrenchment, not evidence integration.

  \item \textbf{It drives convergence.} That same entrenchment is what makes the
        debate settle: tasks with steeper confidence ramps reach consensus in fewer
        rounds in all 12 cells (task-level Spearman $\rho$ between $-0.73$ and
        $-0.89$; \autoref{fig:sr:efficiency}). This is the clearest and most
        consistent association in the analysis.

  \item \textbf{It is blind to correctness.} Faster convergence is not more accurate
        convergence. Whether self-reinforcement helps depends entirely on \emph{what}
        it amplifies, which the initial vote distribution fixes in advance: when the
        correct option is already among the opening votes, stronger reinforcement
        pushes the group toward it ($r_{pb} = +0.20$ on GPQA, $+0.15$ on
        HiddenBench); when no agent starts correct, the same force only locks in a
        wrong answer sooner ($r_{pb} = -0.11$ and $-0.07$). The accuracy scatter is
        given in Appendix~\ref{app:sr:supp} (\autoref{fig:sr:accuracy}).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{plots/sr/fig4_sr_efficiency.png}
  \caption[Self-reinforcement slope versus rounds to consensus]{Mean segment slope $\bar\beta$ vs.\ mean rounds to consensus at the
           task level.  Each point is one task; colours denote memory window.
           Dashed line: pooled OLS fit.}
  \label{fig:sr:efficiency}
\end{figure}

The three findings describe a single behaviour: self-reinforcement is the engine
that converges the debate, but it converges on whatever the opening votes already
favour rather than on the correct answer, amplifying the initial composition
regardless of its quality, and rising confidence is no guide to that quality since
the group grows more sure whether its final answer is right or wrong. This is the
measured form of the entrenchment mechanism that \citet{oh2026entrenchment} identify
as a root cause of belief entrenchment, that \citet{estornell2024multillm} derive
from response homogeneity, and that \citet{cau2026agreementdrift} describe as a
selective amplifier of locally consistent change; it also mirrors reports that
debate confidence rises anti-Bayesianly without new evidence
\citep{prasad2025certain, bertalanic2026costofconsensus}. The seeded experiment
(\autoref{sec:results:evid}) confirms this dependence on the initial composition
causally.

\subsection{Bistability}
\label{sec:results:bistability}

Bistability asks whether a task admits more than one stable consensus outcome, and
whether the opening vote distribution decides which one the debate reaches. We read
it per (task, configuration) cell from two signatures: $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, the
inverse-Simpson diversity of the endpoint attractors (how many stable outcomes
coexist across repetitions), and Cramér's $V$, which measures how strongly the
initial votes select among them (basin sensitivity). A cell is \emph{monostable}
when one attractor dominates ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} < 1.5$), \emph{multistable} when
several coexist and the initial votes predict the endpoint, and \emph{stochastic}
when several coexist but the endpoint does not follow from the start; the full
construction is given in \autoref{sec:method:bistability}. Repetitions are eligible
if they settle on a unanimous endpoint (the $1.97\,\%$ that end split are excluded).
Three findings follow.

\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
  \item \textbf{Multiple stable outcomes are the norm, and they belong to the task.}
        Across the 600 cells, $76\,\%$ show more than one coexisting attractor
        ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} > 1.5$), split between deterministic basin selection
        (multistable) and outcomes that vary independently of the opening votes
        (stochastic; the per-dataset breakdown is in Appendix~\ref{app:bistab}).
        This prevalence figure is conditioned on the screened task set
        (\autoref{sec:setup:design}) and should not be read as the motif rate
        across an unselected task population. This
        structure is a property of the question, not the configuration: neither
        topology nor memory shifts either metric (all task-level confidence intervals
        contain zero), while per-task $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is highly consistent across
        the six configurations (mean pairwise Spearman $\rho \approx 0.90$ on GPQA and
        $0.84$ on HiddenBench). Multiple absorbing states are the expected structure
        for this class of system, where naming-game dynamics admit one equilibrium
        per available convention
        \citep{baronchelli2018consensus, ashery2025conventions}; our repetition
        design adds the ability to see which of them a given task actually visits,
        where prior LLM studies report a single run or run-level means
        \citep{cau2026agreementdrift, mehdizadeh2026topologymemory}.

  \item \textbf{Bistability helps exactly when the default answer is wrong.} Across
        tasks, more coexisting attractors go with \emph{higher} accuracy on
        HiddenBench ($\rho = +0.44$) but slightly \emph{lower}, non-significant
        accuracy on GPQA ($\rho = -0.16$). The opposition follows from what the
        dominant attractor is: on GPQA, where agents usually start near the correct
        answer, a single attractor is already the right one and extra basins only add
        wrong-answer traps; on HiddenBench, where the majority attractor is the shared
        wrong answer, competing basins give the correct minority a foothold from which
        to win. Consistent with this, on HiddenBench the correct attractor is selected
        more sharply than a wrong one (Cramér's $V$ of $0.42$ versus $0.33$): the
        opening votes predict the outcome more reliably when that outcome is correct.

  \item \textbf{It is the basin count behind self-reinforcement.} Bistability and
        self-reinforcement are two views of the same convergence. Monostable cells
        carry the steepest confidence ramps, and $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ anticorrelates
        with the mean self-reinforcement slope at the task level ($\rho = -0.75$ on
        GPQA, $-0.44$ on HiddenBench): when one attractor dominates, agents lock in
        early and confidence climbs unchecked, whereas when several coexist the
        reinforcement signal is split across diverging trajectories.
        Self-reinforcement is the within-basin amplifier; bistability counts the
        basins.
\end{enumerate}

Bistability is thus the structural counterpart of the amplifier: each task carries a
small set of stable outcomes fixed by the question itself, and the opening vote
distribution selects among them, so a diversity of attractors helps only when the
single basin it would otherwise collapse into is the wrong one.

\subsection{Dominance}
\label{sec:results:dominance}

Dominance measures how unequally influence is shared. From the confidence-weighted
conversion-credit graph, the Herfindahl index $D$ runs from $0$ (every agent
converts equally many peers) to $1$ (a single agent causes every opinion change);
the full construction is given in \autoref{sec:method:dominance}. One structural
caveat shapes what can be tested: in the star, the centre is the only agent a leaf
can ever convert to, so its raw $D$ is mechanically inflated and its surrogate null
degenerate for most repetitions. We therefore report raw $D$ for both topologies but
test for \emph{emergent} concentration, above what the wiring alone forces, only in
the fully-connected topology, where all agents are structurally equal (per-cell $D$
values in Appendix~\ref{app:dom:supp}). Three findings follow, moving from whether a
leader emerges, to whether that leader helps, to what its dominance costs.

\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
  \item \textbf{A leader emerges even without a hub.} In the fully-connected
        topology, where no agent is structurally privileged, conversion credit still
        concentrates on a de-facto leader above the topology-keyed null: the excess
        is positive in 49 of 50 GPQA tasks and all 50 HiddenBench tasks, and the
        fraction of repetitions flagged as significantly concentrated ($7.7\,\%$ on
        GPQA, $10.9\,\%$ on HiddenBench) runs well above the $5\,\%$ chance baseline
        (\autoref{fig:dom:excess}). On HiddenBench the effect strengthens with larger
        memory windows, as if more context lets a leader consolidate. The same
        endogenous asymmetry in nominally symmetric LLM groups is documented
        elsewhere, where agents form preferential-attachment links
        \citep{mehdizadeh2025homophily} and structural position predicts coordination
        success \citep{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory}.

  \item \textbf{A leader helps only when the debate chooses it.} Because the group
        converges onto the dominant agent's position, that agent's quality decides
        the outcome. Where the leader \emph{emerges} from a fully-connected debate,
        higher dominance goes with higher accuracy on HiddenBench ($\rho = +0.56$),
        and on GPQA the emergent hub even carries a genuine expertise signal (correct
        $42\,\%$ of the time against a $25\,\%$ baseline). Where the leader is instead
        \emph{imposed} by the wiring, the star hub, higher dominance goes with lower
        accuracy ($\rho = -0.29$ on HiddenBench), because a pre-assigned and often
        uninformed centre entrenches the shared initial bias. Dominance is thus not
        uniformly benign: it amplifies whichever agent leads, and only an
        endogenously selected leader tends to be the right one.

  \item \textbf{Concentration costs rounds.} Whatever it does to accuracy, dominance
        consistently slows convergence. Within each task, configurations with higher
        mean $D$ take more rounds to settle (per-task Spearman $\rho = +0.55$ on GPQA
        and $+0.73$ on HiddenBench, positive in nearly every task): a dominant agent
        must press its position across many rounds before subordinates capitulate.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{plots/dominance/fig2_excess_fc.png}
  \caption[Emergent dominance excess in fully-connected debates]{Left: distribution of excess $z$-scores in fc (testable
           repetitions only); dashed line: $z = 0$ (null).
           Right: flagged fraction by memory window; dashed: 5\,\%
           chance baseline.
           HiddenBench consistently exceeds GPQA in both magnitude and
           flagged rate.}
  \label{fig:dom:excess}
\end{figure}

Together these make dominance another face of the amplifier. The debate reliably
elects a leader even when its wiring imposes none, and the group's fate then follows
that leader: concentration helps when the leader is endogenously selected and
well-informed, and hurts when a fixed hub entrenches a shared error, while in every
case a more dominant leader makes consensus slower to reach. The contrast between an
emergent and an imposed hub is taken up directly in
\autoref{sec:results:eff_topology}.


\subsection{Limit Cycles}
\label{sec:results:lc}

Limit cycles are the rarest motif in this system, and the only one that is a failure
rather than a variant of convergence: a limit cycle is a debate that never settles,
oscillating between vote configurations instead of reaching a fixed point. We detect
them with categorical recurrence quantification, flagging a sequence when its
determinism exceeds a shuffle-null floor and its dominant period lies in a genuine
oscillatory band ($2 \leq \hat{P} \leq \lfloor L/2 \rfloor$); the full construction
is given in \autoref{sec:method:lc}. The cooperative design suppresses oscillation
almost entirely: $99.5\,\%$ of agent vote sequences and $98.7\,\%$ of system
composition sequences converge to fixed points and never enter the test. Three
findings follow.

\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
  \item \textbf{They are rare but genuine.} Of the small non-converged remainder, a
        significant periodic signature survives in 47 agent-level and 32 system-level
        cases, both far above the shuffle-null floor (detection funnel in
        Appendix~\ref{app:lc:supp}). Inspected individually, $70\,\%$ of the flagged
        agent cases are clean period-2 orbits whose recurrence matrices show the
        characteristic checkerboard stripe (Appendix~\ref{app:lc:supp}); the rest are
        near-convergent artefacts. The signal is real, not a test artefact.

  \item \textbf{They occur only in the star topology.} Almost every genuine cycle
        sits in a star configuration (45 of 47 agent-level, 26 of 32 system-level);
        applying the same criteria to the fully-connected cases leaves none that are
        genuine, all being stable-revisit or near-convergent artefacts. Flagged cases
        also concentrate on HiddenBench (34 of 47 agent-level).
        \autoref{fig:lc:timelines} shows the two clearest examples: a collective cycle
        in which all four agents alternate between two options in near-perfect
        synchrony for the full 15 rounds, and a hub-driven cycle in which the group
        follows a single agent's A$\leftrightarrow$C alternation from round~5 onward.

  \item \textbf{The mechanism is hub-driven majority inversion, purely an efficiency
        failure.} Every limit cycle runs to the hard round cap without ever reaching
        consensus, so early stopping never fires. Inspection suggests a consistent, if
        uncertified, causal chain: agents defer to an observed peer majority, the star
        hub rebroadcasts that majority for the opposite option after each flip, and
        with two independently defensible evidence threads and a hub that starts in
        the minority, each flip recreates the conditions that caused it. The absence
        of genuine cycles in the fully-connected topology follows directly: with no
        single node aggregating all peer signals, the inversion loop cannot form. The
        practical consequence is that a cycle's final majority vote is a low-confidence
        artefact of wherever it lands on round~15, so a hard round cap is the only
        reliable stop and repetitions that reach it should be flagged as unresolved
        rather than reported as a decision. Failure to recognise termination
        conditions is itself a catalogued failure mode of deployed multi-agent systems
        \citep{cemri2025mast}, and our result names a specific dynamical route to it.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/lc/lc_timelines_new.png}
  \caption[Vote-sequence timelines for two limit cycles]{Vote-sequence timelines for two representative limit cycles.
           Each cell shows the vote (letter) held by an agent (row) at
           a given round (column); colour encodes the option.
           Left (a): collective cycle, all four agents alternate together
           (GPQA / star / $W{=}2$; ground truth B).
           Right (b): hub-driven cycle, agents split into an alternating
           A$\leftrightarrow$C pattern from round~5 onward
           (HiddenBench / star / $W{=}5$; ground truth A).
           Neither repetition reaches consensus; both run to $T = 15$.}
  \label{fig:lc:timelines}
\end{figure}

Limit cycles are therefore the failure mode of the same convergence machinery seen
elsewhere: when a single aggregator keeps reconstructing the opposite majority,
self-reinforcement never gets to lock the group in, and the debate oscillates instead
of settling. That this happens only under the star, and never when every agent sees
every other, fits the corroborating observation that unanimous states are otherwise
effectively absorbing \citep{bertalanic2026costofconsensus}.

\subsection{Causal Evidence for the Amplifier Mechanism}
\label{sec:results:evid}

The four motif analyses characterise the \emph{dynamics} of debate.
This section asks a complementary question: does the opening vote composition
causally determine the outcome, or is that association confounded?
Three experiments address it in order: a within-task distribution analysis at
$R = 1{,}000$ that establishes the observational pattern, a seeded dose-response
that fixes the number of correct agents at $t=0$ and confirms the causal
interpretation, and an adversarial devil's-advocate probe that tests the
amplifier's robustness.

For the observational analysis, three individual GPQA questions (q84, q125, q144),
selected for mid-range accuracy and high initial-vote entropy to ensure genuinely
uncertain outcomes, were each run for $R = 1{,}000$ repetitions at $W = 2$,
fully-connected, $N = 4$.

\paragraph{Ground-truth presence in the initial vote distribution.}
The dominant finding is stark: whether the ground-truth option appears at all in
the initial vote distribution is by far the strongest predictor of final accuracy,
consistent across all three tasks
(\autoref{fig:highrep:init_votes_split}, \autoref{tab:highrep:init_votes}).

When the correct answer is absent from all initial votes, the system almost never
reaches it: accuracy is $0.4$\,\%, $3.1$\,\%, and $6.9$\,\% for q84, q125, and
q144 respectively ($\chi^2 > 120$, $p < 10^{-27}$ in all cases).
The rare correct answers in the absent condition are consistent with stochastic
noise rather than genuine recovery.
Within the present condition, accuracy is roughly $2\times$ higher when the correct
option also holds the initial plurality than when it is present but not the
plurality (e.g.\ $0.620$ vs $0.257$ for q84, the latter being the present-but-not-plurality
subgroup, i.e.\ the $743 - 213 = 530$ repetitions where the correct option is in the
opening profile but does not lead it).
These findings suggest that the system operates primarily as a vote amplifier:
debate selectively strengthens options already represented in the initial
distribution and overwhelmingly reinforces whichever option holds the initial
plurality.
The capacity to surface a correct answer that no agent endorsed at the outset is
negligible under the present design.

\paragraph{Debate length and confidence do not track correctness.}
Neither the length of a debate nor its confidence trajectory predicts whether it is
right: longer debates are no more accurate, and confidence rises monotonically across
rounds whether the final answer is correct or not (full feature-by-outcome
correlations in Appendix~\ref{app:highrep}). What the outcome does track is the
opening vote composition, which the next experiment manipulates directly.

\paragraph{Causal test: seeding correct agents.}
\label{sec:results:seeded}
The observational pattern is confounded: whether the ground truth is present at
$t=0$ co-varies with task difficulty, agent confidence, and initial entropy. To
isolate the causal contribution of the opening composition, we fix it directly. On
$50$ GPQA tasks (the same $W=2$, fully-connected, $N=4$ configuration, $R=50$
repetitions each) we build every repetition's initial four-agent vote profile by
sampling from a pool of genuine, independent single-agent responses under a fixed
number of correct seeds $k \in \{0,1,2,3\}$: exactly $k$ of the four agents begin on
the correct option, the rest on real wrong answers. Only the initial composition is
manipulated; every vote is a real response and the debate that follows is unmodified.
Sweeping $k$ turns the earlier binary contrast into a dose-response curve.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig4_seeded_causal.png}
  \caption[Causal dose-response of seeding correct agents]{Accuracy as a function of
           the number of correct agents seeded into the initial vote profile
           ($k \in \{0,1,2,3\}$) on $50$ GPQA tasks ($R=50$ each, $W=2$,
           fully-connected).
           (a) Per-task accuracy (grey, one line per task) and the mean over tasks
           (orange).
           (b) Pooled accuracy with Wilson $95\,\%$ CI (blue) against the accuracy a
           pure plurality vote at $t=0$ would already achieve (dashed grey). Debate
           exceeds this amplifier baseline for a correct minority or tie but falls
           below it for a correct supermajority.}
  \label{fig:highrep:seeded_causal}
\end{figure}

Four findings follow.

\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy rises monotonically with the number of correct seeds.}
        Pooled over $2{,}500$ repetitions per condition, accuracy climbs from
        $1.8\,\%$ at $k=0$ to $26.2\,\%$, $63.7\,\%$, and $86.3\,\%$ at $k=1,2,3$
        (\autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_causal}). This is not merely a pooled average:
        adding one correct seed raises per-task accuracy in $50$ of $50$ tasks for
        each of the first two steps and $47$ of $50$ for the last. Causally, the
        composition of the opening votes is the dominant lever on the outcome.

  \item \textbf{Debate is a lossy amplifier, not a reasoner.} Comparing each
        condition against the accuracy a pure plurality vote at $t=0$ would already
        achieve (dashed line in \autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_causal}) isolates what the
        debate itself contributes. Where the correct answer starts behind, debate
        helps: a lone correct minority ($k=1$, plurality accuracy $0\,\%$) is carried
        to the right answer $26\,\%$ of the time, and an even two--two split ($k=2$,
        baseline $38\,\%$) resolves correctly $64\,\%$ of the time. Where the correct
        answer starts ahead, debate \emph{erodes} it: a correct $3/4$ supermajority
        ($k=3$) that plurality voting would preserve with certainty is talked out of
        the right answer in roughly one repetition in seven ($86\,\%$, not $100\,\%$).
        And from a standing start it generates almost nothing, recovering the answer
        de novo in only $1.8\,\%$ of $k=0$ repetitions, confined to $21$ of $50$
        tasks. Debate amplifies the initial signal while blurring it toward a coin
        flip, rather than reasoning its way to the truth.

  \item \textbf{Outcomes are all-or-nothing and decided early.} Every repetition in
        every condition ends fully unanimous: the correct seeds either convert the
        whole group or capitulate entirely, with no repetition ending on the ground
        truth as a surviving minority. When the correct side wins it wins fast,
        reaching a plurality within the first one to two rounds (mean first round
        $1.3$). Persuasion is front-loaded; the opening exchange, not protracted
        deliberation, decides the outcome.

  \item \textbf{A single persistent wrong voice overturns the group.} The amplifier's
        indifference to correctness is starkest under adversarial perturbation.
        Re-running the $k=2$ condition with one of the two non-correct agents
        instructed to argue a wrong option throughout ($R=25$) collapses accuracy from
        $63.7\,\%$ to $10.0\,\%$, lower in all $50$ of $50$ tasks and worse than a lone
        correct minority with no adversary ($k=1$, $26.2\,\%$), even though the
        adversary starts outnumbered two-to-one. When these runs reach unanimity
        ($87\,\%$ of them), they converge to the adversary's wrong answer $99\,\%$ of
        the time. An agent that never updates signals conviction under the very rule
        that lets a correct minority occasionally win, so the group is pulled toward
        whichever voice is most persistent, not most correct.
\end{enumerate}

The dose-response places the system in a recognisable region of the social-dynamics
literature. A single correct agent is a committed $25\,\%$ minority, and
\citet{centola2018tipping} identify ${\approx}25\,\%$ as the critical mass at which a
committed minority overturns an established human convention; our correct minority
tips the group about one time in four, short of reliable takeover, plausibly because
$N=4$ admits no finer minority and our agents carry the explicit interaction memory
that \citet{centola2018tipping} find raises the threshold. The adversarial collapse
is the same mechanism run in reverse: \citet{agarwal2025cwpor} document a
confidence-weighted persuasion override, in which a confident-but-wrong interlocutor
talks a correct judge out of its answer, and \citet{yao2025sycophancy} formalise
inter-agent sycophancy as a driver of premature consensus, now targeted by
prompt-level mitigations \citep{pitre2025consensagent}.

Taken together, the seeded sweep upgrades the amplifier reading from correlation to
intervention: the multi-agent system is a \emph{lossy amplifier of its initial vote
distribution}. It reliably strengthens whatever the opening votes favour, more so as
that signal grows, yet it neither manufactures a correct answer that no agent held
nor protects a correct one that a persistent dissenter attacks. The composition of
the opening votes, not the length or intensity of the debate, is the dominant lever
on whether collective reasoning succeeds. This intervention was run on GPQA only; the
observational ground-truth-presence result holds on both benchmarks
(\autoref{sec:results:sr}), and extension of the causal claim to HiddenBench is
discussed in \autoref{sec:discussion:limitations}.

\subsection{Effective Topology}
\label{sec:results:eff_topology}

The topology parameter fixes who may read whom, but what matters for the outcome is
who actually moves whom. We extract the \emph{effective influence topology} of each
fully-connected run from its conversion-credit graph: whenever an agent flips its
vote, each neighbour that already held the new vote receives credit proportional to
the flipping agent's confidence (the credit function of the dominance metric,
\autoref{sec:results:dominance}). Summing over rounds and row-normalising assigns
each run one of four structural types (\autoref{fig:efftopo:examples}):
\emph{flat} (influence nearly uniform), \emph{hub} (one moderately dominant agent),
\emph{two-hub} (two agents of comparable influence), and \emph{star} (one agent
responsible for $\geq 75\,\%$ of all conversions). Three findings follow.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/eff_topology/fig1_topology_examples.pdf}
\caption[Effective topology types in FC runs]{%
  Archetypal examples of the four effective topologies observed in
  fully-connected runs ($N{=}4$).
  Arrow width is proportional to vote-flip influence weight.
  \textbf{Orange fill}: hub (most influential agent).
  \textbf{Green border}: agent that held the correct answer at round~0.
  The hub-share percentage is shown below each panel.}
\label{fig:efftopo:examples}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
  \item \textbf{Influence self-organises into a hierarchy, usually a two-hub one.}
        Even though every agent in a fully-connected run is wired identically,
        influence is almost never uniform: flat runs are rare (${\approx}5$--$7\,\%$).
        The dominant type is two-hub ($58.7\,\%$ of GPQA runs, $59.2\,\%$ of
        HiddenBench), two agents of comparable influence acting as competing faction
        leaders, and its most common origin is a two--two initial vote split. A clean
        star, one agent driving three-quarters of all conversions, is rare
        ($2.3\,\%$ GPQA, $5.4\,\%$ HiddenBench).

  \item \textbf{The rare star is far more accurate, because its hub is selected, not
        imposed.} Effective star runs reach by far the highest accuracy on both
        benchmarks ($0.485$ on GPQA and $0.562$ on HiddenBench, against fully-connected
        means of $0.423$ and $0.264$; \autoref{fig:efftopo:accuracy}), while the far
        more common two-hub tracks near or below the mean. The reason is not that a hub
        structure is intrinsically good but that, when a star forms, the debate has
        \emph{elected} a hub that disproportionately started correct: $54.5\,\%$ of
        GPQA star hubs and $85.5\,\%$ of HiddenBench star hubs held the right answer at
        round~0, against a $25\,\%$ chance baseline. The outcome then follows that hub
        almost deterministically, correct in $86\,\%$ (GPQA) and $92\,\%$ (HiddenBench)
        of runs when the hub started right and collapsing to $16\,\%$ and $11\,\%$ when
        it started wrong. The two-hub battle is mediocre precisely because it lacks
        this selection signal: its correct faction wins $61\,\%$ of the time when
        present, but the group still converges, on a wrong answer, when no agent
        starts correct. The star is the amplifier at its most concentrated, and it
        happens to amplify the agent most likely to be right.

  \item \textbf{Diverse initialisation is the lever that produces stars.} Whether a
        star forms is driven almost entirely by the opening vote spread: with all four
        agents starting on the same option P(star) is $0.3\,\%$, rising monotonically
        to $3.3\,\%$, $6.7\,\%$, and $22.7\,\%$ as the number of distinct starting
        votes grows to four (\autoref{fig:efftopo:design}). This yields a concrete
        design guideline: prefer a fully-connected topology with deliberately diverse
        initialisation over a prescribed star. A nominal star fixes its hub before the
        debate can select one, and on the hard HiddenBench questions that pre-assigned
        centre holds the correct answer only $15\,\%$ of the time, against the
        $85.5\,\%$ of an FC-elected hub. Enforced perspective diversity has been
        proposed independently as a remedy for belief entrenchment
        \citep{oh2026entrenchment} and follows from the theoretical analysis of debate
        homogeneity \citep{estornell2024multillm}; our contribution is the mechanism,
        that diversity works by raising the chance the debate elects an influence hub,
        and the elected hub tends to have started correct.
\end{enumerate}

  Star topology is the clear accuracy leader on both benchmarks;

\end{figure}

Effective topology is the within-run face of the amplifier. The debate reliably
elects an influence hierarchy that the wiring never specified, and the group's answer
follows whichever agent wins that election, so accuracy is high exactly when the
elected hub is the correct one. This is the structural counterpart to the seeded
result (\autoref{sec:results:evid}), which establishes the same dependence on the
opening composition causally, and to dominance (\autoref{sec:results:dominance}),
which measures the same concentration in aggregate. It also mirrors reports that
nominally symmetric LLM populations develop endogenous structural asymmetry, whether
through an agent's betweenness and clustering predicting its coordination success
\citep{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory} or through self-formed preferential-attachment
links \citep{mehdizadeh2025homophily}.
