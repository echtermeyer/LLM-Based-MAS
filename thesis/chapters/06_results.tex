\subsection{System Performance}
\label{sec:results:perf}

This section reports overall system accuracy and efficiency across all
420 task--configuration combinations (35 tasks $\times$ 2 datasets $\times$
3 memory windows $\times$ 2 topologies, $R = 50$ repetitions each,
$N = 21{,}000$ total).
Two metrics are reported throughout:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy}: fraction of repetitions in which the majority vote
        at the final round matches the ground-truth answer.
  \item \textbf{Efficiency}: mean number of rounds until the early-stopping
        criterion fires or the hard cap $T = 15$ is reached.
\end{itemize}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Single-Agent Baseline and Debate Gain}
\label{sec:results:perf:single_vs_multi}

To isolate the contribution of debate, we compare the multi-agent outcome
against two baselines defined at round~0, before any discussion takes place:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-agent accuracy}: the fraction of individual agent answers
        that are correct at round~0, averaged across all four agents and all
        repetitions.
  \item \textbf{Majority-vote baseline}: the accuracy of the plurality vote
        over all four agents at round~0 (ties broken arbitrarily).
        This is the best a voting ensemble can do without any interaction
        and represents the fair single-agent comparison point.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{plots/general/fig1_single_vs_multi.png}
  \caption[Accuracy decomposition: per-agent, majority, debate]{Accuracy decomposition for GPQA (left) and HiddenBench (right):
           per-agent accuracy at round~0, majority-vote accuracy at round~0,
           and multi-agent accuracy after debate.
           Arrow and annotation show the debate gain (majority $\to$ final).
           Dashed line: random chance at $25\,\%$ for four-option questions;
           note that most HiddenBench tasks have three options, where the chance
           level is $33\,\%$ (see text).
           All configurations pooled ($N = 10{,}500$ per dataset).}
  \label{fig:perf:single_vs_multi}
\end{figure}

with an oracle upper bound (fraction of repetitions in which at least one
agent holds the correct answer at round~0).

The two datasets tell qualitatively different stories
(\autoref{fig:perf:single_vs_multi}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GPQA.}
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $36.7\,\%$, and majority voting
        raises this modestly to $39.4\,\%$ by aggregating four independent
        guesses.
        Debate adds a further $\mathbf{+1.3}$\,percentage points (pp),
        reaching $40.7\,\%$.
        This marginal gain is consistent with findings across the multi-agent
        debate literature, where homogeneous agents on knowledge-intensive
        tasks tend to show small absolute accuracy improvements over single-agent
        baselines \citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, liu2025stopovervaluing}.
        The model has reasonable prior knowledge on GPQA questions; debate
        refines but does not transform the outcome.
        Notably, the oracle bound of $68.8\,\%$ reveals that the correct answer
        is present among the agents in roughly two thirds of runs: the limiting
        factor is not knowledge but the group's ability to identify and amplify
        the correct voice.

  \item \textbf{HiddenBench.}
        Here the dynamics are strikingly different.
        The per-agent accuracy at round~0 is $15.5\,\%$, already well below
        random.
        Majority voting does not help, it \emph{reduces} accuracy further
        to $8.3\,\%$.
        This is a direct consequence of correlated errors: agents do not fail
        randomly across the options but share a systematic bias, all
        gravitating towards the same wrong answer.
        When four agents independently commit to the same wrong option,
        majority voting amplifies that shared error rather than cancelling it
        out, an LLM analogue of the biased information sampling documented
        in human hidden-profile experiments \citep{stasser1985pooling}.
        Debate reverses this partial collapse: final accuracy reaches
        $29.4\,\%$, a gain of $\mathbf{+21.1}$\,pp over the majority baseline.
        This gain is the substantive effect; in absolute terms $29.4\,\%$ is
        still \emph{at or slightly below} the chance level, because most
        HiddenBench tasks have three options rather than four (31 of 35 tasks,
        $M = 3$, so the repetition-weighted chance level is $\approx 32\,\%$
        rather than $25\,\%$; see below).
        The oracle bound of $46.6\,\%$ confirms that the correct option is
        present among the agents in roughly half of runs; debate recovers
        roughly $\frac{29.4 - 8.3}{46.6 - 8.3} \approx 55\,\%$ of the
        accuracy that is in principle recoverable from the majority baseline.
\end{itemize}

Because the chance level differs by option count, we report HiddenBench accuracy
split by $M$.
The $31$ three-option tasks reach $30.3\,\%$ (chance $33.3\,\%$) and the $4$
four-option tasks reach $22.7\,\%$ (chance $25\,\%$); both sit just below their
respective chance lines.
The correct reading is therefore not that debate lifts HiddenBench above chance,
but that it recovers most of the ground lost by the majority-biased starting
point: the starting majority vote is far below chance ($8.3\,\%$), and debate
returns the group roughly to the chance line.

\paragraph{Correlated errors, two ways.}
The oracle bounds make the difference between the two benchmarks precise.
If the four agents erred independently, the probability that at least one holds
the correct answer at round~0 would be $1 - (1 - p_{\mathrm{agent}})^4$, where
$p_{\mathrm{agent}}$ is per-agent accuracy.
For GPQA this predicts $1 - (1 - 0.367)^4 = 83.9\,\%$, well above the observed
oracle of $68.8\,\%$: GPQA agents' errors are \emph{positively correlated} in the
``is anyone right'' sense, they tend to be wrong on the same questions.
For HiddenBench the independence prediction is $1 - (1 - 0.155)^4 = 49.0\,\%$,
almost exactly the observed oracle of $46.6\,\%$: at the level of ``is the correct
option present,'' HiddenBench agents are close to independent.
The HiddenBench collapse is therefore not a failure to cover the correct answer
but a correlation in \emph{which wrong option} the agents choose, which is what
drives the majority vote down to $8.3\,\%$ while the oracle stays near the
independence prediction.
This is the sharper statement of the ``systematic bias'' described above.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
The contrast between GPQA and HiddenBench is not simply a difficulty
difference.
It reflects a qualitative shift in the role of debate.
On GPQA, debate acts as a \emph{refinement}: agents start mostly correct,
and the system consolidates.
On HiddenBench, debate acts as a partial \emph{correction}: agents share a
systematic bias that drives the majority vote well below chance, and structured
discussion recovers most of that deficit without pushing the group clearly above
chance.
The self-reinforcement, limit-cycle, bistability, and dominance analyses in
the following sections explain when and why this correction succeeds or fails.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Topology has no detectable effect on accuracy.}

        against star across the $35$ tasks, the difference is negligible and its
        $95\,\%$ confidence interval contains zero for both datasets
        (GPQA $\Delta \approx 0.0$\,pp, $p = 0.96$; HiddenBench
        $\Delta = +1.6$\,pp, $p = 0.16$). We find no evidence that the choice of
        topology determines whether the system reaches the correct answer.

  \item \textbf{Topology has a large and significant effect on efficiency.}
        Star topology requires $0.47$ more rounds on GPQA and $1.84$ more
        rounds on HiddenBench compared to fully connected (both $p < 0.001$).
        The hub bottleneck in the star slows convergence: every spoke must
        route information through a single central node, which aggregates
        conflicting signals and commits more slowly.

  \item \textbf{The efficiency cost of star is larger on HiddenBench.}
        On questions where agents disagree more persistently, the hub receives
        more conflicting input per round and takes longer to commit.
        On GPQA, where agents converge quickly regardless of topology, the
        hub bottleneck is less consequential.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Effect of Memory Window}
\label{sec:results:perf:window}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{plots/general/fig3_memory_window.png}
  \caption[Accuracy and rounds by memory window]{Accuracy (left column) and mean rounds (right column) for
           $W \in \{1, 2, 5\}$ across both datasets and topologies.
           Blue: fully connected; red: star.
           At the task level (task-blocked Friedman over $n = 35$ tasks), the
           memory window has no effect on accuracy in three of the four
           dataset/topology cells; HiddenBench/fc is nominally significant
           ($p = 0.019$) but does not survive Benjamini--Hochberg correction
           across the four cells ($q = 0.076$).}
  \label{fig:perf:memory_window}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Memory window has essentially no effect on accuracy.}
        Task-blocked Friedman tests (unit $=$ task, $n = 35$) are
        non-significant for GPQA/fc ($p = 0.17$), GPQA/star ($p = 0.28$) and
        HiddenBench/star ($p = 0.68$). The one exception, HiddenBench/fc
        ($p = 0.019$, with $W{=}2$ peaking at $32.1\,\%$), does not survive
        Benjamini--Hochberg correction across the four cells ($q = 0.076$), so
        we treat it as at most a weak, isolated signal rather than a general
        memory effect. Whether agents receive only the previous round ($W=1$)
        or the last five rounds ($W=5$) does not systematically change how
        often the system reaches the correct answer.

  \item \textbf{Memory window affects convergence speed on HiddenBench.}
        Larger $W$ is associated with faster convergence: $W=1$ produces
        the most rounds (fc: $5.47$, star: $7.56$), while $W=5$ is the
        fastest (fc: $4.99$, star: $6.54$), with $W=2$ intermediate
        (task-blocked Friedman $p < 0.001$ for both HiddenBench topologies).
        A longer context window may allow agents to track the trajectory
        of the debate and resolve disagreements more efficiently.
        On GPQA the window effect on rounds is at most small: GPQA/fc shows a
        modest, non-monotonic variation (Friedman $p = 0.013$, range
        $3.64$--$3.85$ rounds) while GPQA/star is not significant ($p = 0.68$).

  \item \textbf{The expected monotonic ordering is observed on HiddenBench}
        but absent on GPQA.
        Since GPQA agents converge in very few rounds regardless of $W$,
        additional history provides no useful signal.
        On HiddenBench, where convergence is slow and contested, more context
        appears to help agents distinguish a genuine emerging consensus from
        transient fluctuations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Round Distributions and the Rounds--Accuracy Asymmetry}
\label{sec:results:perf:rounds}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/general/fig4_round_distribution.png}
  \caption[Round-count distributions by configuration]{Fraction of repetitions stopping at each round count,
           for all four (dataset, topology) panels and all three memory
           windows (colours).
           The hard cap at $T = 15$ is marked by the dashed red line.
           The minimum possible value is $T_r = 2$: with early-stopping
           threshold $u = 3$, three consecutive unanimous rounds
           (rounds 0, 1, 2) trigger an early stop.}
  \label{fig:perf:round_dist}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GPQA converges fast.}
        The distribution is strongly right-skewed with a mode at
        rounds 2--3.
        Very few repetitions reach the hard cap:
        $p_{\mathrm{cap}} \leq 1.6\,\%$ across all GPQA configurations.

  \item \textbf{HiddenBench converges slowly and often fails to converge.}
        The distribution is substantially flatter and shifted right.
        The hard cap is reached in $1.2$--$14.3\,\%$ of repetitions
        depending on configuration, with star topologies accounting for
        the highest failure rates ($11.0$--$14.3\,\%$).
        This spike represents pure efficiency failures: the group never
        reached unanimous consensus within the round budget.
        Fully-connected topologies show dramatically lower cap rates
        ($1.2$--$3.3\,\%$), reflecting their faster convergence.

  \item \textbf{Star shifts the distribution right across both datasets.}
        Both topology panels in each dataset row show that star has a
        heavier tail and a larger cap spike than fully connected,
        consistent with the mean-rounds differences in
        \autoref{tab:perf:topology}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Rounds and accuracy.}
The relationship between rounds to consensus and accuracy reveals a
striking asymmetry (full plot in Appendix~\ref{app:perf:rounds_acc}).
On GPQA, accuracy peaks at rounds~3--4 ($47.1\,\%$) and is lower both
for the earliest stops at round~2 ($36.1\,\%$) and for longer debates
(around $40\,\%$ or below at most later stopping points, though the
individual late-round buckets are sparse): neither very fast nor very slow
consensus is reliably correct.
On HiddenBench the pattern is opposite: repetitions stopping at round~2
achieve only $6.4\,\%$ accuracy, far below random, because early
consensus means the group has locked in on the majority-biased wrong answer
before any corrective exchange.
Accuracy rises steadily with more rounds: $23.8\,\%$ at round~3,
$31.7\,\%$ at round~4, $33.1\,\%$ at round~5.

This asymmetry is fundamental to understanding the dynamics reported in the
following sections.
On GPQA, self-reinforcement of an early consensus is often adaptive.
On HiddenBench, it is the primary failure mode: agents self-reinforce a
shared bias, and the system reaches confident unanimity on the wrong answer.
Full per-configuration accuracy, efficiency, cap rates, and vote-flip counts
are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:perf:configs}.


\subsection{Self-Reinforcement}
\label{sec:results:sr}

Self-reinforcement (SR) is measured as the OLS slope $\beta_{\mathrm{run}}$
of agent confidence on round number within each \emph{vote-stable run}
(a maximal block of $\geq 3$ consecutive rounds where an agent holds the same
vote).
The prevalence $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ is the fraction of runs with
$\beta_{\mathrm{run}} > 0$; the null is 0.5 (random walk).
Across the full dataset (21\,000 repetitions, both benchmarks, all 6 configs)
the length filter yields 94\,173 run records.
Of the 84\,000 agent trajectories, 43.5\,\% (36\,531) never flip their vote
across the entire debate and form the extreme SR group; because a debate is
recorded only once it has reached at least three rounds, every one of these
never-flipping trajectories yields a vote-stable run of length $\geq 3$ and is
retained.
This agent-level count should not be confused with the ``never flipped'' subgroup
of \autoref{tab:sr:subgroups} ($n = 12{,}748$ runs), which is defined at the
\emph{repetition} level: it collects the runs that belong to the subset of
repetitions in which \emph{no} agent ever flipped (a fully static debate), a
stricter condition than an individual agent holding its vote while others move.
The two numbers therefore measure different things, individual never-flipping
agents versus runs inside fully static repetitions, and are not expected to match.

\paragraph{Prevalence.}
\autoref{tab:sr:overview} and \autoref{fig:sr:psr_violin} show
$p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ and mean $\bar\beta$ for all 12 (dataset, topology, $W$)
cells.
$p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ ranges from 0.742 to 0.875 across all cells, well above
the null of 0.5.
In every cell all 35 tasks individually exceed 0.5
(binomial sign test: $p < 10^{-4}$ in all 12 cells).
Cluster-bootstrapped 95\,\% confidence intervals for $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$
exclude 0.5 in all 12 cells; the same is true for $\bar\beta$ (all CIs
exclude 0).
\textbf{Self-reinforcement is a universal property of this system: it is
present in every configuration and on both benchmarks.}

\paragraph{Configuration effects.}
\autoref{fig:sr:mean_slope_W} and \autoref{fig:sr:heatmap}
show how $\bar\beta$ varies across configurations.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Memory window ($W$).}

        the Page trend test for decreasing order
        ($\bar\beta(W{=}1) > \bar\beta(W{=}2) > \bar\beta(W{=}5)$)

        On HiddenBench the trend is weaker (HB/star only marginally
        significant at $p = 0.037$), suggesting that peer re-exposure,
        rather than self re-exposure in longer contexts, drives
        confidence accumulation.

        Fully-connected (fc) consistently produces higher $\bar\beta$ than star.
        The difference is highly significant for HiddenBench across all $W$

$W$ effect (at most 0.10).

highest slopes of any subgroup

\paragraph{Mediation: diversity $\to$ SR $\to$ rounds.}
The subgroup results show that more dispersed initial vote configurations
are associated with both lower SR and more rounds.
To test whether SR mediates this diversity--rounds relationship,
we applied Baron--Kenny mediation~\citep{baron1986moderator} with a
5\,000-draw \emph{task-stratified} percentile bootstrap (resampling whole
tasks, so the interval respects the clustering of repetitions within a task).
Variables were z-scored within each (dataset, topology, $W$) cell
to partial out config-level means ($n = 20{,}997$ repetitions).

More diverse starts produce more rounds ($b = +0.402$, step 1) and
suppress SR ($b = -0.376$, step 2).
After controlling for diversity, SR strongly predicts fewer rounds
($b = -0.542$, step 3).
The indirect path (diversity reduces SR, which in turn increases rounds)
accounts for approximately \textbf{51\,\%} of the total effect,
with the remaining 49\,\% being a direct path from diversity to rounds
(richer argument exchange, more vote flips).
This pooled proportion slightly exceeds both dataset-specific values
(GPQA: 44\,\%, HiddenBench: 48\,\%); the pooled estimate is not a within-dataset
quantity but is computed on the pooled, config-standardised sample, so it can lie
outside the range of the two strata when the $X$--$M$ and $M$--$Y$ associations
differ between them. We read all three as indicating that SR is a major but not
exclusive mediator of why diverse initial conditions produce longer debates.
Two caveats attach to this analysis. First, we use the Baron--Kenny causal-steps
decomposition~\citep{baron1986moderator} with a bootstrapped indirect effect;
this is a regression decomposition under standard no-unmeasured-confounding
assumptions, not a counterfactual mediation analysis, and the mechanical
association between run length and the opportunity for a slope to accumulate
(\autoref{sec:discussion:limitations}) means the causal ordering should be read
cautiously. Second, the $n = 20{,}997$ repetitions (of $21{,}000$) exclude three
runs for which the diversity or SR summary was undefined.

\paragraph{Efficiency.}
\autoref{fig:sr:efficiency} shows task-level mean rounds plotted against
mean $\bar\beta$ for all (dataset, topology) panels.
The Spearman correlation between $\bar\beta$ and mean rounds to consensus is
negative in all 12 cells; computed at the task level ($n = 35$ tasks per cell)
it ranges from $\rho = -0.77$ to $-0.91$ ($p < 10^{-6}$ throughout).
Higher SR is consistently associated with \emph{fewer} rounds across all
configurations.
This finding is the clearest and most consistent result in the SR analysis:
\textbf{self-reinforcement is the convergence mechanism of the debate.}

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
\autoref{fig:sr:accuracy} shows $\bar\beta$ against task accuracy.
\emph{Marginally}, the point-biserial correlation between SR and correctness
is near zero in all 12 cells: $r_{pb}$ ranges from $-0.007$ to $+0.052$
at the repetition level, and none of the GPQA cells reach significance
($p > 0.3$ in all six).

The marginal flatness is, however, a consequence of conditioning on the full
dataset, not a substantive null result.
When repetitions are split by whether the ground-truth option appeared in the
initial vote distribution, the picture reverses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GT absent from initial votes} (31\,\% of GPQA reps,
        53\,\% of HiddenBench reps): SR is \emph{negatively} associated with
        accuracy ($r_{pb} = -0.11$ on GPQA, $-0.04$ on HiddenBench).
        At the task level the GPQA effect survives (cluster-bootstrap $95\,\%$
        CI $[-0.16, -0.06]$), whereas the HiddenBench effect does not
        (CI $[-0.08, +0.01]$, which includes zero). When no agent starts on
        the correct answer, faster lock-in means the system converges on a
        wrong answer sooner.

  \item \textbf{GT present in initial votes}: SR is \emph{positively}
        associated with accuracy ($r_{pb} = +0.20$ on GPQA, $+0.18$ on
        HiddenBench; both $p < 10^{-10}$).  When the correct answer is
        already represented, stronger reinforcement amplifies the correct
        initial position.
\end{itemize}

These two effects are of similar magnitude and opposite sign, cancelling in
the marginal to produce the near-zero $r_{pb}$ observed across the full
sample.
The linear probability model confirms this: with the slope standardised, the
interaction term (SR $\times$ GT presence) is $+0.11$ per standard deviation of
$\beta$ for both datasets ($p < 10^{-37}$); in raw slope units the interaction
is $+0.42$ (GPQA) and $+0.48$ (HiddenBench). We use the linear
probability model here because the coefficient is directly interpretable as a
change in the probability of a correct outcome, which is the quantity of interest;
the same interaction can be estimated with a logistic link if a bounded-probability
specification is preferred.

A further analysis shows that high-SR repetitions amplify the initial vote
distribution more strongly: the Pearson correlation between initial GT
fraction and accuracy is $r = 0.86$ in the high-SR half versus $r = 0.54$
in the low-SR half (GPQA; HiddenBench: $0.59$ vs.\ $0.42$).
\textbf{SR does not select for correct answers; it amplifies the initial
vote composition.}
Whether this helps accuracy depends entirely on whether the initial
distribution already favours the correct option, a point the high-repetition
analysis also establishes independently
(\autoref{sec:results:highrep}).

Also notable: confidence gain is essentially independent of correctness.
Mean confidence rise from round 0 to the final round is 1.66 points (correct)
vs.\ 1.64 points (incorrect) on GPQA and 1.51 vs.\ 1.48 on HiddenBench.
At the task level the correct-versus-incorrect difference is not significant
on GPQA (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.10$, with an unstable sign) and only marginal
on HiddenBench ($p = 0.004$, magnitude $\approx 0.05$ points).
The system grows more confident regardless of whether its final answer is
right or wrong.

\subsection{Limit Cycles}
\label{sec:results:lc}

Limit cycles are the rarest motif in the dataset.
The cooperative debate design strongly suppresses sustained oscillation:
99.4\,\% of agent vote sequences and 98.6\,\% of system composition sequences
converge to fixed points and are excluded before any test is run.
\autoref{tab:lc:funnel} shows the full detection funnel.
Of the small fraction that fail to converge (candidates), 6.3\,\% (agent)
and 7.9\,\% (system) carry a statistically significant periodic signature,
yielding \textbf{29 agent-level} and \textbf{23 system-level} limit cycles
across the dataset.
Both counts are well above the null floor. Assessed at the repetition level
(the unit that is independent under the null, since star spokes driven by a
common hub are not independent successes), the agent-level excess gives
$p \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-7}$ (17 flagged repetitions spanning 15 tasks) and
the system-level excess $p \approx 2 \times 10^{-16}$: the flagged cases are
genuine periodic structure, not test artefacts.

\paragraph{Topology.}
The star topology dominates: 27 of 29 agent-level and 18 of 23 system-level
flagged cases occur in star configurations.
All 52 flagged cases (both topologies) were inspected under the same fixed
criteria used in the oscillation taxonomy below, a case counts as a genuine
period-2 oscillation only if it shows a clean alternation with $\hat{P} = 2$,
$DET \geq 0.70$, and no dominant long hold, so the classification is not applied
selectively to the fc cases. Under these criteria the 7 fc cases (2 agent + 5
system) are all stable-revisit or near-convergent artefacts (large uniform blocks
from vote persistence, no periodic diagonal stripes), whereas the majority of star
cases pass.
\textbf{Only the star topology produces genuine limit cycles}, the same
conclusion as in the qualitative analysis of the mechanism described below.

\paragraph{Dataset.}
Flagged cases concentrate in HiddenBench: 24 of 29 agent-level, 21 of 23
system-level.
GPQA contributes a notable cluster: 5 agent flags all arise from two questions
(qid~90 and qid~98, both star/$W{=}2$).
The qid~90 / rep~43 case is the clearest collective cycle in the new dataset:
all four agents flag as genuine period-2 oscillation simultaneously
(DET ranging from 0.86 to 1.00), indicating full group synchrony.

\paragraph{Oscillation taxonomy.}
Among the 29 flagged agent cases, 20 (69\,\%) are classified as genuine
period-2 oscillations (alternating sequence with no dominant long hold,
$DET \geq 0.70$, $\hat{P} = 2$).
The remaining 9 are cases whose determinism falls below the $0.70$ threshold
or that contain a dominant long hold (4), higher-period stable-revisit
patterns (4), or a single period-8 artefact.
At the system level, 7 of 23 cases are genuine period-2 oscillations.

\autoref{fig:lc:timelines} shows vote-sequence timelines for the two
most informative cases.

for all 15 rounds.
The right panel (HiddenBench/star/$W{=}5$/q20/rep~45) shows a hub-driven
cycle: the group starts fragmented, the hub (agent~0) locks into an
A$\leftrightarrow$C alternation by round~5, and three of the four agents
follow in synchrony until the hard cap.

\paragraph{Cross-level co-occurrence.}
Six repetitions are flagged simultaneously at both agent and system level;
five are in HiddenBench/star and the sixth is the GPQA/star qid~90/rep~43
collective cycle described above, in which all four agents oscillate in
synchrony.
This is consistent with the mechanism: when the hub drives a genuine period-2
cycle, all spokes typically join within one or two rounds, producing a
system-level flag as well as individual agent-level flags.

\paragraph{Non-convergence as a task property.}
Non-convergence rates differ by task: on GPQA no task exceeds 1.7\,\% of
repetitions failing to converge; on HiddenBench three tasks exceed 5\,\%
(qid~8: 8.0\,\%, qid~9: 5.7\,\%, qid~31: 5.3\,\%).
Cross-config rank consistency of per-task non-convergence is moderate
(HiddenBench cross-config Spearman mean $\rho = 0.18$, GPQA near zero),
suggesting that non-convergence reflects task-level ambiguity more on
HiddenBench than on GPQA.

\paragraph{Mechanism.}
Inspection of the flagged cases suggests a consistent causal picture, which we
state as a hypothesis rather than a certified mechanism.
Every limit cycle runs to the hard round cap ($T = 15$): limit cycles are a
pure efficiency failure in which early stopping never triggers.
The proposed causal chain has five conditions that appear to interact:
(1) unconditional
social deference (agents abandon their own Phase~A argument in Phase~B
upon observing a peer majority); (2) the hub's broadcast power in the star
topology reconstructs the majority for the opposite option after each flip;
(3) arguments neither escalate nor resolve the underlying evidence ambiguity
across rounds; (4) the question provides two independently defensible evidence
threads with no dominant refutation; (5) the hub begins in a minority or tie.
When all five hold, each flip appears to recreate the conditions that caused it,
producing a self-sustaining alternation.
The absence of genuine limit cycles in fc is consistent with condition~(2):
no single node aggregates all peer signals simultaneously in fc, so the
hub-driven inversion loop cannot form.
A full qualitative log analysis certifying this chain, of the kind we conducted
on an earlier dataset, is left to future work; here the five conditions are
offered as a reading of the flagged trajectories, not as a proven cause.

\paragraph{Accuracy and design implications.}
Because every LC repetition runs to the hard cap without reaching consensus,
the group's final majority vote is a low-confidence artefact of wherever
the oscillation happens to land on round~15.
LC prevalence is too low for reliable inferential comparison, but the
structural implication is clear: star topology is the only configuration in
this system capable of producing genuine limit cycles, and a hard round cap
is the only reliable stopping condition for those cases.
Flagging repetitions that reach $T$ as unresolved, rather than returning a
spurious majority, is the recommended system response.

\subsection{Bistability}
\label{sec:results:bistability}

Bistability is measured per (task, config) cell using two complementary
signatures: $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (inverse Simpson diversity over endpoint
attractors, measuring coexistence) and Cramér's $V$ with a permutation
$p$-value (measuring whether the initial-vote distribution selects the
attractor, i.e.\ basin sensitivity).
A repetition is eligible if it settles (trailing-constant $k \geq 3$) on a
unanimous endpoint; non-unanimous-split endings are excluded
(2.12\,\% of reps).
Cells are labelled \emph{monostable} ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} < 1.5$),
\emph{multistable} ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5$ and $p_{\mathrm{basin}} < 0.05$),
or \emph{stochastic} ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5$ but $p_{\mathrm{basin}} \geq 0.05$).

\paragraph{Convergence accounting.}
Of 21\,000 repetitions, 98.6\,\% settle within the hard round cap and
96.5\,\% converge to a unanimous attractor.
No cell has fewer than two converged repetitions, so all 420 cells are
classified.

\paragraph{Prevalence.}
\autoref{tab:bistab:overview} summarises the label distribution.
Of the 420 cells, 90 (21.4\,\%) are monostable, 176 (41.9\,\%) are
multistable, and 154 (36.7\,\%) are stochastic.
\autoref{fig:bistab:violin} shows the $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $V$
distributions stratified by option count $M$.
$N_{\mathrm{eff}} > 1.5$ in 330 of 420 cells (78.6\,\%);
the overall mean is 2.06 (median 2.01).

The stochastic class deserves comment: every one of the 154 stochastic cells
has $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5$ (mean 2.31).
Stochastic cells are \emph{not} monostable, they exhibit genuine
multi-attractor coexistence at roughly the same $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ level as
multistable cells (mean 2.27), but the final attractor does not depend
deterministically on the initial-vote composition.
The distinction between multistable and stochastic is therefore about
\emph{basin sensitivity}, not about the number of attractors.

\paragraph{Configuration effects.}
\autoref{fig:bistab:heatmaps} shows mean $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $V$
across all 12 (dataset, topology, $W$) configurations.
Neither topology (fc vs.\ star) nor memory window $W$ produces a significant
or consistent effect on either metric. Testing at the task level (unit $=$
task, $n = 35$), all 12 topology comparisons (per dataset $\times$ $W$, for
each metric) have paired differences whose $95\,\%$ confidence intervals contain
zero (smallest Mann--Whitney $p = 0.16$; none survive Benjamini--Hochberg
correction), and the memory-$W$ effect is non-significant in all eight
(dataset, topology) strata (effect sizes near zero, none surviving
correction). We report this as an absence of evidence for a structural effect
rather than as proof of strict equivalence.
\textbf{Bistability is, to the precision we can measure, topology- and
memory-invariant in this system.}

\paragraph{Task-level consistency.}
The question of whether bistability is a property of the task or a config
artefact is answered by the pairwise Spearman correlation of per-task
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ across the six configurations.
For GPQA the mean pairwise $\rho = 0.913$ (min 0.869, max 0.941);
for HiddenBench $\rho = 0.782$ (min 0.597, max 0.880).
Of the 35 GPQA tasks, 17 are labelled multistable in $\geq 4$ of the 6
configs, and 9 HiddenBench tasks meet this criterion.
\textbf{Bistability is a stable property of specific tasks, not a config
artefact.}

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
\autoref{fig:bistab:accuracy} plots $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ against task
accuracy.
On GPQA the Spearman correlation is $\rho = +0.03$, flat.
On HiddenBench the cell-level correlation is a weak positive
($\rho = +0.15$), but it does not survive aggregation to the task level:
the task-level Spearman ($n = 35$) is $\rho = +0.14$ ($p = 0.43$) on
HiddenBench and $\rho = +0.04$ ($p = 0.81$) on GPQA, and the partial
correlation controlling for initial-vote diversity $D_0$ is likewise
non-significant on HiddenBench ($\rho_{\mathrm{partial}} = +0.18$,
$p = 0.32$).
We therefore do not read $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ as predicting accuracy in either
direction; the apparent cell-level association reflects the non-independence
of repetitions within a task.

Label-wise, on HiddenBench stochastic cells have the lowest mean accuracy
(0.256) and multistable cells the highest (0.338), but a task-clustered
bootstrap of this difference straddles zero (diff $= -0.082$, $95\,\%$ CI
$[-0.17, +0.00]$, $p \approx 0.06$), so the apparent accuracy penalty on
random-convergence cells is at most a marginal task-level trend rather than
a significant effect.

\paragraph{Correct-basin depth.}
A notable finding concerns the association between attractor correctness and
basin selection strength.
Because plug-in Cramér's $V$ depends on the contingency-table dimension, we
make this comparison within a fixed option count: among the HiddenBench
three-option ($M = 3$) cells, those whose dominant attractor is the correct
answer have substantially higher $V$ than those whose dominant attractor is
wrong ($0.435$ vs.\ $0.333$; cell-level $p = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$).
Aggregating to the task level, a cluster bootstrap over the $31$ three-option
HiddenBench tasks gives a $V$ difference of $0.102$ ($95\,\%$ CI
$[0.03,\ 0.18]$, $p = 0.005$), so the effect survives task clustering.
On GPQA ($M = 4$) the difference is negligible ($V = 0.464$ vs.\ $0.457$,
cell-level $p = 0.49$; task-level bootstrap $p = 0.81$).
Correct attractors therefore exhibit stronger basin selection on HiddenBench:
not only is the correct answer more likely to dominate when it does dominate,
but the initial-vote composition predicts that outcome more reliably.
Two caveats attach. First, plug-in $V$ (like plug-in $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$) is
biased upward in sparse tables; the bias-corrected $V$ shows the same ordering
(bias-corrected means $0.347$ for correct- vs.\ $0.226$ for wrong-dominant
cells). Second, we restrict the contrast to $M = 3$ cells and never compare
raw $V$ across benchmarks with different option counts.

\paragraph{Cross-motif: SR and bistability.}
Monostable cells have the highest mean SR slope (0.555), followed by
multistable (0.412) and stochastic (0.370); this ordering is consistent
across configurations.
At the task level ($n = 35$) the association between $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and
mean SR slope is strong and significant on GPQA ($\rho = -0.79$,
$p \approx 0$) but does not reach significance on HiddenBench
($\rho = -0.26$, $p = 0.13$).
This negative relationship has a natural interpretation: SR is the
within-basin amplification mechanism.
In cells where one attractor dominates (monostable), agents lock in early
and accumulate steep confidence ramps.
In multistable cells the system splits across attractors, different
agent subgroups lock into different options, so the SR signal is
diluted across multiple trajectories.
Bistability and self-reinforcement are therefore complementary at the
system level: strong SR $\to$ fast within-basin convergence;
high $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ $\to$ the system visits multiple basins across
repetitions.

\subsection{Dominance}
\label{sec:results:dominance}

Dominance is measured via the Herfindahl index $D$ over the
confidence-weighted influence graph: how concentrated is the total
conversion credit among agents?
$D = 0$ means influence is distributed equally; $D = 1$ means one agent
causes all opinion changes.
The surrogate null randomises the source identity within each neighbourhood,
testing whether concentration exceeds what the topology alone would produce.
The star topology's null is degenerate for 73--87\,\% of repetitions
(leaf conversions are forced onto the centre), so \emph{emergent excess}
($z$-score and flagged fraction) is only identified for the
fully-connected topology; raw $D$ is reported for both.

\paragraph{Accounting.}
Of 21\,000 repetitions, 17\,801 (84.8\,\%) involve at least one vote
change and have a well-defined $D$.
The 3\,199 excluded reps (15.2\,\%) converged unanimously from round 0
or had no opinion changes; they carry no dominance information.
Of the valid fc repetitions, 72\,\% (GPQA) and 83\,\% (HiddenBench) have
a non-degenerate null and contribute to the $z$ analysis.

\paragraph{Topology dominates raw D.}
\autoref{tab:dom:overview} and \autoref{fig:dom:D_dist} show the
$D$ distributions.
Star topology produces 3.1--4.1$\times$ higher $D$ than fc across all
(dataset, $W$) combinations (paired Wilcoxon, all $p < 10^{-10}$).
This is partly mechanical: the star's centre is the sole eligible
conversion source for every leaf, so its influence share is structurally
inflated.
The magnitude difference is therefore expected and should not be
over-interpreted as a behavioural finding.

\paragraph{Memory window effect.}
The W effect on $D$ is significant for HiddenBench only:
paired Wilcoxon (W=1 vs W=5) gives $p = 0.003$ for fc and
$p = 0.001$ for star ($\Delta D \approx +0.02$--$0.03$).
On GPQA both topologies are flat ($p > 0.19$).
The pattern is an entrenchment effect: more message history gives an
emerging leader more prior material to reference, slightly strengthening
its position. But the effect size is modest ($\Delta D < 0.04$) and
task-specific.

\paragraph{Hub concentration and correctness.}
In fc the hub (highest-influence agent) holds on average 42--45\,\% of total
influence share (GPQA $0.42$, HiddenBench $0.45$); in star it holds 76--79\,\%.
The hub's vote coincides with the final group consensus in virtually every
repetition (hub\_is\_consensus $\approx 1.0$ across all configs). This is close
to definitional, since the hub is the agent others converted \emph{toward}, and
in a run ending in unanimity that is nearly by construction the agent whose vote
the group adopted; we note it only to motivate the more informative quantities
below, hub correctness and capitulation, which are not fixed by the definition.

Because the hub vote coincides with the group's, ``hub correctness'' here is
just the group's accuracy under another name; we therefore read it only as
such and defer the non-tautological question, whether the \emph{recurring}
dominant hub is better informed, to the stability analysis below.
On GPQA the group's final accuracy exceeds the $1/M$ chance baseline
(task-level, one-sided $p = 0.003$); on HiddenBench it is at or below the
$1/3$ baseline (31.7\,\%, not significant), so the dominant agent on
HiddenBench is not systematically the better-informed one.

\paragraph{Hub identity stability.}
Hub identity is moderately unstable across repetitions of the same task
(normalised HHI over hub identities: 0.02--0.16; dominant agent wins
33--49\,\% of reps).
The hub role is not permanently attached to a specific agent but shifts
across runs.
Yet the dominant hub, \emph{when} it recurs, is more likely to hold the
correct answer on GPQA (40.5\,\% vs 25\,\% baseline; task-level
$p \approx 0.01$, surviving correction) and at chance on HiddenBench.
This suggests that on GPQA a question-specific ``expertise signal''
partially determines which agent wins the hub role, while on HiddenBench
hub assignment is effectively random with respect to correctness.

\paragraph{Dominance and accuracy.}
We test this at the task level ($n = 35$), pairing each task's accuracy
against its mean $D$.
On \textbf{HiddenBench/fc} higher $D$ predicts higher accuracy
(task-level Spearman $\rho = +0.44$, $p = 0.008$; correct runs have
$D = 0.219$ vs $0.150$ for incorrect, task-paired $d = +0.059$,
$p = 1 \times 10^{-4}$; survives Benjamini--Hochberg correction).
On \textbf{GPQA/fc} the same direction is only marginal ($d = +0.025$,
$p = 0.044$) and does not survive correction across the four cells
($q = 0.06$). The rep-level GPQA/star association reported in earlier drafts
does \emph{not} survive task aggregation ($d = +0.003$, $p = 0.81$).
Crucially, \textbf{HiddenBench/star runs the other way}: higher $D$ predicts
\emph{lower} accuracy ($d = -0.051$, $p = 0.010$, survives correction).
Dominance is therefore not uniformly benign: on HiddenBench a decisive leader
helps when every agent sees every other (fc) but hurts when influence is
forced through a single hub (star).

The mechanism differs by dataset and topology.
On GPQA the hub is somewhat more likely to be correct, so a high-$D$ rep is
one where a more-likely-correct agent accumulates most conversion credit.
On HiddenBench/fc the hub is not more correct, yet high-$D$ reps still have
higher accuracy, suggesting that decisive leadership per se (avoiding diffuse
indecision among equally uninformed agents) can be productive; but on
HiddenBench/star, where a single hub broadcasts to isolated spokes, that same
concentration entrenches the shared initial bias and lowers accuracy.

\paragraph{Conversion structure.}
$D$ correlates strongly with the fraction of total conversion
weight attributed to the single most influential source agent
(task-level Spearman $\rho = 0.85$ on GPQA, $0.84$ on HiddenBench).
$D$ measures exactly conversion concentration, as intended.
Notably, $D$ is \emph{negatively} correlated with total conversion
volume ($\rho = -0.34$ to $-0.56$): high-$D$ repetitions involve fewer
total opinion changes, but those that occur are concentrated on one
agent.
The dominant pattern is a single persuasive agent triggering a small
cascade, not a high-activity debate converging by attrition.

\paragraph{Independence from bistability.}
$D$ is uncorrelated with $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in both datasets
($\rho = -0.07$ to $+0.01$, all $p > 0.3$) and does not differ
significantly across bistability labels (monostable/multistable/stochastic).
Dominance and bistability operate on orthogonal dimensions: one describes
\emph{who} drives opinion change within a run; the other describes
\emph{how many attractors} the system visits across runs.

\subsection{High-Repetition Distribution Analysis}
\label{sec:results:highrep}

The preceding sections characterise system behaviour at the task-population level,
aggregating over 35 tasks per dataset at $R = 50$ repetitions each.
This section takes a complementary, within-task perspective: three individual GPQA
questions (q84, q125, q144) were each run for $R = 1{,}000$ repetitions under the
fixed configuration $W = 2$, fully-connected topology, $N = 4$ agents.
The aim is to characterise the distribution of outcomes across many runs of the same
task and to identify which properties of the initial vote composition predict
accuracy and convergence speed.
The three tasks were selected on the basis of mid-range accuracy and
high initial-vote entropy, ensuring genuinely uncertain outcomes
and substantial run-to-run variability.

For each run the following quantities are extracted.
\emph{Initial-state features} (causal, observable before any debate):
initial vote entropy $H_0$ (Shannon entropy in bits; with $N = 4$ agents and $M = 4$
options $H_0$ takes five discrete values $\{0, 0.81, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0\}$
corresponding to the vote patterns $(4,0,0,0)$ through $(1,1,1,1)$); initial
plurality size (count of agents on the most popular option; a deterministic function
of $H_0$); and two binary indicators for whether the ground-truth option is present
in any initial vote and whether it holds the initial plurality.
\emph{Trajectory signatures} (post-hoc): total vote flips (count of individual
opinion changes across all rounds) and confidence change (mean final confidence
minus mean initial confidence).

\paragraph{Ground-truth presence in the initial vote distribution.}
The dominant finding is stark: whether the ground-truth option appears at all in
the initial vote distribution is by far the strongest predictor of final accuracy,
consistent across all three tasks
(\autoref{fig:highrep:init_votes_split}, \autoref{tab:highrep:init_votes}).

When the correct answer is absent from all initial votes, the system almost never

q144 respectively ($\chi^2 > 120$, $p < 10^{-27}$ in all cases).
The rare correct answers in the absent condition are consistent with stochastic
noise rather than genuine recovery.
Within the present condition, accuracy is roughly $2\times$ higher when the correct
option also holds the initial plurality (e.g.\ $0.620$ vs $0.257$ for q84).
These findings suggest that the system operates primarily as a vote amplifier:
debate selectively strengthens options already represented in the initial
distribution and overwhelmingly reinforces whichever option holds the initial
plurality.
The capacity to surface a correct answer that no agent endorsed at the outset is
negligible under the present design.

\paragraph{Initial vote entropy and efficiency.}
\autoref{fig:highrep:h0} shows accuracy and mean rounds to consensus as a

Higher $H_0$ is associated with longer debates in all three tasks.
The effect on accuracy is not monotone: it is largely a confound of $H_0$ with the
probability that the ground truth is present in the initial votes.
At $H_0 = 0$ all agents agree on one option, which is the ground truth only by
chance; at $H_0 = 2$ every option gets one vote so the ground truth is always
present but as a minority.
Within the GT-present subset, $H_0$ negatively predicts accuracy ($r = -0.23$ for
q84, $r = -0.29$ for q144): a more spread-out start places the correct option in a
weaker competitive position.
$H_0$ therefore encodes a tradeoff: higher entropy increases the probability of the
correct option being present but decreases its initial plurality and thus its chance
of winning.

\autoref{fig:highrep:rounds} shows the relationship between rounds to consensus

task-specific structure rather than a general deliberation benefit.
Vote flips and rounds are tightly coupled ($r = 0.83$--$0.92$, all $p \approx 0$):
turbulent trajectories with many opinion changes are the same runs that fail to
reach fast consensus.
Confidence increases monotonically across rounds regardless of outcome (mean gain
$\approx 1.6$--$1.9$ points on the 1--10 scale) and carries at most a negligible
predictive signal for final correctness ($|r| < 0.09$ across all tasks and all
confidence features; only one of the three tasks reaches nominal significance).

almost perfectly across all three tasks (\autoref{fig:app:highrep:distributions}
in Appendix~\ref{app:highrep}), confirming that confidence gain is a property of
the debate process itself rather than a signal of outcome quality.
Full feature-by-outcome correlations are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:highrep}.

\subsection{A Causal Test: Seeded Initial Conditions}
\label{sec:results:seeded}

The preceding analysis is observational: whether the ground truth is present at
$t=0$ co-varies with task difficulty, agent confidence, and initial entropy, so its
apparent effect on accuracy could be confounded.
To isolate the causal contribution of the initial vote composition, we ran a
controlled intervention on $34$ GPQA tasks (the same $W=2$,
fully-connected, $N=4$ configuration, $R=50$ repetitions each).
These are GPQA Tier-3 tasks from the same task population as the main study (the
three high-repetition tasks q84, q125, and q144 are among them); the seeded arm
requires a response pool rich enough to construct both conditions, and one of the
$35$ tasks could not be paired across both arms and is excluded, leaving $34$.
For each task we first collected a pool of genuine, independent single-agent
responses, then constructed the initial four-agent vote profile of each run by
sampling from that pool under one of two constraints:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{init\_0} (ground truth absent): exactly zero of the four agents
        begin on the correct option;
  \item \textbf{init\_1} (ground truth seeded): exactly one of the four agents
        begins on the correct option, i.e.\ the ground truth is present but as a
        $1/4$ minority.
\end{itemize}
Only the \emph{composition} of the starting votes is manipulated; every initial
vote is a real agent response, and the debate that follows is unmodified.
The contrast between the two conditions therefore measures the causal effect of
placing the ground truth into the initial distribution, and the init\_0 arm
separately measures the system's capacity to \emph{generate} the correct answer de
novo when no agent starts with it.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig4_seeded_causal.png}
  \caption[Causal effect of seeding the ground truth]{Causal effect of seeding the ground truth into the initial vote
           distribution.
           (a) Per-task accuracy for all $34$ tasks, paired: grey = init\_0
           (ground truth absent), blue = init\_1 (ground truth seeded as a $1/4$
           minority).
           (b) Pooled accuracy with Wilson 95\,\% CI.
           $R=50$ per task per condition, $W=2$, fully-connected.}
  \label{fig:highrep:seeded_causal}
\end{figure}

The effect is large and unambiguous (\autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_causal}).
Pooled over $1{,}700$ runs per condition, seeding a single correct agent raises
accuracy from $1.8\,\%$ (init\_0) to $23.7\,\%$ (init\_1), a roughly $13\times$
increase (pooled two-proportion $\chi^2 = 364$; we treat the task-paired test
below as the primary one).
The lift holds in \emph{every} task: init\_1 exceeds init\_0 in all $34$ of $34$
tasks (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank $p = 3.6\times10^{-7}$, median per-task lift
$0.18$).
This confirms causally what the $R=1{,}000$ analysis showed observationally: the
presence of the correct option in the opening vote profile is the single largest
determinant of whether the system arrives at it.
At the same time, init\_0 accuracy is not exactly zero.
The correct answer was recovered de novo in $15$ of $34$ tasks
($31$ runs in total), so the debate does possess a weak generative channel rather
than acting as a pure amplifier.

\paragraph{De-novo recovery is bounded by task difficulty.}
That generative channel is not a general reasoning capacity: it fires only on tasks
that are already easy, and vanishes on the hard ones.
We measure difficulty independently of the debate by the task's single-agent
accuracy $\mathrm{acc}_1 = n_\text{correct}/200$, the correct fraction of the fixed
pool of $200$ independent single-agent responses from which the initial votes are
drawn (identical across both conditions).
This index is a faithful measure of intrinsic difficulty rather than an artifact of
the seeding procedure: for the three $R=1{,}000$ tasks it matches the naturally
observed $t=0$ per-agent accuracy almost exactly (q84 $0.30$ vs $0.28$, q125 $0.28$
vs $0.26$, q144 $0.39$ vs $0.40$).
De-novo recovery rises monotonically with $\mathrm{acc}_1$ across the $34$ tasks
(Spearman $\rho = 0.51$, $p = 2.3\times10^{-3}$;
\autoref{fig:highrep:recovery_difficulty}), and the dependence is stark at the
hard end: the hardest quartile of tasks (single-agent accuracy $0.5$--$12.5\,\%$)
recovers the ground truth in \emph{zero} of its $400$ init\_0 runs.
Concretely, on the biased, low-accuracy tasks, exactly those where independent
reasoning would be most valuable, the system never manufactures the correct answer
that no agent initially held; it only rediscovers answers that are intrinsically
defensible to begin with.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig7_recovery_difficulty.png}
  \caption[De-novo recovery rate versus task difficulty]{Init\_0 de-novo recovery rate against task difficulty, measured as
           single-agent accuracy ($n_\text{correct}/200$ from the response pool;
           higher $=$ easier), one point per task (marker size $\propto$ number of
           recovered runs).
           Red diamonds: quartile means.
           The hardest quartile (shaded) recovers the ground truth in $0$ of $400$
           runs; recovery only occurs on easier tasks.
           Spearman $\rho = 0.51$, $p = 2.3\times10^{-3}$.}
  \label{fig:highrep:recovery_difficulty}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Convergence dynamics: convert-all or capitulate-all.}
Both conditions reach fully unanimous final states in $100\,\%$ of runs, so the
seeded correct agent has a strictly binary fate: it either converts the entire group
or capitulates completely, no run ends with the ground truth surviving as a
minority (\autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_dynamics}a).
Across the $1{,}700$ init\_1 runs, the seed converts the group in $23.7\,\%$ of runs
and capitulates in the remaining $76.3\,\%$.
When the minority does win, it does so early: the correct option climbs to a
plurality within the first one to two debate rounds
(\autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_dynamics}b), and de-novo recoveries in init\_0
appear equally early (mean first-appearance round $1.45$).
Persuasion in this system is front-loaded, the opening exchange, not protracted
deliberation, decides the outcome, echoing the earlier finding that longer debates
do not produce better answers.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig5_seeded_dynamics.png}
  \caption[Convergence dynamics under seeding]{Convergence dynamics under seeding.
           (a) Fate of the single correct agent in init\_1: every run ends
           unanimous, so the seed either converts the group (blue) or capitulates
           (grey).
           (b) Round at which the correct answer takes hold, when it reaches a
           plurality in won init\_1 runs (blue) and when it first re-appears in
           recovered init\_0 runs (red).}
  \label{fig:highrep:seeded_dynamics}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What lets the minority win, and does the intervention match observation?}
Within init\_1, the correct agent's odds depend on how its three opponents are
arranged (\autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_mechanism}a).
A unified wrong bloc (all three opponents on one option, $3$-$0$-$0$) holds the
ground truth to a $19.5\,\%$ win rate, whereas a fully split opposition ($1$-$1$-$1$)
lets it win $32.3\,\%$ of the time (pooled $\chi^2 = 7.9$, $p = 4.9\times10^{-3}$).
Aggregated to the task level this difference is only suggestive: across the $31$
tasks that contain both bloc types the paired per-task difference is not significant
(Wilcoxon $p = 0.053$; cluster-bootstrap $95\,\%$ CI for the win-rate gap
$[-0.00,\ +0.15]$, marginally including zero).
A task-clustered logistic model (GEE with an exchangeable working correlation,
grouped by task) of the win outcome on standardised predictors confirms three
contributions, though only one is strong once clustering is respected: a divided
opposition helps ($\beta = -0.16$ per unit of top-bloc size, $p = 9.5\times10^{-4}$),
a starting confidence advantage for the correct agent helps ($\beta = +0.16$,
$p = 0.03$), and the task's intrinsic defensibility (correct fraction of the pool)
also helps ($\beta = +0.33$, $p = 0.03$).
The small pseudo-$R^2$ ($0.03$) indicates that these initial features shift the odds
but do not determine the outcome, leaving substantial room for the stochastic course
of the debate itself.
Finally, the intervention is broadly consistent with the observational data: for the
three $R=1{,}000$ tasks, seeded init\_1 accuracy has the same rank order as the
naturally-occurring ``exactly one correct minority vote'' subset
(\autoref{fig:highrep:seeded_mechanism}b; q144 $0.28$ seeded vs $0.365$ observed,
q84 $0.16$ vs $0.19$, q125 $0.12$ vs $0.25$).
Seeded accuracy runs somewhat below the observational value in all three tasks, so we
read this as directional external support rather than exact reproduction; the gap is
consistent with the seeded pool enforcing an exact $1/4$ minority rather than sampling
the natural minority configurations.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/highrep/fig6_seeded_mechanism.png}
  \caption[Minority conversion mechanism and external validity]{Mechanism of minority conversion and external validity.
           (a) P(ground truth wins) in init\_1 by the arrangement of the three

\end{figure}

Taken together, the seeded experiment upgrades the central claim of this section
from association to intervention: the multi-agent system functions predominantly as
a \emph{selective amplifier} of its initial vote distribution.
Seeding the correct answer as a lone minority multiplies accuracy roughly $13$-fold,
yet even then the correct agent is out-voted three times out of four, and the system
almost never manufactures a correct answer that no agent initially held.
The composition of the opening votes, not the length or intensity of the debate, is
the dominant lever on whether collective reasoning succeeds.
This intervention was run on GPQA only; the observational ground-truth-presence
result holds on both benchmarks (\autoref{sec:results:sr}), but the causal claim
itself is established here for GPQA, and its extension to HiddenBench is discussed as
a limitation in \autoref{sec:discussion:limitations}.

\subsection{Linguistic Dimensions}
\label{sec:results:linguistic}

The four motifs above are read from the discrete vote and the numeric
confidence.
This section examines the complementary textual channel: each public message is
scored on three ordinal $1$--$5$ dimensions, \emph{conviction} (how certain
the message sounds), \emph{anchoring} (whether the stated belief source is the
agent's own reasoning or deference to peers), and \emph{dialogic orientation}
(engagement with a named peer), as defined in
\autoref{sec:method:linguistic}.
Because the scorer is an instance of the same model family as the debate agents,
these scores are used only for \emph{relative} comparisons and never as an
absolute measure of argument quality; the associations below are reported at the
task-cell level (420 cells) with rank statistics throughout.

\paragraph{Level effects are weak; diversity is the signal.}
The mean level of each dimension carries little information about system
behaviour.
Across all 420 cells the Spearman correlations between a cell's mean dimension
score and its MAS metrics are uniformly small: for accuracy all three are
$|\rho| \leq 0.11$; the largest correlation of any mean score with any metric is
conviction with mean rounds at $\rho = -0.30$ ($p < 10^{-3}$).
Dataset differences in level exist but are modest, GPQA messages score
slightly higher than HiddenBench on all three dimensions (e.g.\ system-level
conviction $3.55$ vs.\ $3.30$, dialogic $3.01$ vs.\ $2.51$), and the
hub and leaf roles in the star topology are nearly indistinguishable
(hub vs.\ leaf conviction $3.44$ vs.\ $3.44$, $p = 0.99$; small but significant
differences on anchoring and dialogic, both $|\Delta| < 0.05$).
The informative quantity is not how the agents talk on average but how
\emph{differently} they talk from one another within a run.

\paragraph{Stylistic diversity predicts faster convergence.}
For each run we compute the between-agent standard deviation of each dimension
, a measure of within-run stylistic diversity, and correlate it with
outcomes at the cell level (\autoref{tab:ling:diversity}).
All three diversity measures are strongly and negatively associated with mean
rounds to consensus: conviction diversity $\rho = -0.55$, anchoring diversity
$\rho = -0.54$, dialogic diversity $\rho = -0.51$ (all $p < 10^{-10}$).
Runs in which agents adopt visibly different argumentative styles resolve
\emph{faster} than runs in which they sound alike.
The effect holds within each dataset separately (GPQA: anchoring-diversity
$\rho = -0.41$, dialogic $\rho = -0.32$; HiddenBench: dialogic $\rho = -0.38$,
anchoring $\rho = -0.35$; all $p < 10^{-3}$), so it is not an artefact of pooling
two benchmarks with different base rates.
Diversity is also weakly associated with lower attractor multiplicity
(conviction-diversity vs.\ $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$: $\rho = -0.19$, $p = 10^{-4}$),
consistent with the interpretation below.

\paragraph{Interpretation and its limits.}
The efficiency association has a natural reading that connects to the
self-reinforcement finding.
When agents argue in a uniform style, similar conviction, similar stance
toward peers, there is little genuine friction to resolve, and the group locks
into whichever position it already leans toward: this is within-basin
self-reinforcement, which produces steep confidence ramps and fast, but not
necessarily correct, convergence (\autoref{sec:results:sr}).
Stylistically heterogeneous groups instead sustain genuine back-and-forth, which
lengthens the debate.
The accuracy side of this story does not generalise: stylistic diversity is
mildly \emph{beneficial} on GPQA (anchoring diversity $\rho = +0.18$) but mildly
\emph{harmful} on HiddenBench (conviction diversity $\rho = -0.24$), so diversity
buys deliberation time without a consistent accuracy payoff, the same
dataset-dependence seen for every other motif.
Two caveats bound these results.
First, the scorer shares a model family with the agents, so absolute levels may
be biased and only the relative, rank-based associations should be trusted.
Second, these are cell-level correlations, not interventions: we did not
manipulate stylistic diversity directly, so the direction of causation between
style and convergence speed is not established here.
We therefore report stylistic diversity as a further, weaker diagnostic
correlate of convergence dynamics, not as an independent design lever.

\subsection{Synthesis: One Mechanism, Four Signatures}
\label{sec:results:synthesis}

The preceding sections measured four motifs, a high-repetition distribution, and
a linguistic channel independently.
Read together, they do not describe four separate phenomena but four views of a
single underlying mechanism.
This subsection states that mechanism explicitly, shows how each measurement is a
signature of it, and separates what the data establish from what they do not.

\paragraph{The convergent finding.}
Across every analysis in this chapter, the property that most strongly governs
the group's final answer is the composition of the votes \emph{before any debate
takes place}.
The high-repetition experiment shows this observationally: whether the
ground-truth option is present at $t = 0$ moves per-task accuracy from near zero
(0.4--6.9\,\%) to 36--58\,\%, and the seeded-initialisation experiment shows it
causally: placing a single correct agent into an otherwise-wrong opening profile
raises pooled accuracy roughly thirteen-fold (1.8\,\% to 23.7\,\%), in all 34
tasks tested (Sections~\ref{sec:results:highrep} and~\ref{sec:results:seeded}).
The self-reinforcement analysis supplies the mechanism by which the opening
profile is converted into the outcome: SR is universal
($p_{\mathrm{SR}} \in [0.74, 0.88]$ in every cell), it is the convergence engine
(task-level $\rho(\bar\beta, \text{rounds}) \in [-0.91, -0.77]$ everywhere), and
its accuracy effect is a pure amplification, positive when the correct option
is already present ($r_{pb} = +0.20$ GPQA / $+0.18$ HB) and negative when it is
absent ($-0.11$ / $-0.04$), cancelling to near zero in the margin.
In short, the debate reliably \emph{amplifies} whichever position the opening
distribution favours; it only rarely \emph{generates} a position no agent
initially held (de-novo recovery in 15 of 34 seeded tasks, but at just 1.8\,\%
of runs).
We state this as the central empirical claim of the thesis: within the system we
tested, deliberation functions predominantly as a selective amplifier of its
initial vote distribution rather than as a mechanism that reasons a new answer
into existence.

\paragraph{How each motif is a signature of the same mechanism.}
The remaining three motifs describe \emph{how} the amplification plays out, not a
separate process.
\emph{Bistability} is the cross-run face of amplification: because the outcome is
selected by the (stochastic) opening profile, repeated runs of the same task land
in different basins, so multi-attractor coexistence is common
($N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5$ in 78.6\,\% of cells) and is a stable property of the
task rather than of the configuration (cross-config $\rho = 0.78$--$0.91$).
The negative coupling between bistability and self-reinforcement
(task-level $\rho(N_{\mathrm{eff}}, \bar\beta) = -0.79$ on GPQA) is exactly what the
amplifier picture predicts: strong within-basin amplification means fast lock-in
to one attractor per run, whereas high attractor multiplicity dilutes the SR
signal across competing basins.
\emph{Dominance} is the within-run face: amplification is typically driven by a
small, concentrated cascade ($D$ correlates $\approx 0.85$ with the top source's
share and \emph{negatively} with total conversion volume), i.e.\ one agent tips a
near-balanced start rather than the group converging by diffuse attrition.
\emph{Limit cycles} are the boundary case in which amplification fails to
terminate: when the opening profile is symmetric between two defensible options
and the star hub sits in the minority, each flip reconstructs the opposing
majority and the group never locks in, running to the hard cap.
The linguistic channel adds one more consistent correlate: stylistically uniform
groups amplify faster (diversity vs.\ rounds $\rho \approx -0.5$), the textual
shadow of within-basin self-reinforcement.

\paragraph{The one axis that matters, and the one that does not.}
Two negative results sharpen the picture.
First, the \emph{structural} design axes we varied, communication topology and
memory window, do not change \emph{whether} the system reaches the correct
answer: topology has no accuracy effect at the task level (the confidence
interval for the fc--star gap contains zero on both benchmarks), the memory
window has none that survives multiple-comparison correction (a single
HiddenBench/fc cell is nominally significant, $q = 0.08$), and bistability is
invariant to both.
Their influence is confined to the \emph{dynamics}: star topology is slower,
is the sole source of genuine limit cycles, and mechanically concentrates
influence; larger memory windows speed HiddenBench convergence slightly.
Second, the system's own internal signals are not indicators of correctness:
confidence rises by essentially the same amount on correct and incorrect runs
($+1.66$ vs.\ $+1.64$ on GPQA), and on HiddenBench early unanimity is
anti-correlated with accuracy (round-2 stops reach only 6.4\,\%).
What \emph{does} move accuracy, the opening vote composition, is set before
any of these knobs act.

\paragraph{Scope of the claim.}
These statements are bounded by what we varied and what we held fixed.
We manipulated topology, memory window, task difficulty, benchmark, and (in the
seeded experiment) the opening vote composition; we did \emph{not} vary the base
model, the number of agents ($N = 4$), agent heterogeneity, or the cooperative
framing.
The amplifier characterisation is therefore established \emph{for a homogeneous,
cooperative, four-agent debate on one model family across two benchmarks}, with
one axis (initial composition) tested causally and the rest observationally.
Whether the same mechanism dominates for heterogeneous models, larger groups, or
adversarial protocols is an open question we return to in
\autoref{sec:discussion}.
The value of the motif lens, developed in the next chapter, is that each of these
signatures is separately measurable from ordinary interaction logs, so a
practitioner can check which regime a given system is in rather than having to
assume it.
