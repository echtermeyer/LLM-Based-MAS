This chapter gives an overview of related literature of this thesis. It begins 
with \autoref{sec:related:mad} and \autoref{sec:related:dynamics} that survey what
multi-agent debate does and how the interaction dynamics inside it have been
studied. \autoref{sec:related:lenses} continues by weighing the available ways of analysing such
systems against one another and \autoref{sec:related:gap} finishes by 
stating the research gap this thesis addresses.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Debate: Design and Critique}
\label{sec:related:mad}

The multi-agent debate (MAD) paradigm was introduced by
\citet{du2024improving}, who let several instances of a language model
independently propose answers and then critique and revise them over multiple
rounds before committing to a collective response. This ``society of minds'' setup
was reported to improve factual accuracy and mathematical reasoning while
reducing hallucination, treating the underlying model as a black box. The core
intuition was that partially independent agents make partially independent errors
which deliberation can correct. Since then it has motivated a broad family
of systems, from confidence-weighted round tables across heterogeneous models
\citep{chen2024reconcile} to evaluation panels that judge text more reliably
than a single evaluator \citep{chan2024chateval}.

Two design axes are common throughout and are the ones we vary too.
Communication topology fixes who reads whom. Hereby, sparse structures can match
fully-connected debate at substantially lower token cost
\citep{li2024sparsetopology}, and in scale-free networks, hub nodes biased toward
a correct or wrong answer shift accuracy substantially \citep{liu2025problemsolving}. Memory
fixes how much interaction history each agent retains. The two were recently
crossed in a single setup by \citet{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory}, who find
no main effect of topology on coordination success but a strong interaction with
memory. Longer memory slows convergence in decentralised networks and accelerates
it in centralised networks, so the same parameter pushes the system in opposite
directions depending on the structure of the network. Faster settling in the centralised
case means locking into a fragmented plateau rather than reaching
agreement. Beyond these two, the decision protocol itself matters, with voting
favouring reasoning tasks and consensus favouring knowledge tasks, and adding
agents helping where adding rounds can hurt \citep{kaesberg2025voting}.

Against this largely optimistic literature, also a more critical literature has emerged.
\citet{liu2025stopovervaluing} find that MAD frequently fails to outperform
chain-of-thought \citep{wei2022chainofthought} or self-consistency
\citep{wang2023selfconsistency} despite far greater compute, and report that
model heterogeneity improves MAD frameworks.
Furthermore, \citet{zhang2025debateorvote} show that majority voting alone accounts for most
of the attributed gains, and prove that under their setup debate does not
improve expected correctness on its own, since agents' beliefs change from round to
round but on average do not move toward the correct answer. 
\citet{estornell2024multillm} supply the mechanism. When agents are
similar in capability or in the responses they produce, debate dynamics become
static and the procedure converges to the majority opinion, so that where the
majority reflects a misconception shared through common training data, debate
converges to the misconception.

Two 2026 results push this further and are the closest published statements of
what we measure. \citet{oh2026entrenchment} decompose the failure into a biased
static initial belief and homogenised dynamics that amplify the majority view
regardless of correctness. \citet{bertalanic2026costofconsensus} show that
unguided homogeneous debate matched or underperformed \emph{isolated
self-correction} at several times the token cost, and that a control injecting
rationales from unrelated problems performed no worse than genuine peer
exchange. They name the resulting loss an \emph{oracle gap}, the distance
between the accuracy achievable from the team's own generation pool and the
accuracy the vote actually returns, a quantity we measure directly in
\autoref{sec:results:perf}.

Almost all of this work, whether it supports or questions debate, judges it by
its \emph{final outcome}. They largely ignore the deliberative process behind that
outcome, and whether two systems with the same accuracy reach it through
different dynamics.


\subsection{Interaction Dynamics in LLM Collectives}
\label{sec:related:dynamics}

Another area of work looks inside the debate at how positions evolve
and influence one another. Each of our four motifs corresponds to a debate
phenomenon that this literature documents to a very different degree, and we take
them in turn, before turning to the opening vote distribution, which is not
itself a motif but the input on which they all act.

\paragraph{Rising confidence.}
\citet{prasad2025certain} stage repeated adversarial debates and find pervasive
miscalibration. Models begin overconfident, grow \emph{more} confident that they
are winning as the exchange proceeds even without new supporting evidence, and
frequently both claim victory at once, which is a logically impossible. 
These are zero-sum debates, where winning is not determined by correctness.
\citet{bertalanic2026costofconsensus} show that an agent's confidence before
communication barely predicts whether it is right, and that peer exchange even
further weakens that link. Confidence that keeps rising regardless of correctness
is a typical sign of positive feedback, which motivates a dynamical treatment.

\paragraph{Conformity and influence concentration.}
\citet{wynn2025talk} show that debate can \emph{decrease} accuracy over time
even when stronger models outnumber weaker ones, as agents abandon correct
answers rather than challenge flawed arguments. Sycophancy
\citep{sharma2023sycophancy} and social conformity are among the contributing
factors they examine. \citet{cho2025herd} find the gap
between an agent's own confidence and its perceived confidence in peers governs
how likely it is to conform, and \citet{mehdizadeh2025peerpressure} characterise
the dose-response as a sigmoid with a model-specific threshold.
\citet{cau2026agreementdrift} identify an aggregate directional asymmetry they
call \emph{agreement drift}, which survives in balanced populations and so cannot
be reduced to a majority effect. When influence concentrates in a few agents, a
leader-follower structure emerges \citep{chen2023consensus}. The outcome then
depends heavily on how competent the central agent is
\citep{conformitydynamics2026}, a dependency our star topology is designed to
expose.

\paragraph{Premature lock-in.}
\citet{chuang2025debate} report that role-playing LLM groups converge more
strongly than human groups do, indicating a systematic bias toward
premature agreement. Purely cooperative framings furhter sharpen this, since with no dissenting
pressure a group can collapse into a mutually reinforcing echo chamber. A
structurally sharper version is captured by HiddenBench
\citep{li2026hiddenbench}, which adapts the hidden-profile paradigm from social
psychology \citep{stasser1985pooling, schulzhardt2012synergy}. When the evidence
needed for the correct answer is distributed across agents, collective accuracy
collapses far below what a single fully-informed agent achieves, because agents
lock onto shared information and fail to pool their private knowledge. We adopt
HiddenBench as our second benchmark for exactly this reason.

\paragraph{Oscillation and non-termination.}
Sustained oscillation is almost absent from this literature. A debate that
cycles through the same positions without converging is a limit cycle
\citep{strogatz2015nonlinear}, yet debate studies typically fix the number of
rounds and count any run that fails to settle as ``no consensus'', so a cycle
is never distinguished from slow convergence. This gap is itself informative, as we
find genuine limit cycles to be rare outside the star topology
(\autoref{sec:results:lc}).

\paragraph{The opening distribution.}
That round-zero answers govern the outcome is stated in several forms.
\citet{hu2025stabilitydetection} make it a formal premise, proving that debate
improves on majority vote under the assumption that at least one
initial response is generated under the correct concept.
\citet{zhu2026demystifying} report that initial answer diversity correlates
significantly with final accuracy and propose a diversity-aware initialisation
to raise the chance that a correct hypothesis is present at the start.
Empirically, \citet{wu2025reallydebate} find that an agent starting inside a
correct majority is almost perfectly stable while one facing an incorrect
majority recovers in only a minority of cases. In every case the opening distribution is observed as
it arises rather than constructed, leaving its effect confounded with task
difficulty and agent confidence, which is what \autoref{sec:results:evid} is
designed to separate.

\medskip
\noindent The vocabulary for describing these phenomena was introduced in
\autoref{sec:bg:motifs}. What remains open is whether it is more than an analogy
when applied to LLM populations. Recent work suggests it is.
\citet{ashery2025conventions} show that populations of LLM agents spontaneously
reach consensus on an arbitrary shared choice, that strong collective biases can
arise even when no individual agent has one, and that a committed minority can
overturn the majority position. The last effect mirrors the critical-mass threshold
known from human groups \citep{centola2018tipping}. \citet{okawa2026biasedconsensus}
adds a mathematical account, adapting a model from physics to debate. It predicts
that a collective norm emerges once conformity passes a threshold, although at the
small group sizes typical of debate this threshold is smoothed into a gradual
transition.


\subsection{Ways of Analysing Multi-Agent Systems}
\label{sec:related:lenses}

Before stating the gap, it is worth making explicit \emph{why} a
complex-systems treatment is the appropriate instrument, by situating it against
the alternatives. Each approach answers a different question and has different
costs.

\paragraph{Outcome evaluation.}
The dominant approach scores a system by final task performance
\citep{du2024improving, liu2025stopovervaluing, zhang2025debateorvote}. It is
objective, comparable across systems, and measures what practitioners care
about. But an outcome is only a single number summarising a whole trajectory. Two
systems with the same accuracy look identical, even if one corrected its errors
and the other slid into a shared mistake. Thus, it only says \emph{whether} a configuration
worked, not \emph{why}.

\paragraph{Model internals and transcript reading.}
Opening the models is the wrong level for our question. Consensus, oscillation,
and influence concentration are properties of the \emph{group} trajectory, not of
any single forward pass \citep{miehling2025agentic}, and the internals of
API-served models are inaccessible anyway. Reading transcripts is more
informative, and we use it informally to examine flagged limit-cycle cases
(\autoref{sec:results:lc}). However, on its own it is subjective, does not scale
to tens of thousands of repetitions, and cannot be tested against a null.

\paragraph{Influence attribution.}
Another approach measures how strongly agents influence one another. This is done
either with transfer entropy, which tracks how much one agent's messages
predict another's \citep{infodynamics2026}, or by removing agents and observing
how the outcome changes \citep{agentsthatmatter2026, cui2025introspecloo}. Both
give stronger estimates of influence than the adoption-based signature we use,
but they require access to the model or repeated re-runs. We return to this
trade-off in \autoref{sec:conclusion:future}.

\paragraph{Learned diagnostics from logs.}
A very recent approach trains a predictor on features taken from model outputs
or debate logs. It is used either to decide whether a question needs debate at all
\citep{fan2026imad} or to decide when a majority vote should be overturned
\citep{minoritysentinel2026}. This shows that ordinary outputs and logs contain enough signal
to support useful decisions. However, such predictors do not explain what is
happening. They learn that certain feature patterns precede failure, but do not
say which dynamical regime the system is in, and they need labelled outcomes for
training.

\paragraph{The dynamical approach.}
Our approach treats the debate as a dynamical system and measures
recurring patterns in its state trajectory \citep{strogatz2015nonlinear,
driscoll2024dynamical}. Its advantages address the gaps above. It operates at the
group level, scales to the full dataset, needs only interaction logs and no
labelled outcomes, and gives each motif a falsifiable signature that can be tested
against a principled null. However, it also has disadvantages. For instance,
transferring motif concepts from physical and biological systems to an LLM
collective is a modelling choice, not a law. The detection thresholds are partly
conventional, and a four-agent group is small for some of these measures. Without
intervention, the detectors also identify \emph{signatures} and
\emph{associations} rather than certified attractors or causes. We adopt this
approach because it is the only one that can name and measure the failure modes
documented above while remaining applicable to black-box models. We keep its
limitations in mind when interpreting every result (\autoref{sec:discussion:limitations}).


\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{sec:related:gap}

The literature above has described the problem but offers no suitable way to
measure it. Outcome-based evaluation, whether it supports or questions debate,
only measures where a deliberation ends and ignores how it got there. The
dynamics literature does look at the trajectory, but documents one failure mode
at a time (overconfidence, herding, premature consensus) and explains each
through a shared cause such as sycophancy, not through the motifs they resemble.
None of these findings is tested against a null model that shows what the same
statistic looks like by chance, so a detection rate alone could simply reflect
the small, discrete state space.

More importantly, where complex-systems concepts have been applied to LLM
collectives, the tasks were chosen to have no correct answer.
\citet{ashery2025conventions} study arbitrary choices where any shared label is
equally good. \citet{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory} check that the model itself
prefers none of their symbols, so that correctness does not confound the
dynamics. \citet{cau2026agreementdrift} choose a non-empirical proposition for
the same reason. This makes those studies clean. If the goal is to understand how
a group settles on a shared choice, having no better choice available is exactly
right. But it leaves open the question a practitioner faces, which is not whether
the group locks in but whether it locks in on the right answer.

This thesis takes up that question. It aims to detect self-organization motifs
empirically in \emph{truth-valued} deliberation, following the three research
questions of \autoref{sec:intro:rqs}. For \emph{measurement}, we define detectors
for self-reinforcement, multistability, dominance, and limit cycles. Each is
grounded in the literature and has its own null model. We run each configuration
many times, which makes it possible to compute attractor statistics across
repetitions. Prior work repeats each configuration but studies an opinion scale rather than
truth-valued tasks \citep{cau2026agreementdrift}, or does not report the
distribution of end states across runs \citep{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory}, so
it does not measure multistability. For \emph{characterisation},
we apply the detectors to tasks with a correct answer, so that every dynamical
quantity can be split by correctness, across two different information regimes.
For \emph{causation}, we set the composition of the opening votes ourselves
instead of observing it, which separates its effect from task difficulty and
agent confidence. Throughout, we treat the \emph{absence} of a motif as a
finding, not a failed measurement.
