\subsection{Multi-Agent Debate: Design and Failure Modes}
\label{sec:related:mad}

The multi-agent debate (MAD) paradigm was introduced by
\citet{du2024improving}, who let several instances of a language model
independently propose answers and then critique and revise them over multiple
rounds before committing to a joint response. This ``society of minds'' setup
was reported to improve factual accuracy and mathematical reasoning while
reducing hallucination, using an identical procedure across tasks and treating
the underlying model as a black box. The core intuition, that partially
independent agents make partially independent errors which deliberation can
surface and correct, has since motivated a broad family of systems.

Subsequent work has varied the debate design along several axes.
\citet{liang2024encouraging} frame MAD as a remedy for the
\emph{Degeneration-of-Thought} problem, in which a single reflecting model
cannot escape a confident but incorrect initial position; they introduce a
``tit-for-tat'' exchange moderated by a judge agent and report that both an
adequate level of disagreement between agents and a well-chosen debate
termination rule are necessary for gains. \citet{chen2024reconcile} propose
ReConcile, a round-table procedure in which \emph{heterogeneous} models exchange
explanations and confidence-weighted votes, arguing that the diversity arising
from different model families is critical to the improvement. Beyond reasoning,
\citet{chan2024chateval} apply multi-agent debate to text evaluation, using a
panel of models to produce more reliable judgements than a single evaluator.

A second line of work studies the structural parameters of debate.
\citet{li2024sparsetopology} show that restricting who communicates with whom, a
sparse communication topology, can match or exceed fully-connected debate while
substantially reducing token cost. \citet{liu2025problemsolving} extend this to
arbitrary network topologies and report that random networks perform comparably
to fully-connected ones at far lower cost, that hub placement matters (a
correctly-biased hub node boosts performance), and that strong group consensus
tends to coincide with correct answers. \citet{kaesberg2025voting} isolate the
decision protocol itself, finding that voting protocols help reasoning tasks
while consensus protocols help knowledge tasks, that adding agents improves
performance but adding discussion rounds can degrade it. Efficiency-oriented
designs such as GroupDebate \citep{liu2024groupdebate} organise agents into
discussion groups that debate internally and exchange summaries, reducing tokens
while retaining accuracy.

Against this largely optimistic backdrop, a critical literature has emerged that
questions whether debate delivers what it promises. \citet{liu2025stopovervaluing}
evaluate MAD across nine benchmarks and four base models and find that it
frequently fails to outperform simple single-agent baselines such as
chain-of-thought and self-consistency, despite consuming substantially more
inference-time compute; they argue that model heterogeneity, rather than debate
per se, is what reliably helps. \citet{zhang2025debateorvote} decompose MAD into
its constituent mechanisms and show that majority voting alone accounts for most
of the gains usually attributed to debate; they further prove that, under their
formalisation, debate induces a martingale over agents' belief trajectories and
therefore does not improve expected correctness on its own. \citet{yang2025revisiting}
reframe MAD as a form of test-time scaling and find its value to be conditional,
offering limited advantage over single-agent scaling except when problems are
hard and the base model is weak. What unifies both the affirmative and the
critical strands is that they evaluate debate almost exclusively by its
\emph{final outcome}. The deliberative process that produces that outcome, and
whether two systems reaching the same accuracy do so through genuinely different
dynamics, remains largely outside the scope of these evaluations.


\subsection{Dynamics and Emergence in LLM Collectives}
\label{sec:related:dynamics}

A distinct and more recent line of work looks inside the debate at the
\emph{dynamics} of how agents' positions evolve and influence one another. Much
of it documents systematic pathologies rather than aggregate accuracy.
\citet{prasad2025certain} stage repeated adversarial debates between LLMs and
find pervasive miscalibration: models begin overconfident, grow \emph{more}
confident that they are winning as the debate proceeds even when no new
supporting evidence is introduced, and frequently both claim a high probability
of victory simultaneously, a logical impossibility. This upward, anti-Bayesian
confidence drift is precisely the kind of positive-feedback signature that
motivates a dynamical treatment.

Several studies document conformity and herding. \citet{wynn2025talk} show that
when agents differ in capability, debate can \emph{decrease} accuracy over time
even when stronger models outnumber weaker ones, because models frequently shift
from correct to incorrect answers in response to peer reasoning, favouring
agreement over challenging flawed arguments; they attribute this to sycophancy
and social conformity and warn that debate needs safeguards against persuasive
but incorrect reasoning. \citet{cho2025herd} characterise this herd behaviour
quantitatively, finding that the gap between an agent's self-confidence and its
perceived confidence in peers governs how likely it is to conform, and that the
format in which peer information is presented modulates the effect.
\citet{choi2025groupconformity}, across more than two thousand simulated
debates, report that agents tend to align with the numerically dominant group or
with more capable agents, reducing opinion diversity in a manner that mirrors
human group conformity. Earlier, \citet{zhang2024socialpsych} had already shown
that LLM collectives exhibit human-like social behaviours such as conformity and
consensus reaching, drawing an explicit connection to social-psychology theory.

These behaviours are increasingly understood as \emph{emergent} properties of
the collective rather than of any single agent. \citet{chen2024agentverse}
observe emergent social behaviours in multi-agent collaboration and discuss
leveraging the beneficial ones while mitigating the harmful ones.
\citet{chen2023consensus} study consensus seeking directly and find that, absent
explicit instruction, LLM agents default to an averaging strategy, with the
number of agents, assigned personalities, and network structure all shaping the
negotiation. \citet{chuang2025debate} construct a benchmark of human opinion
dynamics with paired public messages and private beliefs and report that
role-playing LLM groups converge far more strongly and rapidly than human groups
do, indicating a systematic bias toward premature agreement. A structurally
sharper version of this failure is captured by HiddenBench
\citep{li2026hiddenbench}, which adapts the hidden-profile paradigm from social
psychology \citep{stasser1985pooling, schulzhardt2012synergy}: when the evidence
needed for the correct answer is distributed across agents, collective accuracy
collapses from 80.7\,\% (a single agent with complete information) to 30.1\,\%,
because agents lock onto shared information and fail to pool their private
knowledge.

The vocabulary needed to name these phenomena precisely comes from
complex-systems theory. Recurring interaction structures are studied as
\emph{network motifs}, small subgraphs that appear more often than in a
randomised network and that generate characteristic behaviour
\citep{milo2002network, alon2007network}; the complementary notion of a
\emph{dynamical motif}, a recurring pattern in the system's trajectory such as an
attractor or an oscillation, has recently been formalised for recurrent networks
\citep{driscoll2024dynamical}. The individual regimes are classical: positive
feedback and lock-in \citep{arthur1989competing}, and limit cycles, fixed points,
and multistability in nonlinear dynamics \citep{strogatz2015nonlinear}. On the
influence side, opinion-dynamics models formalise how agreement and social power
propagate through a network and when influence concentrates on a single node
\citep{degroot1974consensus, jia2015opinion}, and the continuous-opinion,
discrete-action framework \citep{martins2008coda} matches the setting of agents
that hold latent confidence but cast discrete votes. At the level of principle,
\citet{miehling2025agentic} argue that agentic AI must be analysed through a
systems-theoretic lens precisely because group-level behaviour cannot be
predicted from individual agent properties, but their contribution is conceptual
and supplies no empirical instrument for detecting such behaviour.


\subsection{Ways of Analysing Multi-Agent Systems}
\label{sec:related:lenses}

Before stating the gap, it is worth making explicit \emph{why} a
complex-systems treatment is the appropriate instrument, by situating it against
the alternative ways one might analyse an LLM collective. Each lens answers a
different question and carries different costs.

\paragraph{Outcome and benchmark evaluation.}
The dominant approach scores a system by its final task performance on a
benchmark \citep{du2024improving, liu2025stopovervaluing, zhang2025debateorvote}.
Its strengths are decisive: it is objective, comparable across systems, and
directly measures the quantity practitioners ultimately care about. Its weakness
is equally decisive for our purposes. An outcome is a scalar summary of a
trajectory, and two systems that reach the same accuracy through entirely
different dynamics, one by genuine error correction, the other by a confident
slide into a shared mistake, are indistinguishable under it. It can say
\emph{whether} a configuration worked but not \emph{why}, and it provides no
vocabulary for a run's failure mode.

\paragraph{Mechanistic interpretability.}
A second approach would open the models themselves, analysing weights, activations,
or attention to explain behaviour. This operates at the wrong level of abstraction
for our question: the phenomena of interest, consensus, oscillation, influence
concentration, are properties of the \emph{group} trajectory, not of any single
forward pass, and emerge from interaction rather than from internal computation
\citep{miehling2025agentic, chen2024agentverse}. It is also infeasible for the
closed, API-served models typical of applied multi-agent systems, whose internals
are inaccessible.

\paragraph{Qualitative transcript analysis.}
A third approach reads the debate transcripts directly, characterising the content
of arguments and the rhetoric of persuasion. This is rich and interpretable, and
we draw on it informally when reading the mechanism behind the flagged
limit-cycle cases (\autoref{sec:results:lc}). But on its own it is subjective, does not scale to tens
of thousands of runs, and yields no falsifiable structure that can be tested
against a null. It complements a quantitative lens rather than replacing it.

\paragraph{The complex-systems / dynamical lens.}
The approach taken here treats the debate as a dynamical system and measures
recurring patterns in its state trajectory \citep{strogatz2015nonlinear,
driscoll2024dynamical}. Its advantages match the gaps above: it operates at the
group level where the interesting behaviour lives, it is quantitative and scales to
the full dataset, it requires only interaction logs (not model internals), and each
motif has a falsifiable signature that can be tested against a principled null. Its
disadvantages must be stated as plainly. The transfer of motif concepts from
physical and biological systems to an LLM collective is a modelling choice, not a
law; the detection thresholds are partly conventional; a four-agent group is small
for some of these measures; and, absent intervention, the detectors identify
\emph{signatures} and \emph{associations}, not certified attractors or causes. We
adopt this lens because it is the only one of the four that can name and measure
the dynamical pathologies the previous subsection documented, while remaining
applicable to the black-box models of practical interest, but we carry its
limitations forward explicitly into the interpretation of every result
(\autoref{sec:discussion:limitations}).


\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{sec:related:gap}

Three observations follow from the literature above. First, the mainstream
evaluation of multi-agent debate is outcome-centric: both its proponents and its
critics measure final accuracy and largely leave the deliberative trajectory
unexamined \citep{du2024improving, liu2025stopovervaluing, zhang2025debateorvote}.
Second, the emerging work on interaction dynamics does examine the trajectory,
but it typically documents a single pathology at a time, overconfidence, herding,
or premature consensus, in descriptive terms
\citep{prasad2025certain, wynn2025talk, cho2025herd, choi2025groupconformity},
without connecting them to the formal dynamical regimes they resemble. Third, the
complex-systems vocabulary that would unify these observations, self-reinforcement,
limit cycles, bistability, and dominance, is at most invoked informally: the
motif and opinion-dynamics frameworks that could make the phenomena measurable
\citep{milo2002network, driscoll2024dynamical, jia2015opinion} have not been
applied to LLM-based debate, and the call for a systems-theoretic treatment
\citep{miehling2025agentic} has not been operationalised.

The gap this thesis addresses is therefore the absence of an \emph{empirical,
literature-grounded detection} of self-organization motifs in LLM-based
multi-agent debate. We treat the debate not as a black box to be scored by its
output, but as a dynamical system whose trajectory carries measurable structure.
Concretely, we (i) define detectors for self-reinforcement, limit cycles,
bistability, and dominance, each grounded in the corresponding complex-systems or
opinion-dynamics literature and equipped with a principled null model, rather than
in ad hoc thresholds; (ii) characterise how these motifs appear or vanish under
controlled variation of the two canonical structural axes of multi-agent design,
communication topology and memory window; and (iii) relate motif presence to
task outcomes across two deliberately contrasting regimes, a truth-verifiable
benchmark where agents start with reasonable priors (GPQA-Diamond) and a
hidden-profile benchmark where they start systematically wrong
(HiddenBench), so that the role of a reliable individual signal can be isolated.
In doing so we also treat the \emph{absence} of a motif as an informative
finding, a stance the outcome-centric literature has no natural place for.
