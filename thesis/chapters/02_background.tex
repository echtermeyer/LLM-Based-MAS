This section provides the conceptual vocabulary needed to follow the rest of
the thesis. \autoref{sec:bg:complex} introduces complex systems,
self-organization, and dynamical systems as the formal language for describing
collective behaviour, including the long-run regimes a system can settle into.
\autoref{sec:bg:motifs} introduces motifs and the opinion-dynamics models of
belief updating under repeated interaction. \autoref{sec:bg:mad} fixes what is
meant by an LLM agent and by multi-agent debate, and lays out the design space
along which debate systems vary. The exact motifs this thesis detects, and the
methodology used to detect them, are defined in \autoref{sec:method};
this section only fixes the vocabulary they build on.


\subsection{Complex Systems and Self-Organization}
\label{sec:bg:complex}

A \emph{complex system} is a collection of many interacting components whose
collective behaviour cannot be inferred from the properties of the individual
components alone \citep{anderson1972more, ladyman2013complex}. The behaviour is
called \emph{emergent}: a stock-market bubble, for instance, is produced by
millions of traders acting on local information with no coordinating
authority, yet the resulting price dynamic is not present in any individual
trade \citep{mitchell2009complexity}. When such emergent order arises without
any central controller, the system is called \emph{self-organizing}
\citep{ashby1947principles, camazine2001self}. Four ingredients recur across
self-organizing systems \citep{camazine2001self}: (i) \emph{positive
feedback}, which amplifies small deviations and drives the system away from
uniformity; (ii) \emph{negative feedback}, which prevents runaway growth and
bounds the system within reachable states; (iii) \emph{fluctuations}, the
stochastic perturbations that allow the system to explore the state space; and
(iv) \emph{repeated local interactions}, through which global structure
accumulates over many iterations from purely local rules. The organising
principle is encoded entirely in these interactions, not in any single
component and not in any external authority. The same local rule can
therefore lead to different long-run outcomes depending on where the system
starts and which fluctuations it encounters: the interaction rule fixes the
repertoire of possible end states, while the initial configuration and the
realised noise jointly select which one is reached.

To study self-organizing systems rigorously, one needs a language for how
states evolve over time. That language is \emph{dynamical systems theory}
\citep{strogatz2015nonlinear}. A dynamical system consists of a state, which
collects everything needed to describe the system at a given moment, and an
update rule that turns the current state into the next one. The set of all
possible states is the \emph{phase space}, and as time advances the system
traces a \emph{trajectory} through it. The classical theory assumes a
deterministic rule acting on a continuous state, but three extensions cover
the cases that matter here. In a \emph{stochastic} system the rule assigns
probabilities to the possible next states instead of fixing one, so the
trajectory becomes a Markov chain. In a system with \emph{memory} the next
state depends on a window of recent states rather than on the current one
alone. In a \emph{discrete-state} system the state takes one of finitely many
values, and the rule becomes a table of transitions rather than a differential
equation. The debate systems studied in this thesis combine all three: the
state is discrete, since each agent casts one of finitely many votes; the
update is stochastic, since agents sample their responses; and it carries
bounded memory, since each agent conditions on a finite window of recent
rounds.

The central question in dynamical systems theory is where trajectories end up.
After an initial transient, most trajectories settle into specific regions of
phase space called \emph{attractors}, towards which nearby trajectories
converge and in which they then remain. The classical image is a ball rolling
under gravity on a hilly landscape: wherever the ball starts, friction brings
it to rest in one of the valleys. Not every resting point is an attractor,
however. A ball balanced on a hilltop is also at rest, but it is unstable,
because any small push moves it away rather than back. An attractor is
\emph{stable}: small deviations from it decay. The set of starting points from
which the system reaches a given attractor is that attractor's \emph{basin of
attraction}. The simplest and most common long-run regime is
\emph{fixed-point convergence}, in which the system settles into a single state
and stays there. A structurally different regime is the \emph{limit cycle}, a
closed loop of states that the system revisits indefinitely without ever
settling. A limit cycle is itself attracting: a small perturbation pushes the
trajectory off the loop only briefly before the dynamics pull it back. In the
stochastic, discrete setting of a debate, these two regimes take the form of
absorbing states, which are never left once entered, and of sets of states
that keep being revisited.

A qualitatively richer situation arises when the landscape contains not one
valley but several, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:doublewell}. A system is
\emph{multistable} when two or more distinct attractors coexist under the same
parameters \citep{feudel2008complex}. \autoref{fig:doublewell} shows the
simplest example, a double-well potential with two stable states separated by
an unstable peak. The boundary between the two basins of attraction is the
\emph{separatrix}. Two runs of the same experiment with identical parameters,
but starting on opposite sides of the separatrix, end in entirely different
long-run states. When many runs of the same system are observed, the share of
runs that reach each attractor therefore reflects the relative size of the
basins. The double-well is deterministic and continuous, whereas the debate we
study is a finite-state stochastic chain. There, coexisting attractors become
competing absorbing states, and basin size is no longer a geometric area but a
probability: the share of repetitions that end in each absorbing state, given
how the debate is initialised \citep{menck2013basin}.

\subsection{Motifs and Opinion Dynamics}
\label{sec:bg:motifs}

The patterns that arise in dynamical systems are not arbitrary. Across
biological networks, electronic circuits, social groups, and neural dynamics,
the same recurring organisational building blocks appear with consistent
functional properties. Complex-systems research calls these recurring patterns
\emph{motifs}. The concept was introduced for networks by
\citet{milo2002network}, who defined a \emph{structural motif} as a small
subgraph that appears significantly more often in a real network than in a
randomly rewired surrogate with the same degree sequence. A classic example is
the \emph{feed-forward loop}, a three-node pattern in which A influences both B
and C while B also influences C, whose functional role recurs across
transcriptional, signalling, and neuronal networks \citep{alon2007network}. A
complementary concept is the \emph{dynamical motif} \citep{driscoll2024dynamical}:
a recurring pattern in the \emph{trajectory} of a system rather than in its
wiring. Where a structural motif is a statement about who interacts with whom,
a dynamical motif is a statement about how the system behaves over time. The
two are related, since structural motifs are studied precisely because each
generates a characteristic dynamical regime, but they are not equivalent: the
same structural motif can produce different dynamics under different
parameters, and the same dynamical pattern can arise from different structural
configurations.

Positive feedback is a mechanism that links structural and dynamical motifs:
deviations are amplified rather than corrected, turning an early advantage into
a self-reinforcing lead. \citet{arthur1989competing} gave the canonical social
example with technology lock-in, where increasing returns keep an early leader
ahead even when alternatives are objectively superior. Positive feedback can
also operate through network structure: a node positioned to receive and relay
signals from many others accumulates causal weight, concentrating influence
through its position rather than through any informational advantage.
Understanding whether such concentration is a property of the prescribed
topology or of the dynamics themselves requires distinguishing structural
connectivity from effective connectivity \citep{friston2011connectivity}.
\emph{Structural connectivity} is the static graph encoding who is allowed to
communicate with whom. \emph{Effective connectivity} is the causal influence
one unit actually exerts on another across a recorded trajectory; it can be
estimated from the trajectory under a model of how influence operates, even
when the prescribed wiring is fixed. The same structural graph can give rise
to very different effective-connectivity patterns depending on which nodes
happen to speak with authority at critical moments.

For the specific setting of agents forming and updating beliefs through
repeated local interaction, there is a dedicated family of formal models:
\emph{opinion dynamics}. The models differ in what they assume about the
opinion variable (continuous or discrete), the update rule (deterministic or
stochastic), and who listens to whom (all peers, similar others, or no one).
The foundational DeGroot model \citep{degroot1974consensus} considers $N$
agents, each holding a real-valued opinion, who at every step replace their
opinion with a weighted average of the opinions of the agents they trust. The
model contains no ground truth and no external information. When the trust
network is strongly connected and aperiodic, the process converges to a
consensus that is itself a weighted average of the initial opinions; the weight
an agent's initial opinion carries in that consensus is its \emph{social power}
\citep{jia2015opinion}. An agent gains social power by being trusted by other
high-power agents, not by being correct. The extreme case, in which almost all
social power rests on a single agent while the others carry almost none, is the
\emph{autocratic} pole of the model \citep{jia2015opinion}; it is the
opinion-dynamics form of the dominance motif we detect. Convergence to a single
consensus requires the trust network to be both strongly connected and
aperiodic, and each condition can fail in a way that maps onto one of our
motifs. If the network contains two or more closed groups that listen only to
each other, those groups settle on different consensus values, a group-level
multistability; if aperiodicity fails, the process never settles but cycles
through a repeating sequence of states, a limit cycle. Multistability and limit
cycles are thus the two ways the same convergence guarantee can break down.

Three refinements of this basic picture recur in what follows. Agents often
hold a graded internal belief but must commit to a discrete public choice: the
continuous-opinion, discrete-action (CODA) model \citep{martins2008coda}
captures exactly this split and is the template for our agent state, whose
latent confidence evolves continuously while its vote stays discrete
(\autoref{sec:setup:protocol}).
When the opinions are discrete labels with no intrinsic ranking, naming-game
and related discrete-consensus models describe how a population settles on a
shared convention \citep{baronchelli2018consensus}, the regime our
multistability analysis appeals to (\autoref{sec:results:multistability}).
And a stubborn agent that never updates acts as a fixed anchor that can pull
the group toward its position \citep{friedkin1999social}, precisely the variant
our devil's advocate probe instantiates (\autoref{sec:results:seeded}).


\subsection{LLM Agents and Multi-Agent Debate}
\label{sec:bg:mad}

A \emph{large language model} (LLM) is a neural network, in current practice a
Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention}, trained to predict the next token in a
sequence and aligned to follow instructions through supervised fine-tuning and
reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt}. In
the broad sense, ``LLM agent'' covers any system that places an LLM in a loop
with tools, environment feedback, or other models. In the narrow sense used
throughout this thesis, an \emph{LLM agent} is an LLM in a message-passing loop
with other LLMs: it receives a state comprising the task, its prior position,
and messages from peers; it produces an action consisting of a written argument
and a vote; and the result is fed back as the next state. Sampling is
stochastic: temperature controls the sharpness of the output distribution, and
raising it increases response diversity at the cost of coherence, so two agents
given identical prompts at a positive temperature can produce different
responses. An agent's effective state is its context, the accumulated text it
conditions on, and context length is finite; beyond a certain history depth,
earlier rounds must be truncated or summarised, which is why the extent of
retained interaction history is a design parameter with functional
consequences.

The standard single-model inference-time alternatives to multi-agent protocols
are chain-of-thought prompting \citep{wei2022chainofthought}, which improves
reasoning by eliciting step-by-step intermediate text from one model, and
self-consistency \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}, which samples several
reasoning paths from a single model and returns the majority answer. Both can
be applied without additional models or communication rounds and serve as the
principal baselines against which multi-agent protocols are compared. A third
alternative is \emph{self-correction}, in which a single model critiques and
revises its own answer from self-generated feedback
\citep{madaan2023selfrefine}. Without any external feedback, however, such
intrinsic self-correction often fails to improve reasoning and can even degrade
it \citep{huang2024selfcorrect}, which is one motivation for bringing in other
agents as a source of critique.

Placing several such agents in a shared loop yields an LLM-based
\emph{multi-agent system} \citep{guo2024survey}. Much of this literature assigns
agents distinct functional roles and divides a task among them, for instance by
encoding the stages of a software project as standardised operating procedures
\citep{hong2024metagpt}, or provides general frameworks from which such teams
can be assembled \citep{chen2024agentverse}. \emph{Multi-agent debate} (MAD) is
the special case in which all agents address the same question over the same
answer space, so that their outputs can be compared, contested, and aggregated.
In MAD, several LLM agents independently propose an answer and then exchange
and revise their reasoning over multiple rounds before committing to a final
decision \citep{du2024improving}. Each round typically consists of agents
reading the current arguments of their peers and producing an updated argument
and vote in response. The group commits when it reaches sufficient agreement or
a round limit is hit. The motivating argument is error correction: if agents
make partially independent mistakes, deliberation lets the group surface and
correct them. This gain assumes the mistakes are independent, however:
correlated votes, which social influence itself induces, erode it, and beyond a
threshold the majority no longer outperforms an individual
\citep{ladha1992condorcet}. A second motivation concerns what a single model
cannot do on its own: once a model has settled confidently on an answer,
reflecting on it alone rarely produces a new line of thought, a failure
\citet{liang2024encouraging} call \emph{Degeneration-of-Thought}, and peers can
supply the external challenge that self-reflection lacks. The term ``debate''
also names a distinct proposal in AI safety where two models argue before a
human judge in order to make model reasoning checkable
\citep{irving2018safety}; the collaborative protocol studied here descends from
the reasoning-improvement line rather than from that one, so there is no
separate judge and the agents themselves both argue and decide.

Across its many variants, a debate can be described by a common sequence of
steps (\autoref{fig:mad}a). In \emph{initialisation}, each agent answers the
question without seeing the others; in \emph{exchange}, each agent receives some
subset of its peers' outputs; and in \emph{revision}, it produces an updated
argument and vote. \emph{Termination} decides whether another round of exchange
and revision follows, and \emph{aggregation} combines the individual votes into
a group answer. Every design choice in a debate system fixes how one of these
steps is carried out. A recent systematic review of 141 debate studies
organises these choices along three dimensions \citep{motger2026taxonomy}: who
takes part (\emph{participants}), how information flows between them
(\emph{interaction}), and how the group reaches its answer (\emph{agreement}).

\paragraph{Participants.}
Agents may be instances of one model or of several \citep{chen2024reconcile},
and they may carry distinct personas or functional roles
\citep{chan2024chateval}, including a separate judge that moderates the
exchange and decides the outcome \citep{liang2024encouraging}. Such variation
is the literature's main means of introducing deliberate diversity into the
group, motivated by the independence assumption above: agents that differ are
less likely to share the same errors.

\paragraph{Interaction.}
The \emph{communication topology} fixes who reads whom (\autoref{fig:mad}b). In
a fully-connected group every agent sees every other; in a star, peripheral
agents communicate only through a central hub; and sparser arrangements such as
a ring restrict each agent to a few neighbours \citep{li2024sparsetopology}.
These topologies are usually fixed in advance, but the communication graph can
also be optimised \citep{zhuge2024gptswarm} or adapted while the debate runs
\citep{liu2024dylan}. Within a round, agents can speak sequentially in a fixed
order or simultaneously, each responding to the same context
\citep{chan2024chateval}. The \emph{memory window} fixes how much interaction
history each agent retains from one round to the next (\autoref{fig:mad}c),
controlling the influence of earlier rounds on current decisions.

\paragraph{Agreement.}
The \emph{decision protocol} fixes how individual votes are aggregated into a
group answer: by majority vote, by a vote weighted by each agent's stated
confidence \citep{chen2024reconcile}, by a judge \citep{liang2024encouraging},
or by requiring consensus \citep{kaesberg2025voting}. Tied to it is the
\emph{stopping rule}, which runs the debate for a fixed number of rounds or ends
it early once the group agrees or its answers stabilise
\citep{yin2023eot, hu2025stabilitydetection}.

The same review finds that the field has converged on a narrow default of
static, fully-connected topologies, verbatim exchange, short-term memory, and
vote-based resolution, adopted by convention rather than through systematic
comparison \citep{motger2026taxonomy}. The system studied in this thesis
(\autoref{sec:exp}) stays close to this default, so that its dynamics speak to
debate as it is commonly deployed. It departs from it where measurement
requires, by separating private beliefs from public messages, and it varies the
two interaction choices shown in \autoref{fig:mad}b and~c, topology and memory
window. How these design choices behave in practice, and the growing evidence
that debate can also amplify errors rather than correct them, is the subject of
the related work reviewed next (\autoref{sec:related}).
