\subsection{Complex Systems and Self-Organization}
\label{sec:bg:complex}
% ============================================================

A \emph{complex system} is a collection of many interacting components whose
collective behaviour cannot be inferred from the properties of the individual
components alone \citep{anderson1972more, ladyman2013complex}. The behaviour is
called \emph{emergent}: a stock-market bubble, for instance, is produced by
millions of traders acting on local information with no coordinating authority,
yet the resulting price dynamic is not present in any individual trade
\citep{mitchell2009complexity}. When such emergent order arises without any
central controller, the system is
called \emph{self-organizing} \citep{ashby1947principles, camazine2001self}.
There are four ingredients that recur across self-organizing systems
\citep{camazine2001self}: (i)~\emph{positive feedback}, which amplifies small
deviations and drives the system away from uniformity; (ii)~\emph{negative
feedback}, which prevents runaway growth and bounds the system within reachable
states; (iii)~\emph{fluctuations}, the stochastic perturbations that allow the
system to explore the state space; and (iv)~\emph{repeated local interactions},
through which global structure accumulates over many iterations from purely
local rules. The organising principle is encoded entirely in these interactions,
not in any single component and not in any external authority. Because the same
local rule can lead to different long-run outcomes depending on which
fluctuations are realised and where the system starts, the interaction rule
fixes the \emph{repertoire} of possible attractors while the initial
configuration and the realised noise jointly select which one is reached.

To study self-organizing systems rigorously, one needs a language for how
states evolve over time. That language is \emph{dynamical systems theory}
\citep{strogatz2015nonlinear}. The most general deterministic form is the
discrete-time map
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}(t+1) = F\bigl(\mathbf{x}(t)\bigr),
  \label{eq:bg:det}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is the full state at step $t$ and $F$ encodes the
interaction law. The space of all possible states is the \emph{phase space};
as time advances, the system traces a \emph{trajectory} through it. Three
extensions cover practically important cases. In \emph{stochastic dynamics} the
update is a transition kernel, $\mathbf{x}(t+1) \sim P\bigl(\cdot \mid
\mathbf{x}(t)\bigr)$, and the trajectory is a Markov chain; absorbing states
and recurrent classes then replace fixed points and cycles as the fundamental
long-run objects. In \emph{memory systems} the rule looks back $k$ steps,
$\mathbf{x}(t+1) = F\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}(t), \ldots, \mathbf{x}(t-k+1)\bigr)$,
so the effective state is the window of the last $k$ observations. In
\emph{discrete-state systems} $\mathbf{x}(t)$ takes values in a finite or
countable set, and update rules describe transition tables rather than
differential equations. The debate systems studied in this thesis instantiate
all three at once: the state is discrete (each agent casts one of finitely many
votes), the update is stochastic (sampling temperature), and it carries bounded
memory (each agent conditions on a finite window of recent rounds).

The central question in dynamical systems theory is where trajectories end up.
Most trajectories, after an initial transient, settle toward specific regions
called \emph{attractors}: invariant sets to which nearby trajectories converge.
The classical image is a ball rolling under gravity on a hilly landscape:
wherever the ball starts, friction brings it to rest in one of the valleys.
However, not every fixed set is an attractor; for example, a ball balanced on a hilltop is also an
equilibrium but it is unstable, because any small perturbation moves it away
rather than back. An attractor is a stable fixed set on which small deviations
decay. The \emph{basin of attraction} is the set of initial conditions whose
trajectories converge to that attractor; its \emph{depth} is the barrier height
that must be overcome to escape, while its \emph{width} is the volume of
initial conditions it captures. Depth and width are independent: a deep but
narrow well is easy to stay in once entered but hard to reach from most starting
points. The simplest and most common dynamical regime is \emph{fixed-point
convergence}: the system settles to a state $\mathbf{x}^*$ satisfying
$F(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{x}^*$ and stays there. A structurally different
regime is the \emph{limit cycle}: a closed periodic orbit to which nearby
trajectories spiral, so the system revisits the same sequence of states
indefinitely without ever settling. Unlike the neutrally stable oscillation of
a frictionless pendulum, a limit cycle is attracting (asymptotically stable): small
perturbations push the trajectory off the cycle transiently, but the dynamics
pull it back.

A qualitatively richer situation arises when the phase-space landscape contains
not one valley but several, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:bg:doublewell}. A
system is \emph{multistable} when two or
more distinct attractors coexist for the same parameter values
\citep{feudel2008complex}. \autoref{fig:bg:doublewell} shows the simplest
example, the potential $V(x) = (x^2 - 1)^2$: two stable states at $x = \pm 1$
are separated by an unstable fixed point at $x = 0$. Each attractor has a
basin of attraction; the boundary between basins is the \emph{separatrix}. Two
runs of the same experiment with identical parameters but starting on opposite
sides of the separatrix converge to entirely different long-run states. When
many runs of the same system are observed, the distribution over which attractor
each run reached reflects the geometry of the basins. The double-well of
\autoref{fig:bg:doublewell} is a deterministic, continuous potential, whereas the
debate we study is a finite-state stochastic chain. In that setting the analogue
of coexisting attractors is a set of competing \emph{absorbing states}, and the
analogue of basin volume is not a geometric area but a probability measured
relative to the initialisation distribution, since it is that distribution which
decides how often each absorbing state is reached \citep{menck2013basin}.

  \caption[Multistable double-well energy landscape]{The potential $V(x) = (x^2-1)^2$
    has two stable attractors at $x = \pm 1$, separated by an unstable fixed

    Initial conditions in Basin~A converge to the left attractor; those in

\subsection{Motifs and Opinion Dynamics}
\label{sec:bg:motifs}
% ============================================================

The patterns that arise in dynamical systems are not arbitrary. Across
biological networks, electronic circuits, social groups, and neural dynamics,
the same recurring organisational building blocks appear with consistent
functional properties. Complex-systems research calls these recurring patterns
\emph{motifs}. The concept was introduced for networks by
\citet{milo2002network}, who defined a \emph{structural motif} as a small
subgraph that appears significantly more often in a real network than in a
randomly rewired surrogate with the same degree sequence. \citet{alon2007network}
showed that the \emph{coherent feed-forward loop}, a three-node pattern in
which A influences both B and C while B also influences C, acts as a
sign-sensitive delay filter under AND logic, and this functional property is
conserved a property that recurs across transcription, signalling and neuronal networks.
A complementary concept is the \emph{dynamical motif}
\citep{driscoll2024dynamical}: a recurring pattern in the \emph{trajectory} of
a system rather than in its wiring. Where a structural motif is a statement
about who interacts with whom, a dynamical motif is a statement about how the
system behaves over time. The two are related, since structural motifs are
studied precisely because each generates a characteristic dynamical regime, but
they are not equivalent: the same structural motif can produce different
dynamics under different parameters, and the same dynamical pattern can arise
from different structural configurations.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{plots/bg/motifs.pdf}
  \caption[Structural versus dynamical motif]{The two senses of \emph{motif}.
    \emph{Left:} a structural motif is a recurring wiring pattern; the coherent
    feed-forward loop shown here has A influence both B and C while B also
    influences C, so under AND logic C changes only once both of its inputs do.
    It is a statement about who influences whom. \emph{Right:} a dynamical motif
    is a recurring pattern in the trajectory; here a trajectory (grey) spirals
    onto a limit cycle (blue), a self-sustained periodic orbit. It is a statement
    about how the system behaves over time. The same wiring can yield different
    dynamics, and the same dynamics can arise from different wiring.}
  \label{fig:bg:motifs}
\end{figure}

Positive feedback is a mechanism that links structural and dynamical motifs:
deviations are amplified rather than corrected, turning an early advantage into
a self-reinforcing lead. \citet{arthur1989competing} gave the canonical social
example with technology lock-in, where increasing returns keep an early leader
ahead even when alternatives are objectively superior. Positive feedback can
also operate through network structure: a node positioned to receive and relay
signals from many others accumulates causal weight, concentrating influence
through its position rather than through any informational advantage.
Understanding whether such concentration is a property of the prescribed
topology or of the dynamics themselves requires distinguishing \emph{structural
connectivity} from \emph{effective connectivity} \citep{friston2011connectivity}.
Structural connectivity is the static graph encoding who is allowed to
communicate with whom. Effective connectivity is the causal influence one unit
actually exerts on another across a recorded trajectory; it can be estimated
from the trajectory under a model of how influence operates, even when the prescribed wiring is fixed. The same
structural graph can give rise to very different effective-connectivity patterns
depending on which nodes happen to speak with authority at critical moments.

For the specific setting of agents forming and updating beliefs through
repeated local interaction, there is a dedicated family of formal models:
\emph{opinion dynamics}. The models differ in what they assume about the
opinion variable (continuous or discrete), the update rule (deterministic or
stochastic), and who listens to whom (all peers, similar others, or no one).
The foundational DeGroot model \citep{degroot1974consensus} considers $N$
agents, each holding a real-valued opinion $p_i(t)$. At each step, every agent
replaces its opinion with a weighted average of all agents' current opinions:
\begin{equation}
  p_i(t+1) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} T_{ij}\, p_j(t),
  \qquad \text{i.e.,} \quad
  \mathbf{p}(t) = T^t\, \mathbf{p}(0),
  \label{eq:bg:degroot}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is a row-stochastic trust matrix ($T_{ij} \geq 0$, $\sum_j T_{ij} =
1$). The model contains no ground truth and no external information. When the
trust network is strongly connected and aperiodic, the process converges to a
consensus $c = \boldsymbol{\pi}^\top \mathbf{p}(0)$, where $\boldsymbol{\pi}$
is the unique left eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue~$1$, normalised so that
$\sum_i \pi_i = 1$. The vector $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is called the \emph{social
power} vector \citep{jia2015opinion}: $\pi_i$ is the weight agent $i$'s
initial opinion carries in the final consensus. An agent gains social power by
being trusted by other high-power agents, not by being correct. The extreme
case, in which almost all social power rests on a single agent while the others
carry almost none, is the \emph{autocratic} pole of the model
\citep{jia2015opinion}; it is the opinion-dynamics form of the dominance motif we
detect. Convergence to a single consensus requires the trust network to be both
strongly connected and aperiodic, and each condition can fail in a way that maps
onto one of our motifs. If the network contains two or more closed groups that listen only to each other,
those groups settle on different consensus values, a group-level multistability;
if aperiodicity fails, the process never settles but cycles through a repeating
sequence of states, a limit cycle. Multistability and limit cycles are thus the two
ways the same convergence guarantee can break down.

Three refinements of this basic picture recur in what follows. Agents often hold
a graded internal belief but must commit to a discrete public choice: the
\emph{continuous-opinion, discrete-action} (CODA) model \citep{martins2008coda}
captures exactly this split and is the template for our agent state, whose latent
confidence evolves continuously while its vote stays discrete
(\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:state}). When the opinions are discrete labels with
no intrinsic ranking, \emph{naming-game} and related discrete-consensus models
describe how a population settles on a shared convention
\citep{baronchelli2018consensus}, the regime our multistability analysis appeals to
(\autoref{sec:results:multistability}). And a \emph{stubborn} agent that never
updates acts as a fixed anchor that can pull the group toward its position
\citep{friedkin1999social}, precisely the variant our devil's advocate probe
instantiates (\autoref{sec:results:evid}).

\subsection{LLM Agents and Multi-Agent Debate}
\label{sec:bg:mad}
% ============================================================

A large language model (LLM) is a neural network, in current practice a
Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention}, trained to predict the next token in
a sequence and aligned to follow instructions through supervised fine-tuning
and reinforcement learning from human feedback \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt}.
In the broad sense, ``LLM agent'' covers any system that places an LLM in a loop
with tools, environment feedback, or other models. In the narrow sense used
throughout this thesis, an \emph{LLM agent} is an LLM in a message-passing
loop with other LLMs: it receives a state comprising the task, its prior
position, and messages from peers; it produces an action consisting of a
written argument and a vote; and the result is fed back as the next state.
Sampling is stochastic: \emph{temperature} controls the sharpness of the
output distribution, and raising it increases response diversity at the cost
of coherence, so two agents given identical prompts at a positive temperature
can produce different responses. An agent's effective state is its context, the
accumulated text it conditions on, and context length is finite; beyond a
certain history depth, earlier rounds must be truncated or summarised, which is
why the extent of retained interaction history is a design parameter with
functional consequences.

The standard single-model inference-time alternatives to multi-agent protocols
are \emph{chain-of-thought} prompting \citep{wei2022chainofthought}, which
improves reasoning by eliciting step-by-step intermediate text from one model,
and \emph{self-consistency} \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}, which samples
several reasoning paths from a single model and returns the majority answer.
Both can be applied without additional models or communication rounds and serve
as the principal baselines against which multi-agent protocols are compared.

\emph{Multi-agent debate} (MAD) is a protocol in which several LLM agents
independently propose an answer and then exchange and revise their reasoning
over multiple rounds before committing to a final decision
\citep{du2024improving}. Each round typically consists of agents reading the
current arguments of their peers and producing an updated argument and vote in
response. The group commits when it reaches sufficient agreement or a round
limit is hit. The motivating argument is error correction: if agents make
partially independent mistakes, deliberation lets the group surface and correct
them. This gain assumes the mistakes are independent, however: correlated votes,
which social influence itself induces, erode it, and beyond a threshold the
majority no longer outperforms an individual \citep{ladha1992condorcet}. Reported accuracy gains on factual and mathematical
reasoning \citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, chen2024reconcile} and
the use of debate panels as evaluators \citep{chan2024chateval} have made MAD
a standard building block in collaborative AI systems. The term ``debate'' also
names a distinct proposal in AI safety where two models argue before a human
judge in order to make model reasoning checkable \citep{irving2018safety}; the
collaborative protocol studied here descends from the reasoning-improvement
line rather than from that one.

Debate designs vary along several structural axes. The \emph{communication
topology} fixes who reads whom: a fully-connected group in which every agent
sees every other, versus sparser arrangements such as a star, where peripheral
agents communicate only through a central hub; sparser topologies can match
fully-connected debate at lower token cost \citep{li2024sparsetopology}. The
\emph{memory window} fixes how much interaction history each agent retains from
one round to the next, controlling the influence of earlier rounds on current
decisions. The \emph{decision protocol} fixes how individual votes are
aggregated into a group answer; this choice alone can change which tasks benefit
from debate \citep{kaesberg2025voting}. Additionally, debate systems vary in
whether agents are instances of one model or of several, and whether they are
assigned distinct personas or roles, a dimension the literature explores as a
means of introducing deliberate diversity into the group
\citep{liang2024encouraging}. How these design choices behave in practice, and
the growing evidence that debate can also amplify errors rather than correct
them, is the subject of the related work reviewed next (\autoref{sec:related}).
