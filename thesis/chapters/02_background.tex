\subsection{Complex Systems and Self-Organization}
\label{sec:bg:complex}

A \emph{complex system} is a collection of many components whose interactions
produce collective behaviour that cannot be read off from the components in
isolation. When such a system settles into ordered, coordinated behaviour
without any central controller imposing it, the behaviour is called
\emph{self-organizing} \citep{ashby1947principles}. The order is a property of
the interaction structure, not of any single unit: no agent ``decides'' the
global outcome, yet a global outcome reliably emerges. This is exactly the
regime a multi-agent debate occupies. Four homogeneous agents, each following
the same local rule (read the others, reconsider, vote), collectively produce a
group trajectory, consensus, deadlock, or oscillation, that none of them
individually selects. The central premise of this thesis is that such
trajectories are amenable to the same descriptive tools developed for physical,
biological, and social self-organizing systems.

A recurring theme in that literature is that the space of collective behaviours
is not arbitrary: interacting systems tend to fall into a small number of
qualitatively distinct \emph{regimes}, each with a characteristic signature.
It is useful to separate two senses in which a recurring pattern is called a
\emph{motif}. A \emph{structural} motif is a pattern in the wiring: a small
subgraph, such as a feed-forward loop, that appears in a network more often than
chance and that generates characteristic input--output behaviour
\citep{milo2002network, alon2007network}. A \emph{dynamical} motif is a pattern
in the trajectory rather than the wiring: a recurring feature of how the
system's state evolves over time, such as an attractor or a periodic orbit, a
notion recently formalised for recurrent neural networks
\citep{driscoll2024dynamical}. This thesis is concerned with dynamical motifs:
we hold the communication structure fixed as an experimental variable and ask
what patterns appear in the temporal evolution of the group's votes and
confidences.

Four dynamical regimes are central to this work, each classical in nonlinear
dynamics \citep{strogatz2015nonlinear}. \emph{Self-reinforcement} is positive
feedback: a state that, once entered, amplifies its own persistence. The
canonical example is lock-in through increasing returns, where an early lead
becomes self-perpetuating independently of intrinsic merit
\citep{arthur1989competing}. \emph{Limit cycles} are self-sustained periodic
oscillations: the system never settles to a fixed point but instead revisits the
same states in a repeating order. \emph{Bistability} (more generally
multistability) is the coexistence of several stable outcomes for the same
system, with the initial condition selecting which one is reached; the set of
starting states leading to a given outcome is its \emph{basin of attraction}.
\emph{Dominance} is the concentration of causal influence onto one component, so
that a single node disproportionately drives the collective state. These four
were chosen because each has a precise, testable signature and because each
corresponds directly to a pathology already reported informally in the debate
literature (\autoref{sec:related}).

The influence side of this picture has its own formal home in
\emph{opinion dynamics}, which studies how agreement and social power propagate
through a network of interacting agents. The classical DeGroot model treats each
agent's opinion as a weighted average of its neighbours' opinions and asks when
this process reaches consensus \citep{degroot1974consensus}; later work shows how
the same dynamics let social power concentrate on particular nodes across
repeated interactions \citep{jia2015opinion}. Our agents differ from the classical
setting in one important respect: they hold a latent, continuous confidence but
cast a discrete vote. This continuous-opinion, discrete-action structure is
itself a studied variant of opinion dynamics \citep{martins2008coda}, and it is
what lets us measure self-reinforcement on the continuous channel (confidence)
while detecting cycles and attractors on the discrete channel (votes).

\subsection{Large Language Models and Agents}
\label{sec:bg:llm}

A large language model (LLM) is a neural network, in current practice a
Transformer, trained to predict the next token in a sequence and subsequently
aligned to follow instructions through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement
learning from human feedback \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt}. The result is a model
that maps a textual prompt to a distribution over continuations, from which text
is sampled. Prompting the model to produce intermediate reasoning steps before an
answer, \emph{chain-of-thought} prompting, substantially improves performance on
multi-step reasoning tasks \citep{wei2022chainofthought, kojima2022zeroshot}, and
modern instruction-tuned models such as those in the GPT-4 family are capable of
following structured, multi-part instructions of the kind our protocol requires
\citep{achiam2023gpt4}.

An \emph{LLM agent}, in the sense used here, is an LLM placed in a loop: it is
given a state (the task, its own prior position, and messages from other agents),
it produces an action (a written argument and a vote), and the resulting state is
fed back to it on the next step. Sampling is stochastic: at a positive
temperature the model does not return a single deterministic answer but samples
from its output distribution, so two agents given the identical prompt can
produce different responses. This stochasticity is the source of the initial
diversity that multi-agent debate relies on, and, as later chapters show, it is
also what makes repeated runs of the \emph{same} task on the \emph{same}
configuration land on different outcomes, the raw material for a bistability
analysis. Because we use a single model family throughout, all four agents share
the same priors and the same competence; any diversity in the group is a product
of sampling, not of heterogeneous models. This is a deliberate design choice
(\autoref{sec:exp}) that isolates the dynamics of a \emph{homogeneous} collective,
and it is also a scope limitation we return to in \autoref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Debate}
\label{sec:bg:mad}

\emph{Multi-agent debate} (MAD) is a protocol in which several LLM agents
independently propose an answer to a question and then exchange and revise their
reasoning over multiple rounds before the group commits to a final decision
\citep{du2024improving}. The motivating intuition is an error-correction argument:
if agents make partially independent mistakes, deliberation gives the group a
chance to surface and correct them, much as an ensemble of independent voters can
outperform its average member. Reported gains on factual and mathematical
reasoning \citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, chen2024reconcile}, and
the use of debate panels as more reliable evaluators \citep{chan2024chateval},
follow from this idea.

Debate designs vary along a few structural axes that recur throughout this thesis.
The \emph{communication topology} fixes who can read whom: a fully-connected group
in which every agent sees every other, versus sparser arrangements such as a star,
in which spokes communicate only through a central hub. Sparser topologies can
match fully-connected debate at lower token cost \citep{li2024sparsetopology}, so
topology is a genuine design decision rather than a cosmetic one. The
\emph{memory window} fixes how much interaction history each agent retains from
one round to the next. The \emph{decision protocol} fixes how individual positions
are aggregated into a group answer, and whether the group is asked to vote or to
reach explicit consensus; this choice alone can change which tasks benefit from
debate \citep{kaesberg2025voting}. We treat topology and memory window as
controlled experimental axes and hold the protocol fixed.

Crucially for this thesis, the same interaction that enables error correction also
admits systematic failure. A purely cooperative framing can collapse the debate
into mutual agreement that entrenches an initial mistake, the
\emph{Degeneration-of-Thought} failure mode \citep{liang2024encouraging}. When two
models debate, both tend to grow more confident that they are winning as the
exchange proceeds, even absent new evidence \citep{prasad2025certain}, and agents
frequently abandon a correct answer to conform to a confident peer
\citep{wynn2025talk}. When the information needed for the correct answer is
distributed across agents rather than shared, groups lock onto the shared evidence
and fail to pool the private pieces, so collective accuracy collapses far below
what a single fully-informed agent achieves \citep{li2026hiddenbench}; this is the
LLM analogue of the hidden-profile effect long documented in human groups
\citep{stasser1985pooling}. These pathologies are not accidents of a particular
prompt but properties of \emph{how positions evolve and influence one another over
rounds}, that is, they are dynamical. They are precisely the behaviours the
motif vocabulary of \autoref{sec:bg:complex} is designed to name, and
providing an empirical instrument for detecting them is the task of the following
chapters.
