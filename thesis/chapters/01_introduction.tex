\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed not as isolated
predictors but as interacting agents that reason, argue, and decide in groups.
A prominent instance of this shift is \emph{multi-agent debate} (MAD), in which
several LLM instances independently propose answers, exchange their reasoning
over multiple rounds, and revise their positions before the group commits to a
final decision \citep{du2024improving}. Across factual and mathematical
reasoning tasks, this simple protocol has been reported to improve accuracy and
reduce hallucination relative to a single model answering alone
\citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, chen2024reconcile}, and it now
underpins a growing family of collaborative and evaluative systems
\citep{chan2024chateval}. The appeal is intuitive: independent agents make
partially independent errors, and deliberation offers a mechanism for the group
to identify and correct them.

This optimistic reading, however, rests almost entirely on \emph{outcome}
measurements. The literature evaluates multi-agent systems predominantly by
final task performance, treating the deliberation itself as an opaque process
that either does or does not yield a better answer. Recent critical
reassessments show that this outcome-centric view is fragile: on saturated
benchmarks, debate frequently fails to outperform a single chain-of-thought
baseline \citep{liu2025stopovervaluing, zhang2025debateorvote}. More
troublingly, the failures that do occur are not random noise but exhibit
structure. When two LLMs debate, both tend to grow \emph{more} confident that
they have won as the exchange proceeds, with confidence escalating away from a
rational baseline even when no new supporting evidence is introduced
\citep{prasad2025certain}. Under distributed information, groups of LLM agents
converge prematurely on the evidence they already share and fail to surface the
private knowledge needed for the correct answer, so that collective accuracy
collapses far below what a single agent achieves with complete information
\citep{li2026hiddenbench}. Purely cooperative framings can further collapse the
debate into a mutually agreeing echo chamber, a failure mode termed
Degeneration-of-Thought \citep{liang2024encouraging}.

What these pathologies have in common is that they are \emph{dynamical}. Rising
confidence, premature lock-in, and conformity cascades are properties of how
positions evolve and influence one another across rounds, not of any single
agent's competence at a single point in time. An outcome metric, by
construction, cannot see them: two systems can reach the same final accuracy
through entirely different trajectories, one through genuine error correction
and the other through a confident slide into a shared mistake. Understanding
\emph{when} and \emph{why} multi-agent deliberation succeeds therefore requires
a vocabulary for the interaction dynamics themselves. \citet{miehling2025agentic}
make precisely this argument at a conceptual level, contending that agentic AI
must be studied through a systems-theoretic lens because emergent group-level
behaviour cannot be predicted from individual agent properties alone. Their
position, however, remains programmatic and offers no empirical instrument for
detecting such behaviour in practice.

Complex-systems theory supplies exactly such an instrument. Systems of many
interacting units that produce ordered collective behaviour without central
control, the defining feature of self-organization \citep{ashby1947principles},
are known to fall into a small number of recurring dynamical regimes. These
regimes have precise names and precise signatures: \emph{self-reinforcement},
the positive feedback by which a state amplifies its own persistence
\citep{arthur1989competing}; \emph{limit cycles}, self-sustained periodic
oscillations that never settle \citep{strogatz2015nonlinear};
\emph{bistability}, the coexistence of multiple stable outcomes selected by
initial conditions \citep{feudel2008complex}; and \emph{dominance}, the concentration of influence onto a
single node. Such patterns are studied as \emph{motifs}, recurring structural or
dynamical building blocks whose presence in a system is both detectable and
diagnostic of its behaviour \citep{milo2002network, alon2007network,
driscoll2024dynamical}. The pathologies documented in the MAD literature read
naturally as instances of this vocabulary: escalating confidence is a
self-reinforcement signature, premature consensus is a fixed-point attractor
reached through basin selection, and herding to a confident leader is a
dominance motif. Yet, to our knowledge, these connections have never been made
empirically. The motifs are invoked informally, if at all, and no work has
asked whether they can be \emph{measured} in LLM-based multi-agent systems, nor
under what conditions they arise.

This thesis addresses that gap. We construct a controlled cooperative
multi-agent debate system in which homogeneous LLM agents deliberate over
multiple rounds on tasks with verifiable ground truth, and we develop a suite of
detectors, each grounded in the complex-systems and opinion-dynamics literature,
that identify self-reinforcement, limit cycles, bistability, and dominance in
the resulting interaction logs. We run this system across a controlled grid of
communication topologies and memory configurations on two complementary
benchmarks: GPQA-Diamond \citep{rein2024gpqa}, a graduate-level science
question-answering benchmark where agents typically start with reasonable priors,
and HiddenBench \citep{li2026hiddenbench}, a hidden-profile benchmark
constructed so that agents start systematically wrong and success requires
genuine information pooling. The contrast between the two isolates whether
observed dynamics depend on the presence of a reliable individual signal or
persist even when the group must correct a shared bias. Throughout, we treat
motif detection not as a competitor to accuracy but as an explanatory layer
beneath it: a way to characterise the trajectory that produces an outcome, and
thereby to understand which design choices shape the deliberative dynamics of
LLM collectives.

This explanatory layer has a practical purpose. As multi-agent systems are
deployed in consequential settings, an operator needs to know not only whether a
configuration tends to produce correct answers but whether a given deliberation
is proceeding soundly or pathologically, whether the group is genuinely
correcting an error or confidently converging on a shared mistake, and whether it
will terminate at all. Outcome metrics cannot answer these questions, and no
lightweight instrument for them currently exists \citep{miehling2025agentic}. A
central thread of this thesis is therefore that each motif detector, being
computed from ordinary interaction logs, doubles as a candidate \emph{diagnostic}
of deliberative health. We do not deliver a validated diagnostic tool; we deliver
the empirical groundwork for one, a set of measurable signatures, characterised
across a controlled grid, together with tiered, evidence-graded design principles
that state honestly how far each is supported by our data.

In one sentence, the objective of this thesis is to understand \emph{how} a
cooperative LLM debate arrives at its answer, by measuring the self-organization
patterns in its trajectory, and to turn that understanding into evidence-graded
guidance for building such systems.


\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro:rqs}

The overarching goal of this thesis is to establish whether the dynamical
vocabulary of complex systems can be applied empirically to LLM-based
multi-agent debate, and to characterise the conditions under which
self-organization patterns emerge. We decompose this goal into four research
questions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{Detectability.} Can self-organization motifs,
        specifically self-reinforcement, limit cycles, bistability, and
        dominance, be detected empirically in the interaction dynamics of a
        cooperative LLM-based multi-agent debate system, using detectors
        grounded in the complex-systems literature rather than in ad hoc
        heuristics?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{Conditions of emergence.} Under what system
        configurations do these motifs arise or disappear? We examine the two
        canonical structural axes of multi-agent design, the communication
        topology (fully connected versus star) and the memory window governing
        how much interaction history each agent retains, together with the
        difficulty regime of the underlying task.

  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{Relation to outcomes.} How does the presence and
        strength of each motif relate to system-level outcomes, in particular
        accuracy and convergence efficiency? We ask whether motifs serve as
        diagnostic indicators of when deliberation helps, when it merely
        entrenches an initial disposition, and when it fails to terminate at
        all.

  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] \textbf{The role of initial conditions.} To what extent is
        the outcome of the debate determined by the composition of the votes
        \emph{before} deliberation begins, and can this be established causally
        rather than observationally? We address this both observationally, through
        the ground-truth-presence analysis, and causally, through a seeded
        initial-condition experiment that manipulates only the opening vote profile.
\end{itemize}

A recurring methodological commitment cuts across all four questions. Because
fast, homogeneous convergence is expected to suppress most of these dynamics, we
treat the \emph{absence} of a motif as an informative result rather than a
failed measurement, and we compare every detector against a principled null
model so that a positive finding reflects genuine structure rather than the
chance regularities of a small, discrete state space.


\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A formalised cooperative debate protocol as a measurement
        instrument.} We define a two-phase synchronous multi-agent debate
        protocol with an explicit separation of public messages from private
        beliefs and confidences, designed so that the interaction logs expose
        precisely the quantities that motif detection requires (Section
        \ref{sec:exp}). Topology and memory window are exposed as controlled
        perturbation axes, and a difficulty-based screening procedure focuses
        the compute budget on the tasks where non-trivial dynamics can occur.

  \item \textbf{\suitename: a suite of four motif detectors.} We develop and
        formally specify \suitename{} (Self-Organization Motif Analysis), a suite
        of literature-grounded detectors for self-reinforcement (a slope test on
        within-position confidence trajectories against a sign-test null), limit
        cycles (categorical recurrence quantification analysis with a
        surrogate-data null and a period guard), bistability (an effective-number
        -of-attractors measure paired with a permutation test for
        initial-condition dependence), and dominance (a normalized influence-
        concentration index with a topology-aware null). Each detector states
        explicitly what it can and cannot claim, distinguishing a phenomenological
        signature from a certified attractor (Section \ref{sec:method}).

  \item \textbf{An empirical characterization across topology, memory, and task
        difficulty.} Running the full grid on GPQA-Diamond and HiddenBench, we
        show that self-reinforcement is pervasive across all configurations,
        that genuine limit cycles are rare and arise almost exclusively under the
        star topology through a hub-driven mechanism, and that bistability and
        dominance concentrate in the regimes predicted by the structural design
        (Section \ref{sec:results}).

  \item \textbf{A deflationary reframing of when debate helps.} By contrasting a
        refinement regime (GPQA-Diamond, where agents mostly start correct) with
        a correction regime (HiddenBench, where they start systematically wrong),
        we show that the relationship between motif strength and accuracy depends
        on the reliability of the opening vote distribution, and that the
        structural design axes we varied do not move accuracy. This positions the
        detected motifs as explanations for outcome variability rather than as
        guarantees of improvement (Sections \ref{sec:results} and \ref{sec:discussion}).

  \item \textbf{A causal test of the initial-condition effect.} Beyond the
        observational analyses, we run a controlled seeded-initialisation
        experiment that manipulates only the composition of the opening votes,
        holding the debate itself unchanged. Inserting a single correct agent into
        an otherwise-incorrect opening raises accuracy roughly thirteen-fold and in
        every task tested, while the system almost never recovers a correct answer
        that no agent initially held. This upgrades the central claim from
        association to intervention: the opening vote distribution, not the length
        or intensity of debate, is the dominant lever on the outcome
        (Section \ref{sec:results}).

  \item \textbf{Design principles and diagnostic groundwork for multi-agent
        systems.} From the above we derive evidence-based guidance, graded by how
        strongly our data support each claim, on the effects of topology and memory
        on convergence speed, worst-case runtime, and the entrenchment of dominant
        agents, including the observation that a hard round cap is a necessary
        safeguard because only certain topologies admit non-terminating oscillatory
        debates. We further show that each \suitename{} detector, computed from
        ordinary interaction logs, can serve as an instrument for monitoring
        deliberative health, and we lay out the mapping from principle to
        measurement as groundwork for a diagnostic suite, built on these detectors,
        that we do not ourselves package into a tool
        (Section \ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Structure of this thesis.}
\autoref{sec:infsub} reviews the background the work draws on, self-organization
and dynamical motifs in complex systems and the current state of LLM-based
multi-agent debate. \autoref{sec:related} surveys related work and the ways
multi-agent systems are presently analysed, and locates the research gap this
thesis addresses. \autoref{sec:exp} specifies the debate system as a measurement
instrument: the notation and system macrostate, the two-phase protocol and
stopping rule, the two deliberately contrasting benchmarks, and the experimental
grid. \autoref{sec:method} defines the four \suitename{} detectors and the
principled null models each is tested against, together with the statistical
conventions used throughout. \autoref{sec:results} reports the empirical
characterization: system performance, the four motifs in turn, the
high-repetition and seeded-initialisation experiments, and a synthesis arguing
that the motifs are four faces of one amplifier mechanism. \autoref{sec:discussion}
interprets these findings and distils them into tiered, evidence-graded design
principles. \autoref{sec:limitations} states the scope and methodological limits of
the study and the research agenda they imply. \autoref{sec:conclusion} summarises
the answers to the four research questions.
