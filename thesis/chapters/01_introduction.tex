\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}

Large language models (\acsp{LLM})\acused{LLM} are increasingly used not as isolated
models but as interacting agents that reason, argue, and decide in groups.
A prominent instance of this is \emph{multi-agent debate} (\acs{MAD})\acused{MAD}, in which
several \ac{LLM} instances independently propose answers, exchange their reasoning
over multiple rounds, and revise their positions before the group ends up with a 
final decision \citep{du2024improving}. Across factual and mathematical
reasoning tasks, this simple protocol has been reported to improve accuracy
relative to a single model answering alone
\citep{du2024improving, liang2024encouraging, chen2024reconcile} and to reduce
hallucination \citep{du2024improving}, and it now
supports a growing family of collaborative and evaluative systems
\citep{chan2024chateval}. And the appeal is intuitive: independent agents make
partially independent errors, and deliberation offers a mechanism for the group
to identify and correct them.

However, this optimistic reading is almost entirely based on \emph{outcome}
measurements. The literature evaluates \acp{MAS} mostly by
final task performance, treating the deliberation itself as a mysterious process
that either does or does not yield a better answer. Recent critical
reassessments show that this outcome-centric view is fragile: debate frequently
fails to outperform simple single-agent baselines such as chain-of-thought and
self-consistency \citep{liu2025stopovervaluing}, and does not reliably beat
simpler alternatives such as majority voting
\citep{zhang2025debateorvote} or isolated self-correction
\citep{bertalanic2026costofconsensus}.
More troublingly, the failures that do occur are not random noise but exhibit
structure. When two \acp{LLM} debate, both tend to grow \emph{more} confident that
they have won as the exchange proceeds, with confidence escalating away from a
rational baseline even in the face of opposing arguments
\citep{prasad2025certain}. Under distributed information, groups of \ac{LLM} agents
converge prematurely on the evidence they already share and fail to surface the
private knowledge needed for the correct answer, so that collective accuracy
collapses far below what a single agent achieves with complete information
\citep{li2026hiddenbench}.

What these failures have in common is that they are \emph{dynamical}. Rising
confidence, premature lock-in, and conformity behaviour are properties of how
positions evolve and influence one another across rounds, and not of any single
agent's competence at a single point in time. An outcome metric, by
construction, cannot see them: two systems can reach the same final accuracy
through entirely different trajectories, one through genuine error correction
and the other through a confident slide into a shared mistake. Understanding
\emph{when} and \emph{why} multi-agent deliberation succeeds therefore requires
a vocabulary for the interaction dynamics themselves. \citet{miehling2025agentic}
make precisely this argument at a conceptual level, stating that agentic \ac{AI}
requires a systems-theoretic lens because a focus on individual model capabilities
underestimates emergent system-level capabilities and risks. However, 
their work remains conceptual and offers no empirical instrument for
detecting such behaviour in practice.

Complex-systems theory supplies exactly such an instrument. Systems of many
interacting units that produce ordered collective behaviour without any central
control, the defining feature of self-organization \citep{camazine2001self},
are known to fall into a small number of recurring dynamical regimes. These
regimes have precise names and precise signatures. \emph{Self-reinforcement},
is the positive feedback by which a state amplifies its own position
\citep{arthur1989competing}; \emph{limit cycles} are self-sustained periodic
oscillations that never settle \citep{strogatz2015nonlinear};
\emph{multistability} is the coexistence of multiple stable outcomes selected by
initial conditions \citep{feudel2008complex}; and \emph{dominance} is the
concentration of influence onto a single node \citep{degroot1974consensus,
jia2015opinion}. Such patterns are studied as \emph{motifs}, which are recurring structural or
dynamical building blocks whose presence is detectable and informative about a system's behaviour \citep{milo2002network, alon2007network,
driscoll2024dynamical}. The failure modes documented in the \ac{MAD} literature read
naturally as instances of this vocabulary. For instance, escalating confidence is a
self-reinforcement signature, premature consensus is a fixed-point attractor
reached through basin selection, and herding to a confident leader is a
dominance motif. Thus, the connection has begun to be made.
\citet{ashery2025conventions} demonstrate the spontaneous emergence of
universally adopted social conventions in decentralised \ac{LLM} populations, show
that strong collective biases arise even when individual agents exhibit none,
and find that committed adversarial minorities can impose alternative
conventions on the majority. \citet{okawa2026biasedconsensus} derives a formal model predicting that once
conformity pressure among agents exceeds a critical threshold, even small
individual biases are amplified into a shared collective bias.
However, these studies mainly use coordination tasks in which no option is correct.
They can therefore show that a group converges, but not whether it converges on the correct answer.

This thesis addresses that gap. We construct a controlled cooperative
\ac{MAD} system in which homogeneous \ac{LLM} agents deliberate over
multiple rounds on tasks with verifiable ground truth. On top of this system, we
develop a suite of detectors, each grounded in the complex-systems and
opinion-dynamics literature, that identify self-reinforcement, limit cycles,
multistability, and dominance in the resulting interaction logs. We run this system across a controlled grid of
communication topologies and memory configurations on two complementary
benchmarks: GPQA-Diamond \citep{rein2024gpqa}, a graduate-level science
question-answering benchmark where agents typically start with above-chance priors,
and HiddenBench \citep{li2026hiddenbench}, a hidden-profile benchmark
constructed so that agents start systematically wrong and success requires
genuine information pooling. The contrast between the two isolates whether
observed dynamics depend on the presence of a reliable individual signal or
persist even when the group must correct a shared bias. Throughout, we treat
motif detection not as a competitor to accuracy but as an explanatory layer
beneath it. We think of it as a way to characterise the trajectory that produces an outcome, and
thereby use it to understand which design choices shape the deliberative dynamics of
\ac{LLM} collectives.

This explanatory layer has a practical purpose. As \acp{MAS} are
deployed in high-risk environments, an operator needs to know not only whether a
configuration tends to produce correct answers but also whether a given deliberation
is proceeding soundly or in a broken way, whether the group is genuinely
correcting an error or just confidently converging on a shared mistake, and whether it
will terminate at all. Outcome metrics cannot answer these questions alone.
Related work has started to address this: \citet{fan2026imad} train a classifier
that predicts, from linguistic features of a single agent's self-critique,
whether debate is worth running at all. What is still missing is a tool that names which regime a deliberation
is in, using the vocabulary of dynamical systems, rather than only flagging that
something is wrong. Our detectors are meant to fill this gap. Because each detector is computed from
ordinary interaction logs, it can serve as a candidate \emph{diagnostic} of
deliberative health. We do not deliver a validated diagnostic tool. Instead,
we provide the groundwork for one, a set of measurable signatures
characterised across a controlled grid, together with design principles graded by
how strongly our data supports them.
In one sentence, the objective of this thesis is to understand \emph{how} a
cooperative \ac{LLM} debate arrives at its answer, by measuring the self-organization
patterns in its trajectory, and to turn that understanding into evidence-based
guidance for building such \ac{MAD} systems.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro:rqs}

The goal of this thesis is to establish whether the dynamical
vocabulary of complex systems can be applied empirically to \ac{LLM}-based
\ac{MAD}, and to characterise the conditions under which
self-organization patterns emerge. We split this goal into three research
questions, ordered by the class of evidence each requires.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{Measurement.} Can self-organization motifs,
        specifically self-reinforcement, limit cycles, multistability, and
        dominance, be detected in cooperative \ac{LLM}-based \ac{MAD} using
        detectors grounded in the complex-systems and opinion-dynamics literature? 
        And which of these motifs actually occur, at what frequency, and with what structure?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{Characterisation.} What determines the presence and
        strength of each motif, and what do they predict about system outcomes?
        We examine two structural axes, communication topology and
        memory window, together with the information regime of the task and the
        composition of the opening votes, and ask what each motif implies for
        accuracy, convergence speed, and worst-case runtime.

  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{Causation.} Is the composition of the opening votes
        causally responsible for the outcome? Can the system generate a correct
        answer that no agent initially held, or does deliberation function
        primarily as an amplifier of the initial vote distribution?
\end{itemize}

Throughout, every detector is tested against a null
model that removes the pattern we look for and keeps everything else. This
matters because our setup is small. With only four agents and three or four voting options,
votes repeat and influence concentrates by chance alone. Thus, the null models show
what chance looks like, so a motif with a very low frequency is still a result,
not a failure. We report the motifs we do not find as well as those we do.

\subsection{Contributions \& Structure}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This thesis makes three main contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Self-Organization Motif Analysis (\suitename{}), an instrument for measuring debate dynamics.}
        A two-phase debate protocol that logs private beliefs and confidence scores
        separately from public messages (\autoref{sec:exp}), together with four
        motif detectors, each grounded in the complex-systems or
        opinion-dynamics literature and tested against its own null model
        (\autoref{sec:method}).

  \item \textbf{A repeated-run characterisation at scale.} 30{,}000 debates in the main grid
        across twelve configurations and two contrasting
        benchmarks, with $R = 50$ repetitions per task and configuration, which
        makes across-repetition attractor statistics measurable in the first
        place, plus $14{,}250$ further debates in the follow-up experiments
        (\autoref{sec:results}).

  \item \textbf{A controlled intervention on the opening votes.} A
        seeded-initialisation experiment and an adversarial probe that
        manipulate only the composition of the opening votes while leaving the
        debate itself unchanged (\autoref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\noindent All four motifs describe a single mechanism. The debate has positive
feedback but little negative feedback, so it acts as a selective but lossy amplifier
of its opening votes. It strengthens what the agents already
hold, rarely produces an answer none of them started with, and can destroy a
correct majority. From this we derive evidence-graded design guidance, mapping
each recommendation to an observable that can be computed from ordinary
interaction logs (\autoref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Structure of this thesis.}
The thesis follows the order of the three research questions. \emph{Background}
(\autoref{sec:infsub}) introduces the vocabulary of self-organization and
dynamical motifs and fixes what we mean by an \ac{LLM} agent and \ac{MAD};
\emph{Related Work} (\autoref{sec:related}) surveys how \acp{MAS} are
currently analysed and locates the gap this thesis addresses. The instrument is
then built in two steps. Firstly, \emph{System and Experimental Setup}
(\autoref{sec:exp}) specifies the debate protocol, the two contrasting
benchmarks, and the experimental grid, and secondly, \emph{Detection Methodology}
(\autoref{sec:method}) defines the four \suitename{} detectors together with the
null model each is tested against. \emph{Results} (\autoref{sec:results}) then applies
the instrument, reporting accuracy and efficiency, the four motifs in turn, the
emergent influence topology, and the seeded-initialisation experiment.
\emph{Discussion} (\autoref{sec:discussion}) continues by interpreting the motifs as one
amplifier mechanism, traces it to the near-absence of negative feedback, and derives
evidence-based design principles. It also states the limits of our claims and
the future work they imply. \emph{Conclusion} (\autoref{sec:conclusion}) closes
by answering the three research questions in turn and showing how the
detectors, the characterisation, and the causal test support each other.
