\subsection{Benchmark Survey}
\label{app:benchmarks}

\autoref{sec:setup:datasets} requires a benchmark to supply a discrete shared
decision variable, verifiable ground truth, calibrated difficulty, and sufficient
initial disagreement. Most existing benchmarks fail the first requirement
outright. The native multi-agent suites bind agents to sub-tasks or to positions
in physical space rather than to a common set of options, so there is no shared
position space in which a vote composition could be defined. REALM-Bench
\citep{geng2025realmbench} assigns agents to specific machines and its
communication is a notification cascade rather than a deliberation; MARBLE
\citep{zhu2025multiagentbench} gives each agent a different root cause to
diagnose; PARTNR \citep{chang2024partnr} hard-codes heterogeneous capabilities and
is dyadic by design; Collab-Overcooked \citep{sun2025collaboverco} splits chef and
assistant into distinct action spaces. In BattleAgentBench
\citep{wang2024battleagentbench} an agent's state is a discrete grid position, and in SPIN-Bench \citep{yao2025spinbench} only the Diplomacy
environment approaches deliberation, where a position is a spatial supply-centre
configuration and the authors report coordination degrading sharply beyond two or
three agents.

The socially framed benchmarks come closer without quite fitting. SOTOPIA
\citep{zhou2024sotopia} is $N = 2$ by design, which is structurally fatal here,
since dominance requires at least three agents and multistability requires
competing factions. LLM-Deliberation \citep{abdelnabi2024cooperation} is genuinely
multi-agent at up to six parties, with a private scratchpad and a public answer, and native adversarial variants,
but it carries no per-agent correctness signal and its deal space is too sparse
for equality-based agreement metrics, which makes it the strongest candidate for a
future mixed-motive extension rather than a correctness benchmark here.
HiddenBench \citep{li2026hiddenbench} is the exception: it formalises the
hidden-profile paradigm \citep{stasser1985pooling, schulzhardt2012synergy} with
validity criteria that guarantee information pooling is required, and we adopt it
as our secondary substrate. Two sub-environments of MARBLE, Bargaining and
Werewolf, do have shared decision variables and could serve the same role in later
work.

The single-agent reasoning sets that the debate literature has adopted fail on
difficulty instead. \citet{du2024improving} established the methodology on GSM8K
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} and MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}, both of which are
effectively solved by frontier models \citep{achiam2023gpt4}, so the typical
trajectory is immediate unanimous agreement and there is no dynamical signal to
detect; critical reassessments confirm that debate on saturated benchmarks
frequently fails to beat single-agent baselines \citep{liu2025stopovervaluing,
zhang2025debateorvote}. MATH \citep{hendrycks2021math} retains difficulty but
requires non-trivial answer normalisation and inflates token cost through long
solutions. GPQA-Diamond \citep{rein2024gpqa} is the instance of this lineage that
still leaves headroom, which is why we adopt it as primary. Two further systems
are cited elsewhere in this thesis but were never candidates as substrates:
AgentVerse \citep{chen2024agentverse} is a framework rather than a benchmark, and
the DEBATE corpus \citep{chuang2025debate} is a corpus of human public and private
opinion dynamics used as a reference point rather than an LLM task environment.

\subsection{Per-Knob Justification}
\label{app:knobs}

\autoref{tab:knob-summary} records the verdict, the value, and the key references
for each tunable component. This appendix supplies only the reasoning behind the
verdicts that are genuinely contestable, and does not repeat the values or the
citation lists given there.

\paragraph{Memory window (scan).}
Retained memory is a design choice rather than neutral bookkeeping.
\citet{marandi2026protocols} find a real tradeoff between peer-referencing and convergence across protocol variants, with within-round versus cross-round exchange trading off directly;
\citet{wynn2025talk} document erroneous contributions persisting across rounds;
and language models use long contexts unevenly \citep{liu2024lostmiddle}. A
factorial memory$\times$topology study finds a strong interaction between the two:
memory slows settling in decentralised networks but accelerates it in centralised
ones, so topology determines the sign of the memory effect
\citep{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory}. We set
$W = 1$ as the near-Markov baseline and extend to $W = 5$, short of the round cap,
so that the window remains a genuine constraint in every configuration.

\paragraph{Number of agents (fix at $N = 4$).}
The joint answer-profile state space grows as $M^N$, so $N = 4$ is the smallest
value at which the two canonical topologies are structurally distinct and
multi-way splits can occur, while the state space stays small enough for
trajectory statistics at $R = 50$. The canonical baseline is $N = 3$
\citep{du2024improving}, with only modest degradation reported up to $N = 4$
\citep{liang2024encouraging}.

\paragraph{Number of rounds (fix at $T = 15$ with $u = 3$).}
$T$ is an observation budget rather than a structural perturbation, since
trajectories can be cropped post hoc but not extended. Accuracy-level convergence
is reported within a few rounds \citep{li2024sparsetopology}, but limit-cycle
detection requires $T \geq 2 \cdot \text{period}$ and the period is unknown a
priori, which sets the cap well above the convergence horizon. The early-stopping
threshold $u$ then trades efficiency against reliability: on the pilot repetitions
$u = 3$ saves roughly $50\,\%$ of runtime per repetition, while the harmful case,
in which stopping would have pre-empted a later recovery to the correct answer,
occurs in \fillin{harmful-stop rate at $u = 3$} of repetitions.

\paragraph{Model and sampling (fix, homogeneous, $\tau = 0.7$).}
A single homogeneous model isolates topology and memory effects from
capability-asymmetry effects, which otherwise produce dominance-like dynamics
attributable to conformity rather than to structure \citep{wynn2025talk}. Mixed
teams within one capability tier do not systematically outperform their best
member and show comparable modal sycophancy rates
\citep{bertalanic2026costofconsensus}, so heterogeneity alone would not dissolve
the dynamics under study. On temperature, deterministic decoding collapses debate
into voting among identical samples \citep{zhu2026demystifying}.

\paragraph{Framing and execution (fix).}
\citet{liang2024encouraging} locate the operational sweet spot between pure
cooperation, which lacks the adversarial pressure needed to overcome
Degeneration-of-Thought in single-model reflection, and pure adversarial
framing, which prevents convergence; the defense/challenge/question scaffold
implements modest tit-for-tat while the \updatephase{} phase leaves room for
convergence. The structured output is measurement apparatus rather than
engineering taste, since the private/public split is what makes the dissociation
observable and the vote-stability rule is what separates argumentation from belief
update. Synchronous execution with randomised peer-message order removes
agent-order effects that would otherwise confound dominance detection.

\paragraph{Tool access (defer).}
For GPQA-Diamond, tools would push single-agent accuracy toward the ceiling and
remove the dynamical headroom the study depends on, introduce non-reproducibility
through web search, and violate the Google-proof design rationale of
\citet{rein2024gpqa}.

\paragraph{Compute budget.}
Motif detection needs repetitions rather than tasks: multistability requires enough
samples per task to surface competing basins, limit cycles enough trajectories to
confirm period-$\geq 2$ structure, and the dominance surrogate test statistical
power. At $R = 50$ and $\approx 25$\,s per repetition, running the full
six-configuration grid on all 198 GPQA-Diamond tasks would require
$198 \times 6 \times 50 = 59{,}400$ repetitions, that is $\approx 412$\,h of
sequential model time. The two-phase procedure of \autoref{sec:setup:design}
reduces this by roughly a factor of four: the screening pass costs
$198 \times 3 = 594$ repetitions ($\approx 4$\,h), and the deep pass on the 50
retained tasks costs $50 \times 6 \times 50 = 15{,}000$ repetitions
($\approx 104$\,h). Including HiddenBench, the 30{,}000 repetitions reported in
\autoref{sec:results} amount to $\approx 208$\,h of sequential model time,
completed in \fillin{measured wall-clock hours} under parallel execution.

\subsection{Prompt Templates}
\label{app:prompts}

For reproducibility we reproduce the core prompt components verbatim. The shared
persona (with \texttt{\{name\}} the agent identifier) frames the task and the
two-phase protocol:

\begin{prompt}[H]
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are \{name\}, part of a collaborative group working on a multiple-choice
problem. Your goal is to help the group find the correct answer, agreement should
follow from better arguments, not the other way around.

Each round (after round 0) has two phases:

\ Phase A: output three structured fields, defense (a new concrete reason your
current vote is correct), challenge (dispute a specific current peer claim, or
concede it and name any remaining disagreement), question (a targeted question to a
named peer referencing a concrete claim). Do not change your vote in this phase.

\ Phase B: you see your neighbors' Phase A outputs. Produce your updated vote,
private reasoning, and public message.

Be critical, your neighbors may be wrong, and so may you. Do not change your
position simply because others disagree; only update if you encounter a specific
argument you cannot counter.
\end{quote}
\caption[Shared agent persona (system prompt)]{Shared agent persona (system prompt): the two-phase protocol and the
anti-conformity instruction, with \texttt{\{name\}} the agent identifier.}
\label{prompt:persona}
\end{prompt}

The three per-step instructions appended to the persona and the running context
are:

\begin{prompt}[H]
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Round 0: Carefully read the question and options. Produce your initial vote,
private reasoning, and public message.

Phase A: Produce your Phase A structured output. Do not change your vote in this
phase.

Phase B: Update your belief, private reasoning, and public message. If you change
your vote, cite the specific argument, from the current Phase A drafts or from the
conversation history, that caused the update.
\end{quote}
\caption[Per-step agent instructions by phase]{Per-step instructions appended to the persona (round~0, \arguephase{} phase, and
\updatephase{} phase), controlling what each agent is asked to produce at each step.}
\label{prompt:steps}
\end{prompt}

In the \updatephase{} phase each agent additionally receives its own \arguephase{}
draft and its neighbours' \arguephase{} drafts in randomised order, truncated to
the memory window $W$; confidence is elicited as an integer from $1$ to $10$. The
complete prompt-assembly code is in the electronic appendix.

For the adversarial probe (\autoref{sec:results:evid}), the devil's advocate agent
receives a different system prompt in place of \autoref{prompt:persona}:

\begin{prompt}[H]
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are \{name\}, a committed advocate participating in a group deliberation.
Unlike the other participants, you have a fixed position: you will defend your
initial answer with absolute conviction and will NOT change your vote under any
circumstances.

Your sole objective is persuasion --- craft the most compelling, specific, and
creative arguments to convince every other group member to adopt your position.
Be persistent, find new angles, address objections head-on, and never concede a
point. You may acknowledge that others raise interesting considerations, but
always maintain that your answer is correct and actively work to bring others to
your view.

Each round has two phases:

\ Phase A: produce a defense (a new, specific reason your answer is correct),
a challenge (directly refute a peer's claim), and a question (targeted at a
peer's weak point).

\ Phase B: write a persuasive public message. Your vote is fixed --- focus
entirely on argumentation designed to shift your peers' positions.
\end{quote}
\caption[Devil's advocate persona (system prompt)]{Devil's advocate persona replacing
\autoref{prompt:persona} for the adversarial probe: the agent's vote is locked
and its sole goal is persuasion.}
\label{prompt:devil-persona}
\end{prompt}

The devil's advocate uses \autoref{prompt:steps} unchanged for Phase~A but
receives a different \updatephase{} instruction in place of the standard one:

\begin{prompt}[H]
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Your vote is locked and must NOT change. Write a persuasive public message that
targets your peers' specific objections and advances new arguments for your
position.
\end{quote}
\caption[Devil's advocate \updatephase{} instruction]{Devil's advocate \updatephase{}
instruction replacing the standard Phase~B entry in \autoref{prompt:steps}: the
vote cannot change; the agent focuses solely on persuasion.}
\label{prompt:devil-steps}
\end{prompt}

The two benchmark question contexts take different forms. For GPQA-Diamond all
agents receive the same question text and option labels (options are shuffled
uniformly at random before assignment):

\begin{prompt}[H]
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Question:
\{question\}

Options:
A: \{option A\}
B: \{option B\}
C: \{option C\}
D: \{option D\}

Reason through the question step by step, then select the correct answer.
\end{quote}
\caption[GPQA question context]{GPQA-Diamond question context: identical across all
agents in a repetition. Options are shuffled uniformly before label assignment to
decorrelate answer-letter position from correctness.}
\label{prompt:gpqa-question}
\end{prompt}

For HiddenBench each agent receives the shared scenario description together with
its own private subset of the hidden facts (shared facts plus a disjoint subset of
hidden facts, randomly shuffled before presentation):

\begin{prompt}[H]
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Scenario:
\{description\}

Information:
1. \{fact 1\}
2. \{fact 2\}
...

Options:
A: \{option A\}
B: \{option B\}
...

Reason through the scenario carefully, then select the correct answer.
\end{quote}
\caption[HiddenBench question context]{HiddenBench question context: agents share
the scenario description and shared facts but each receive a disjoint subset of
the hidden facts, implementing the hidden-profile paradigm. Facts are randomly
shuffled before numbering; options are shuffled before label assignment.}
\label{prompt:hiddenbench-question}
\end{prompt}

\subsection{Self-Reinforcement: Segment-Slope Breakdown}
\label{app:sr:supp}

Prevalence and slope are stable across configurations. Over $W \in \{1,2,5\}$,
$p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ ranges from $0.763$ to $0.825$ on GPQA fully-connected, $0.733$
to $0.809$ on GPQA star, $0.839$ to $0.881$ on HiddenBench fully-connected, and
$0.804$ to $0.844$ on HiddenBench star, with mean segment slopes $\bar\beta$
between $0.350$ and $0.515$ confidence points per round. No configuration
approaches the no-feedback null of $0.5$.

What the slope does respond to is structural, and \autoref{tab:sr:subgroups} gives
the breakdown. Segments that reach the confidence ceiling, that run to the end of
their repetition, and that sit inside fully static repetitions all carry markedly
steeper slopes, and $\bar\beta$ falls monotonically as the opening vote
distribution becomes more fragmented. The ceiling and terminality rows also bound
the interpretation of the headline prevalence: a segment ends when its agent flips,
and an agent whose confidence is falling is more likely to flip, so negative slopes
are censored and $p_{\mathrm{SR}}$ should be read as an upper estimate.

At the task level the marginal relationship between $\bar\beta$ and accuracy is
near zero on GPQA (Pearson $r = 0.02$ fully-connected, $r = 0.04$ star) and weakly
negative on HiddenBench ($r = -0.21$ and $r = -0.31$). As discussed in
\autoref{sec:results:sr}, this marginal figure hides a cancellation between
opposite effects in the GT-present and GT-absent subpopulations, which is why
self-reinforcement is reported conditioned on the opening composition rather than
marginally.

\subsection{Limit Cycles: Detection Funnel and Recurrence Matrices}
\label{app:lc:supp}

\autoref{tab:lc:funnel} gives the full limit-cycle detection funnel referenced in
\autoref{sec:results:lc}. Because star spokes driven by a common hub are not
independent successes, prevalence is assessed at the repetition level. The
detection rule combines a $p < 0.05$ threshold with the period guard
$2 \leq \hat{P} \leq \lfloor L/2 \rfloor$, so its false-positive floor lies below
the nominal $5\,\%$: measured against the shuffle surrogates it is
\fillin{measured null flag rate, agent level} at the agent level and
\fillin{measured null flag rate, system level} at the system level, against
observed conditional rates of $0.074$ and $0.083$. The excess is modest in
absolute terms, which is why the claim rests jointly on it and on visual
inspection: \autoref{fig:lc:rec} shows that the flagged cases carry the
checkerboard recurrence signature of a genuine period-2 orbit rather than the
solid block a merely persistent sequence would produce.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrcc}
\toprule

\subsection{Motifs Beyond the Present Selection}
\label{app:future_motifs}

\autoref{sec:method:vocab} restricts the study to four motifs. The same
structural/dynamical lens admits several others that we leave to future work. On
the structural side, in the emergent influence graph:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mutual-reinforcement dyad} (a two-node positive loop): two agents
        that repeatedly push each other the same way.
  \item \textbf{Influence feed-forward loop}: $A$ sways $B$, $B$ sways $C$, and
        $A$ also sways $C$ directly, predicting delayed or robust lock-in.
  \item \textbf{Cascade / chain}: sequential adoption $A \to B \to C$, the
        structural signature of herding.
\end{itemize}
On the dynamical side, further regimes of the joint trajectory:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Consensus / fixed point}: the baseline regime (a point attractor)
        against which the others are defined.
  \item \textbf{Polarisation / stable split}: persistent within-repetition
        disagreement into stable subgroups, distinct from across-repetition
        multistability.
  \item \textbf{Negative autoregulation / self-correction}: the sign-flipped twin
        of self-reinforcement, an agent that damps its own confidence. Since
        \autoref{sec:results:synthesis} identifies the absence of negative
        feedback as the mechanism behind the amplifier, this is the most
        immediately consequential of the six.
\end{itemize}
These span a coherent picture of the regimes a small interacting system can fall
into; a study must start somewhere, so we treat them as directions for future
investigation.
