Each of the four motifs introduced in \autoref{sec:bg:complex} is a hypothesis
about the debate dynamics: if the system self-organizes in the way a
complex-systems reading predicts, then confidence should reinforce within a held
position, repeated repetitions should settle into a small number of initial-condition-selected attractors, influence should concentrate on particular agents, and in some topologies vote sequences may oscillate persistently without converging. This section turns each hypothesis into a
\emph{detector}: a statistic computed from the interaction log together with a
principled null model against which it is tested, so that a positive result
reflects genuine structure rather than the chance regularities of a small, discrete
state space. Together the four detectors form \suitename{} (Self-Organization
Motif Analysis), the detector suite this thesis contributes. Two properties are common to all four and are worth stating up front.
Each detector reads only from ordinary interaction logs, the vote and confidence
channels defined in \autoref{sec:exp}, which is what later lets us propose the
same detectors as diagnostic instruments (\autoref{sec:discussion:design}). And
each detects a \emph{signature}, not a certified dynamical object; every
``Limitations'' paragraph below makes explicit what the corresponding statistic can
and cannot claim, a distinction we carry into the interpretation of every result.

% 5.1 SYSTEM MACROSTATE AND MOTIF VOCABULARY
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System Macrostate and Motif Vocabulary}
\label{sec:method:vocab}

We detect four self-organization motifs in the debate logs. Before defining them
individually, this section fixes the two ideas they share: what we mean by a
\emph{motif}, and the \emph{macrostate} the system-level motifs are read from.

\paragraph{Two notions of motif.}
We use the word \emph{motif} in two complementary senses and are explicit about
which we mean. A \textbf{structural motif}, in the original sense of
\citet{milo2002network} and \citet{alon2007network}, is a recurring \emph{wiring}
pattern: a small subgraph of who-influences-whom that occurs more often than in a
randomised network. It is a statement about interaction structure and can live at
the node level (positive autoregulation, a self-loop) or span several nodes
(hubs, feed-forward loops). A \textbf{dynamical motif}
\citep{driscoll2024dynamical} is instead a recurring pattern in the
\emph{trajectory}, such as an attractor, a sustained oscillation, or a separatrix
between basins; it is a statement about system behaviour rather than wiring. The
two are linked, since a structural motif is studied precisely because it generates
a characteristic dynamical regime, and they map onto the two levels at which our
phenomena live, the agent (node) level and the system level.

\paragraph{The four motifs.}
We do not enumerate motifs exhaustively but preselect a small set, each motivated
by a documented failure mode of LLM-based debate and spanning both senses and
both levels:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Self-reinforcement} (agent level): node-level positive feedback,
        also a structural motif in the strict sense (positive autoregulation).
  \item \textbf{Bistability} (system level): a dynamical motif (coexisting
        attractors) selected by initial conditions.
  \item \textbf{Dominance} (system level): a structural motif in the
        \emph{emergent} influence graph (a concentration of directed influence, an
        emergent hub), keyed to topology.
  \item \textbf{Limit cycle} (agent and system level): a dynamical motif (a
        periodic orbit), read both as individual non-convergence and as cycling of
        the collective configuration.
\end{itemize}
A broader list of motifs that the same lens admits but that we leave to future
work is given in \autoref{app:future_motifs}.

\paragraph{System macrostate.}
The system-level motifs are read off a single agent-anonymised macrostate. As
introduced in \autoref{sec:setup:notation}, the macrostate at round $t$ is the
vote composition
\begin{equation}
  s_t = (n_1^t, n_2^t, \ldots, n_M^t), \qquad \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{M} n_k^t = N,
\end{equation}
where $n_k^t$ is the number of agents on option $k$. Agents are exchangeable, so
only their counts enter; the options stay labelled, since one is correct and they
are not interchangeable. The composition retains what a plain set of options would
discard: a $3$--$1$ split and a $2$--$2$ split are distinct macrostates. Every
option-indexed quantity below ranges over the task's actual $M$ options, so an
option the task does not present can never be voted for or become an attractor.
Bistability is read from the locked-in endpoint; dominance is relational, built on the emergent influence graph, and requires within-repetition agent identity; the system-level limit-cycle test operates on the full sequence $s_0, \ldots, s_T$.

\paragraph{Shared data hierarchy.}
All four detectors respect the same nesting of the data,
\begin{equation}
  \text{dataset} \supset \text{config } (W \times \text{topology}) \supset
  \text{task} \supset \text{repetition} \supset \text{agent},
\end{equation}
with $W \in \{1,2,5\}$, $\text{topology} \in \{\text{fc}, \text{star}\}$, $N = 4$,
and $\text{dataset} \in \{\text{GPQA}, \text{HiddenBench}\}$. Two conventions hold
throughout and are not repeated for each motif. First, the \textbf{repetition} is
the independent unit of replication: agents interact within a repetition, so their
trajectories are \emph{not} independent and must never be treated as separate data
points (pseudoreplication). Second, all analysis is \textbf{conditioned on the
config and dataset}: GPQA and HiddenBench are analysed separately and never pooled,
and the memory-window comparison is always made within a fixed topology and
dataset. Each detector below only states its own \emph{measurement primitive}, the
lowest-level object it scores.

\paragraph{Statistical conventions.}
Three conventions govern every inferential test in \autoref{sec:results}, chosen
to be simple and transparent rather than maximally powerful. (i)~\emph{The task
is the unit of analysis for comparisons across tasks or configurations.} Because
the $R$ repetitions of a cell are re-runs of the \emph{same} question, they are
not independent across tasks; wherever we test whether a quantity differs between
configurations or covaries with accuracy, we first aggregate each task to a single
value (its mean or proportion over repetitions) and test across the $\approx 35$
tasks with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test \citep{wilcoxon1945signedrank}, a Spearman correlation, a
task-blocked Friedman test \citep{friedman1937test}, or a sign test, or else resample whole tasks in a
cluster bootstrap. Repetition-level counts are reported descriptively only.
(ii)~\emph{Multiplicity is controlled per family.} Where a motif is tested across
the twelve configuration cells or several outcome variables, we control the
false-discovery rate with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure \citep{benjamini1995fdr} within each
pre-defined family and report which results survive. (iii)~\emph{Negative claims
are stated as bounded effects, not equivalence.} For the design axes that do not
move accuracy we report the effect size with a task-level confidence interval and
conclude only that it is small and contains zero, i.e.\ that we find no evidence
of a meaningful effect, rather than asserting strict statistical equivalence.

A final property is shared by all four detectors and stated once here: each claim
is \textbf{phenomenological, not causal}. We detect a trajectory or influence
\emph{signature}, not a certified attractor, cycle, or causal hub; separating the
signature from its underlying cause would require dedicated perturbation
experiments, which we leave to future work. Under the cooperative,
convergence-forced design, we also expect several detectors to return
\textbf{sparse or near-null} results; a near-null result is a valid finding, and we
compare observed prevalence against a principled null rather than against zero.

% 5.2 SELF-REINFORCEMENT
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Self-Reinforcement}
\label{sec:method:sr}

\paragraph{Concept.}
In complex systems, self-reinforcement is \emph{positive feedback}: the current
level of a state variable raises the drive toward more of the same, so deviations
are amplified rather than damped \citep{arthur1989competing}. At the motif level
this is positive autoregulation, a node that augments its own production
\citep{milo2002network, alon2007network}. For us the state variable is an agent's
expressed \emph{confidence}, and self-reinforcement is the tendency of an agent to
push its own confidence upward while it holds a position, driven by accumulating
commitment rather than by what the incoming information warrants. The pattern is
documented in the substrate: in multi-turn LLM debate, confidence tends to rise
across rounds in an anti-Bayesian fashion, even where no new information justifies
the rise \citep{prasad2025certain}. A plain upward confidence trajectory is
therefore \emph{necessary but not sufficient}, since rational updating and peer
conformity also push confidence up; the definition below targets the signatures
that go beyond ``the line goes up.'' The measure is option-agnostic, reading
confidence within a held position, so it does not depend on $M$.

\paragraph{What we measure.}
Self-reinforcement has three observable signatures. We measure the first and third
and treat the second as only partially identified:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Directional drift (measured).} While a position is held, confidence
        ratchets up more often than down.
  \item \textbf{Strength scaling (measured).} The drift grows with how strongly the
        agent is re-exposed to its own prior commitments, which the memory window
        $W$ controls directly.
  \item \textbf{Endogeneity (not isolated).} The rise is driven by the agent's own
        commitment rather than by new information; this cannot be cleanly separated
        from peer effects with trajectory data alone.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Measurement primitive: the vote-stable segment.}
The primitive is the \emph{vote-stable segment}. For agent $i$ in repetition $r$, this
is a maximal block of consecutive rounds $[t_s, t_e]$ over which the vote is
constant, $v_i^t = v_i^{t_s}$ for all $t \in \{t_s, \ldots, t_e\}$ (the vote
differs, or the trajectory ends, immediately before $t_s$ and after $t_e$). A segment
is retained only if its length $L = t_e - t_s + 1 \geq 3$, which guarantees at
least two confidence steps. An agent may contribute several segments if it flips, or a
single long segment if it locks in early. Agents are exchangeable, so segments are pooled
within a config and never matched across repetitions.

Within a retained segment we fit an ordinary-least-squares line of confidence ($c_i^t \in \{1,\ldots,10\}$, agent~$i$'s integer confidence at round~$t$) on the
round index,
\begin{equation}
  c_i^t = \beta_0 + \beta_{\mathrm{seg}}\, t + \varepsilon_t,
  \qquad t \in \{t_s, \ldots, t_e\},
\end{equation}
and keep the slope $\beta_{\mathrm{seg}}$, in units of confidence per round, which
is comparable across segments of different length without normalisation. Its sign and
magnitude are the two outputs.

\paragraph{Detection.}
We read the segments in two complementary ways, both conditioned on the config.

The \textbf{presence} test measures directional drift. For each (config, task)
pair, the prevalence of upward segments is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p}_{\mathrm{SR}}
  = \frac{\bigl|\{\text{segments}: \beta_{\mathrm{seg}} > 0\}\bigr|}{\bigl|\{\text{segments}\}\bigr|},
\end{equation}
with flat segments ($\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} = 0$) retained in the denominator. Under the
no-feedback ``wobble'' null an upward and a downward segment are equally likely, so
with a fraction $f$ of flat segments
$P(\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} > 0) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1-f) \leq 0.5$, and $0.5$ is a
conservative reference. Self-reinforcement is indicated when
$\hat{p}_{\mathrm{SR}} > 0.5$, which is a sign test against a \emph{given} baseline.
The confidence ceiling (a segment already at the top of the scale cannot drift further
up) together with the retention of flat segments makes a positive result conservative.

\paragraph{Identifying signature: $W$-scaling as a mechanism discriminator.}
Letting $\bar{\beta}$ be the mean segment slope in a config, the direction of
$W$-scaling discriminates between two candidate mechanisms.
Under an \emph{endogenous} reading, re-reading one's own prior commitments
across a longer window amplifies the internal drive, predicting
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\beta}(W{=}5) > \bar{\beta}(W{=}2) > \bar{\beta}(W{=}1).
\end{equation}
Under a \emph{peer-conformity} reading, a larger window increases the amount of
peer content visible to each agent; if peer expression moderates the agent's own
drive, the ordering may be absent or reversed.
We report the observed $W$ ordering per topology (\autoref{sec:results:sr}),
which speaks to which mechanism dominates.
Prevalence is reported per (config, task) and then summarised across tasks (for
example, ``above $0.5$ in $k$ of $35$ tasks''), so that the effect is shown to be
broad rather than driven by one task. Uncertainty is attached by a cluster
bootstrap over repetitions, stratified by task \citep{efron1979bootstrap}:
repetitions are resampled with replacement within each task and all segments of a
resampled repetition are kept together, so the within-repetition dependence is
respected; a signature is read as present when the resampled interval excludes its
baseline ($0.5$ for prevalence, $0$ for slope differences across $W$).

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In our running scenario ($N = 4$ agents, options $A$--$D$, $A$ correct), suppose agent~0 holds option~$A$ for five consecutive rounds with confidences $c = (5, 6, 6, 8, 9)$ at $t = 0,\ldots,4$. This is one vote-stable segment
of length $L = 5 \geq 3$. Regressing confidence on the round index gives a slope of
$\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} = +1.0$ confidence points per round, a positive (upward) segment
that counts toward $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{SR}}$. Had the same agent instead drifted
$c = (7, 7, 6, 6, 5)$, the slope would be $\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} = -0.5$, a downward
segment counting against it. A segment that never moved, $c = (6,6,6,6,6)$, has
$\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} = 0$ and is a flat segment: retained in the denominator but not
counted as upward, which is what makes the $0.5$ reference conservative. Under the
strength-scaling reading, self-reinforcement predicts that the average of such
slopes grows as the memory window widens from $W{=}1$ to $W{=}5$.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Beyond the phenomenological caveat stated in \autoref{sec:method:vocab}, three
points bound the claim. \emph{Endogeneity is not isolated}: an upward segment is
equally consistent with peer copying and with a peer surfacing genuinely new
information in a later round, and rising confidence alone cannot distinguish these.
\emph{The $W$ ordering provides mechanistic evidence but does not fully resolve the ambiguity}, since a larger window also re-exposes more peer content; the observed direction of the $W$ ordering informs which mechanism dominates without cleanly separating them.
\emph{Convergence is a confound}: on easier tasks fast agreement can itself raise
confidence, which is why prevalence is reported per task and never as a single
pooled figure.

% 5.3 BISTABILITY

\subsection{Bistability}
\label{sec:method:bistability}

\paragraph{Concept.}
In dynamical systems, \emph{multistability} is the coexistence of two or more
stable attractors for one fixed parameter set; which attractor the system settles
into is decided by the basin of attraction containing the initial condition
\citep{feudel2008complex, pisarchik2014control}, and bistability is the
two-attractor case. At the motif level this is the dynamical signature of positive
feedback with a symmetry: the same interaction rules admit several self-consistent
outcomes \citep{milo2002network, alon2007network}. For us the state variable is
the macrostate $s_t$, and bistability is read off the \emph{locked-in endpoint}:
for a fixed task, the debate converges to different consensus answers on different
repetitions, and which one is reached is selected by the initial vote
configuration rather than by the task's correct option. This is the structural
reading of unreliable correction: on a task where the model carries a systematic
bias, a wrong-consensus and a right-consensus attractor can coexist, and the
group's fate is basin competition decided at round $0$, which self-reinforcement
then amplifies and locks in. Seeing more than one final answer is necessary but
not sufficient, since a task may simply be noisy; genuine multistability
additionally requires that the initial condition \emph{predicts} the endpoint
\citep{pisarchik2014control}, and the definition tests both.

\paragraph{Measurement primitive and eligibility.}
The primitive and independent unit is the repetition, summarised by its initial
vote composition $s_0 = (n_1^0,\ldots,n_M^0)$ (the macrostate defined in \autoref{sec:method:vocab}) and its locked-in endpoint attractor. For repetition $r$ we take the per-round
composition $s_0, \ldots, s_T$ and let $k$ be its maximal trailing constant segment;
the repetition has \emph{settled} if $k \geq u = 3$. A settled repetition is
\textbf{converged} (eligible) only if its locked-in macrostate is
\emph{unanimous}, in which case its endpoint attractor $A^{(r)}$ is that option; so
attractors are consensus states and there are at most $M$ of them. Repetitions that
never settle, and those that settle on a stable non-unanimous split (a frozen
disagreement, not a consensus answer), are excluded; bistability is measured on the
unanimous-converged subset only. Scoring is per (task, config) cell over that
cell's $n_c$ converged repetitions. The cell's \emph{dominant attractor} is the
most frequent endpoint option, and each repetition's start is summarised by
\begin{equation}
  g(s_0) \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N\},
\end{equation}
the number of agents whose round-$0$ vote is on the dominant option. This coarse
summary keeps the basin-selection table dense enough to have power at
$n_c \approx 30$; the full initial composition has too many distinct values to be
informative at this sample size.

\paragraph{Signature 1: coexistence.}
The empirical basin distribution over endpoint attractors is
$\hat{P}(A = k) = \tfrac{1}{n_c}\sum_r \mathbf{1}[A^{(r)} = k]$, a basin-stability
estimate of relative basin volumes under the model's own initial distribution
\citep{menck2013basin}. The effective number of coexisting attractors is the
inverse Simpson (Hill) index \citep{hill1973diversity},
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_k \hat{P}(A = k)^2} \in [1, M].
\end{equation}
$N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 1$ is a single attractor, $\approx 2$ two coexisting
attractors, and so on, so $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ reports \emph{how many} attractors
coexist. Because attractors are unanimous options, it is bounded by $M$. Since $M$
varies across tasks, we report $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ raw and compare it only within a
fixed $M$; normalising by $M$ would make a bistable three-option task ($2/3$) look
more multistable than an identically bistable four-option task ($2/4$), an artefact
of option count. In practice GPQA is entirely $M = 4$; only HiddenBench mixes
$M = 3$ and $M = 4$, so its $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ summaries are reported separately by
option count.

\paragraph{Signature 2: basin selection.}
Coexistence alone does not establish multistability; the initial condition must
select the attractor \citep{feudel2008complex, pisarchik2014control}. We measure
the association between $g(s_0)$ and the endpoint $A$ with Cram\'er's $V$
\citep{cramer1946mathematical} on their contingency table,
\begin{equation}
  V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2 / n_c}{\min(R_{\mathrm{tab}} - 1,\, C_{\mathrm{tab}} - 1)}} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2$ is the Pearson statistic and $R_{\mathrm{tab}}, C_{\mathrm{tab}}$
are the table's numbers of rows (distinct $g(s_0)$, at most $N+1$) and columns
(distinct endpoints, at most $M$). $V \approx 0$ means the start is irrelevant;
$V$ near $1$ means the start deterministically selects the endpoint. The null
shuffles the pairing of $g(s_0)$ and $A$ across the $n_c$ repetitions, preserving
both marginals while destroying the start-to-endpoint link
\citep{theiler1992surrogate}; for $B = 1000$ surrogates the add-one one-sided
$p$-value flags basin selection when $p < 0.05$.

\paragraph{Cell label.}
Each cell is labelled
\begin{equation}
  \text{label}(c) =
  \begin{cases}
    \textbf{monostable} & N_{\mathrm{eff}} < 1.5,\\[2pt]
    \textbf{multistable} & N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5 \ \wedge\ p < 0.05,\\[2pt]
    \textbf{stochastic (not bistable)} & N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5 \ \wedge\ p \geq 0.05,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
with the degree of multistability read from $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and capped at $M$.
The cutoff $1.5$ is a reporting threshold, not a test; the inferential claim rests
on $p$. The third case is the essential guard: coexistence without basin selection
is scatter, not multistability. We attach a bootstrap over the cell's repetitions
\citep{efron1979bootstrap} carrying $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ as a descriptive interval on
the attractor count, and, because per-cell power is limited, lean on the
across-task distribution of $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $V$ rather than any single cell.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In our running scenario, take a cell with $n_c = 30$ converged repetitions; the dominant attractor is option~$A$ (correct). The coexistence reading depends only on the endpoint distribution.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Monostable.} Endpoints $28 \times A$, $2 \times B$ give
        $\hat{P} = (0.933, 0.067)$ and
        $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/(0.933^2 + 0.067^2) \approx 1.14 < 1.5$: one valley.
  \item \textbf{Bistable.} Endpoints $15 \times A$, $15 \times B$ give
        $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 2.0$. If repetitions starting with more agents on $A$
        (high $g(s_0)$) end on $A$ and those starting with few end on $B$, then
        $V \approx 1$, $p < 0.05$: two basins selected by the start.
  \item \textbf{Tristable.} Endpoints $10 \times A$, $10 \times B$, $10 \times C$
        with the start predicting each give $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 3.0$,
        $p < 0.05$ (the maximal degree for $M = 3$).
  \item \textbf{Stochastic.} Endpoints $15 \times A$, $15 \times B$ again give
        $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 2.0$, but if $g(s_0)$ is unrelated to the endpoint then
        $V \approx 0$, $p \gg 0.05$: coexistence without basin selection, correctly
        \emph{not} labelled bistable.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Energy-landscape view (visual complement).}
To display the attractors and the barrier between them, we pool the per-round
macrostate visits across the cell and build the empirical potential over the
composition simplex \citep{watanabe2014energy, ezaki2017energy},
$U(s) = -\ln \hat{P}(s)$, with two compositions adjacent iff they differ by exactly
one agent's vote. A local minimum of $U$ is an attractor, and a disconnectivity
graph \citep{becker1997disconnectivity} over the minima shows their number, basin membership, and separating barrier
heights: two valleys with a barrier is the bistable landscape, one valley is
monostable. Because this pooling mixes transient early rounds with the endpoint,
the landscape is a visual complement to Signatures 1--2, not the primary test.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Two points beyond the shared phenomenological caveat. First, classical basin
stability draws initial conditions uniformly \citep{menck2013basin}, whereas our
starts come from the model's own round-$0$ distribution, so $\hat{P}(A)$ estimates
\emph{effective} basin volume under that distribution, not geometric volume.
Second, because $g(s_0)$ counts agents already on the eventual dominant option, a
high $V$ is consistent both with genuine basin selection and with ordinary
majority-vote persistence; the test detects start-to-endpoint dependence but does
not by itself separate these two mechanisms, so we read the association as
necessary but not mechanistically decisive.

% 5.4 DOMINANCE

\subsection{Dominance}
\label{sec:method:dominance}

\paragraph{Concept.}
Dominance is the concentration of directed influence onto a single node, an
\emph{emergent hub}: a structural motif \citep{milo2002network, alon2007network}
read at the system level, since it is a property of the whole influence
distribution. Its formal home is opinion dynamics: in the DeGroot model
\citep{degroot1974consensus} and its social-power extension, an agent's influence
equals its network centrality, and the group is \emph{autocratic} when these are
maximally nonuniform and \emph{democratic} when uniform \citep{jia2015opinion};
dominance is the autocratic pole. It is the structural substrate of the debate
pathologies we target, herding to a confident leader
\citep{chen2023consensus, cho2025herd} by which one agent can degrade collective
accuracy \citep{wynn2025talk, prasad2025certain}. A single converted vote or fast
agreement can manufacture apparent concentration, so we measure the concentration
of influence \emph{sources} against a topology-respecting baseline that removes the
wiring's own contribution. What we detect is a concentration \emph{signature}, not
a causal hub: an adoption edge cannot separate ``$j$ followed $i$'' from
coincidental agreement.

\paragraph{Measurement primitive: the emergent influence graph.}
The primitive and independent unit is the per-repetition influence graph, which
requires within-repetition agent identity (agents remain unlabeled across
repetitions, respected because the per-repetition score is a single scalar).
Dominance is defined over agents and their conversions, so it is independent of the
option count $M$. A \emph{conversion} is the basic event: agent $j$ adopts, at
round $t{+}1$, a vote that a disagreeing neighbour $i$ held at round $t$. The
directed edge $i \to j$ accumulates the confidence-weighted conversions of $j$ onto
$i$'s vote, with credit split among the neighbours already holding the target vote,
\begin{equation}
  w_{ij} = \sum_{t} \frac{1}{m_j^t}\,
           \mathbf{1}\!\left[v_j^{t+1} = v_i^{t} \neq v_j^{t}\right] c_j^{t},
  \qquad
  m_j^t = \bigl|\{k \in \mathcal{N}(j) : v_k^{t} = v_j^{t+1}\}\bigr|,
\end{equation}
where $c_j^t$ is the converting agent's confidence and $\mathcal{N}(j)$ its
topological neighbourhood; the factor $1/m_j^t$ shares one conversion equally among
all eligible sources. Per-agent influence is the weighted out-strength
$\sigma_i = \sum_j w_{ij}$. We use out-strength rather than eigenvector centrality
\citep{bonacich1972factoring}, which is undefined or degenerate on
non-strongly-connected and acyclic graphs \citep{newman2010networks} and
hub-localises \citep{martin2014localization}; our graphs are tiny, sparse,
disconnected, and near-acyclic, so out-strength is the only always-defined node
score.

\paragraph{Concentration statistic.}
Dominance is the concentration of the influence-share vector
$p_i = \sigma_i / \sum_k \sigma_k$, measured by the normalised Herfindahl index
\citep{hirschman1964paternity},
\begin{equation}
  D = \frac{\sum_i p_i^2 - \tfrac{1}{N}}{1 - \tfrac{1}{N}} \in [0, 1].
\end{equation}
$D = 0$ is democratic (equal influence), $D = 1$ a single pure hub. Normalisation
fixes the scale and makes $D$ comparable across configs, unlike Freeman
centralization \citep{freeman1978centrality}, whose maximum is defined only for an
unweighted star. $D$ is undefined when $\sum_k \sigma_k = 0$ (no conversions), and
such repetitions are excluded, as with the fixed-point exclusion for limit cycles.

\paragraph{Detection.}
The null is keyed to topology: a constrained shuffle in the spirit of Theiler's
``Algorithm~0'' \citep{theiler1992surrogate} that, per conversion event, keeps the
converting agent, round, weight, and number of sources but randomises the source
\emph{identity} uniformly among $\mathcal{N}(j)$. In \textbf{fc} the null is
symmetric, so any concentration is emergent; in \textbf{star} the null already
concentrates on the centre by wiring alone, so only the \emph{excess} over it is
behaviourally meaningful. In the star this excess is moreover often undefined: for
a leaf, the only neighbour is the centre, so a leaf conversion has a single
possible source and the shuffle cannot randomise it. As a result the star null is
degenerate for a large majority of star repetitions (73--87\,\%, reported in
\autoref{sec:results:dominance}), and the emergent-excess test is effectively
identified only for the fully-connected topology; raw $D$ is still reported for
both. For $B = 1000$ surrogates the add-one one-sided
$p = \tfrac{1 + \#\{b : D^{(b)} \geq D\}}{1 + B}$ flags a repetition when
$p < 0.05$. Because raw $D$ is mechanically larger in the star, we never compare it
across topologies: across topologies we report the standardised
$z_r = (D_r - \mu_{\mathrm{null}})/\sigma_{\mathrm{null}}$ or the flag rate, and
within a topology we report raw $D$. As for self-reinforcement, an entrenching hub
predicts a directional ordering $\bar{D}(W{=}5) > \bar{D}(W{=}2) > \bar{D}(W{=}1)$
at fixed topology. As a secondary descriptor, not part of $D$, we also report
whether the hub's vote equals the final consensus and its capitulation rate,
separating a followed leader from an overruled one. Uncertainty is attached by a
cluster bootstrap over repetitions stratified by task \citep{efron1979bootstrap},
carrying $D_r$ (or $z_r$) as the unit.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In our running scenario (\texttt{fc} topology), agent~0 opens at $D_8$, with agents~1--3 at $A_6, B_5, C_5$---an illustrative case where the hub drives the group to the wrong answer. The \updatephase{} vote table (confidence subscripts):
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
t & \text{ag}_0 & \text{ag}_1 & \text{ag}_2 & \text{ag}_3\\\hline
0 & D_8 & A_6 & B_5 & C_5\\
1 & D_9 & D_6 & B_5 & C_5\\
2 & D_9 & D_7 & D_5 & C_5\\
3 & D_9 & D_7 & D_6 & D_5
\end{array}
\]
gives three conversions: $0 \to 1$ gains $6$ ($m = 1$); $0 \to 2, 1 \to 2$ each
$2.5$ ($m = 2$); $0 \to 3, 1 \to 3, 2 \to 3$ each $\tfrac{5}{3}$ ($m = 3$). Then
$\sigma = (\tfrac{61}{6}, \tfrac{25}{6}, \tfrac{5}{3}, 0)$,
$p \approx (0.635, 0.260, 0.104, 0)$, and $D = (0.482 - 0.25)/0.75 \approx 0.31$.
Agent $0$ is a clear hub ($64\,\%$ of influence), yet $D$ is far from $1$: the
$1/m_j^t$ split makes each early adopter a co-source for later conversions, so
staggered convergence never yields a pure hub. $D = 1$ needs all credit on one node
(a single conversion, or a simultaneous mass flip onto a sole prior holder); a lone
dissenter folding into a strong majority is the opposite, $D = \tfrac{1}{9} \approx 0.11$.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
With four agents ($N = 4$, GPQA-Diamond) the four shares and a discrete null bound the minimum $p$, so
per-repetition power is low and the signal lives in the aggregate. A
single-conversion repetition gives $D = 1$ trivially, which is controlled by
aggregation and the topology null rather than by discarding. As with every
detector, certifying a causal hub would require perturbation experiments.

% 5.5 LIMIT CYCLES
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Limit Cycles}
\label{sec:method:lc}

\subsubsection{Agent-Level Detection}
\label{sec:method:lc:agent}

\paragraph{Concept.}
In dynamical systems a limit cycle is an isolated closed orbit: a self-sustained
periodic oscillation that requires no external forcing
\citep{strogatz2015nonlinear}. At the motif level, sustained oscillation is the
dynamical signature of feedback loops, the complementary case to the positive
autoregulation behind self-reinforcement \citep{milo2002network, alon2007network}.
For us the state variable is the agent's vote $v_i^t \in \{1,\ldots,M\}$ (the discrete choice defined in \autoref{sec:exp}), one of the task's $M$ options,
and a limit cycle is an agent that keeps cycling through votes (for example
$A, B, A, B, \ldots$) without ever settling. This matters because convergence to
consensus is one system fate, whereas a sustained vote oscillation is a
qualitatively different one, periodic disagreement that never resolves; detecting
it tests whether cooperative debate can become dynamically trapped in a cycle
rather than a point. With only a handful of options, votes repeat by chance and a
transient wobble that later converges is not a cycle, so the definition requires
both that the oscillation is \emph{self-sustained} (the agent does not converge)
and that its periodic structure \emph{beats a chance baseline}. We measure both;
the isolation and stability of a true limit cycle cannot be certified from one
observed trajectory and are not claimed.

\paragraph{Measurement primitive and end-state classification.}
The primitive is the per-agent vote sequence within a repetition,
$v = (v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_T)$, taking the committed \updatephase{} vote at each round.
Let $k$ be the length of the maximal \emph{trailing constant segment} of $v$. If
$k \geq u$ (with $u = 3$, the early-stopping window) the agent has settled: it is
classified as a \textbf{fixed point} and excluded. Otherwise it is a
\textbf{limit-cycle candidate}, subject to an eligibility guard $L \geq 4$ (room
for one repeat of the shortest, period-2, cycle). We deliberately \emph{do not
trim} a trailing block: it cannot be separated into cycle-internal repeats and
genuine convergence, so any keep-$k$ rule would be phase-dependent and unsound.
Excluding converged trajectories wholesale is the definitionally correct move,
since a transient that decays into a fixed point is not a limit cycle.

\paragraph{Recurrence quantification.}
We quantify periodicity with categorical recurrence quantification analysis, using
exact matching (radius $0$) and no embedding, since the vote is itself the state
\citep{cocodale2014crqa}. For sequence positions $a, b$ the recurrence matrix is
\begin{equation}
  R(a,b) = \mathbb{1}[\,v_a = v_b\,], \qquad a \neq b,
\end{equation}
with the line of identity excluded (Theiler window $1$)
\citep{eckmann1987recurrence}. A periodic orbit appears as diagonal lines parallel
to the main diagonal, equally spaced by the period; a fixed point fills a solid
block; scattered points indicate no periodicity \citep{marwan2007recurrence}.
\emph{Determinism} is the fraction of recurrence points on diagonal lines of
length $l \geq l_{\min} = 2$ \citep{zbilut1992embeddings, webber1994dynamical},
\begin{equation}
  DET = \frac{\sum_{l \geq l_{\min}} l\,P(l)}{\sum_{l \geq 1} l\,P(l)},
\end{equation}
where $P(l)$ is the histogram of diagonal-line lengths, and the \emph{dominant
period} is the lag of the densest off-diagonal,
\begin{equation}
  RR(\tau) = \frac{1}{L-\tau}\sum_{a=0}^{L-1-\tau} R(a, a+\tau),
  \qquad \hat{P} = \arg\max_{\tau \geq 1} RR(\tau).
\end{equation}
Crucially, $DET$ alone cannot separate persistence from periodicity: a solid block
(a near-constant sequence) and equally-spaced diagonals (a genuine cycle) both
inflate it. A single stray vote in an otherwise constant sequence can therefore
attain high $DET$ while having dominant period $\hat{P} = 1$, i.e.\ no oscillation.
We guard against this by requiring $\hat{P} \geq 2$, with the upper bound
$\hat{P} \leq \lfloor L/2 \rfloor$ the largest period that can recur at least twice
within the window.

\paragraph{Detection.}
The null uses random-shuffle surrogates (Theiler's ``Algorithm~0''): random
permutations of the agent's own vote multiset \citep{theiler1992surrogate}.
Shuffling preserves the symbol frequencies, so the $M$-option chance-match rate is
built into the null automatically, while destroying temporal order. For
$B = 1000$ surrogates we compute $DET^{(b)}$ and the add-one one-sided $p$-value \citep{phipson2010permutation}
\begin{equation}
  p = \frac{1 + \#\{\,b : DET^{(b)} \geq DET\,\}}{1 + B},
\end{equation}
and flag a limit cycle when
\begin{equation}
  p < 0.05 \quad\text{and}\quad 2 \leq \hat{P} \leq \lfloor L/2 \rfloor,
\end{equation}
that is, when the observed $DET$ exceeds the $95$th percentile of its shuffle null
\emph{and} the dominant period is a genuine, resolvable oscillation rather than
persistence or a sparse high-lag chance match. We report a binary flag per
candidate, prevalence per (config, task) as the flagged share among eligible
candidates, and summaries across tasks per topology and $W$.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In our running scenario, suppose agent~2 fails to settle, cycling $v = (A, B, A, B, A, B)$ across $L = 6$ rounds. Its trailing constant segment has
length $k = 1 < 3$, so it is a candidate, and $L \geq 4$. The recurrence matrix
(main diagonal excluded) is
\[
  R = \begin{pmatrix}
    \cdot & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0\\
    0 & \cdot & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1\\
    1 & 0 & \cdot & 0 & 1 & 0\\
    0 & 1 & 0 & \cdot & 0 & 1\\
    1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdot & 0\\
    0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdot
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The ones lie on diagonals equally spaced by $2$: $RR(1) = 0$, $RR(2) = 1$,
$RR(3) = 0$, so $\hat{P} = 2$, satisfying the guard
$2 \leq \hat{P} \leq \lfloor 6/2 \rfloor = 3$. The offset-2 diagonal carries a line
of length $4$ and the offset-4 diagonal a line of length $2$, so
$DET = (4 + 2)/(4 + 2) = 1.0$. For the null, the multiset $\{3A, 3B\}$ has
$\binom{6}{3} = 20$ arrangements, of which only the two perfectly alternating ones
reach $DET = 1$, giving $p = 2/20 = 0.10$, which does \emph{not} clear $0.05$: at
$L = 6$ even a perfect period-2 cycle lacks the power to be called significant. The same pattern over a full-length candidate ($L \approx 16$) has an exact
combinatorial null probability of $\approx 2/\binom{16}{8} \approx 2 \times
10^{-4}$; with $B = 1000$ shuffle surrogates the add-one $p$-value saturates at
its floor $1/(B{+}1) \approx 10^{-3}$, so such a case is flagged decisively at
that floor, which is exactly why only the non-converged, full-length repetitions are
informative. By
contrast $(A,B,A,B,B,B,B)$ has $k \geq 3$, is a fixed point, and is excluded; and
the near-constant $(A,B,B,B,B,B,B)$ attains high $DET$ but $\hat{P} = 1$ and is
rejected by the period guard as persistence, not oscillation.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Beyond sparsity and the phenomenological caveat, the alphabet is small
($M \in \{3,4\}$), which inflates the chance recurrence rate, and the sequence
length caps the detectable period at $\lfloor L/2 \rfloor$. The shuffle null
controls the chance-recurrence inflation, but the period guard makes the test
conservative, so a small nonzero flag rate remains under a chance baseline; we
compare observed prevalence against this floor rather than against zero.

\subsubsection{System-Level Detection}
\label{sec:method:lc:system}

The agent-level test asks whether an \emph{individual} is trapped oscillating. The
system-level reading asks the complementary question, whether the \emph{collective
configuration} cycles, by applying the identical recurrence machinery to the
macrostate sequence instead of a single agent's votes. The primitive is now the
per-repetition composition sequence $s = (s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_T)$, and the
repetition is both the measurement primitive and the independent unit.

End-state classification carries over with one addition. Let $k$ be the length of
the maximal trailing constant segment of $s$; if $k \geq u$ the configuration has
\textbf{settled} and is excluded. This covers two cases: unanimous consensus, and
a \emph{stable non-unanimous split} (a persistent $3$--$1$ or $2$--$2$
composition). The split is a fixed point, not a cycle, and is precisely the
polarisation regime we flag for future work; here it is correctly excluded.
Classification is done on the sequence, not on whether the debate early-stopped,
since early stopping triggers only on unanimity and a stable split runs to the cap
$T$ while still being a fixed point. The recurrence matrix, $DET$, dominant period,
surrogate null, and flagging rule are identical to the agent-level case, with
$R(a,b) = \mathbb{1}[s_a = s_b]$. The period guard is equally necessary: a stable
split produces a solid recurrence block with $\hat{P} = 1$ and is correctly
rejected.

The two readings answer different questions and neither implies the other. If two
agents swap $A \leftrightarrow B$ each round while the other two hold, every $s_t$
is identical: the agent-level test flags two period-2 cycles while the
system-level test sees a fixed point. Conversely a rotating majority can cycle
$s_t$ with no single agent being periodic. We therefore report both. The macrostate
space has $\binom{N + M - 1}{M - 1}$ compositions (15 for $M = 3$, 35 for $M = 4$)
against $M$ raw vote symbols, so chance recurrence is lower and $DET$ cleaner at
the system level, but there is only one sequence per repetition rather than per
agent, so candidates are far fewer; as at the agent level, a sparse or near-null
result is expected and reported honestly.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Linguistic Scoring}
\label{sec:method:linguistic}

The four motifs above are read from the discrete vote and continuous confidence.
The public messages carry a further, complementary signal: \emph{how} an agent
argues, independently of \emph{what} it votes. We score each message on three
orthogonal linguistic dimensions and later correlate them with the motif measures
(\autoref{sec:results}). This is a descriptive instrument, not a motif detector.

\paragraph{Dimensions.}
Each public message is scored on three dimensions, each a bipolar ordinal scale
from $1$ to $5$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Conviction}, how certain the message sounds, from $1$ (very
        hedged and uncertain) to $5$ (completely categorical). This is the
        linguistic counterpart of the numeric confidence and is defined at every
        round, including round $0$.
  \item \textbf{Anchoring}, the stated \emph{source} of the belief, from $1$
        (purely the agent's own reasoning) to $5$ (purely deference to peers).
  \item \textbf{Dialogic orientation}, the degree of \emph{engagement} with a
        named peer, from $1$ (no reference to any other agent) to $5$ (directly
        quoting or paraphrasing a specific agent).
\end{itemize}
Anchoring and dialogic orientation are deliberately distinct and must not be
conflated: engagement with a peer's content is not the same as deference to it. An
agent that refutes a peer's specific claim scores high on dialogic orientation (it
directly addresses a named agent) but low on anchoring (it holds its own line
against that agent). Because both dimensions describe the relation to peer content,
they are undefined at round $0$, where no peer content yet exists; only conviction
is scored there.

\paragraph{Scoring procedure and limitations.}

Scores are assigned by a lightweight LLM judge \citep{zheng2023mtbench} that reads a single public message,
together with the minimal context needed to resolve peer references, and returns an
integer $1$--$5$ for each dimension. The judge is prompted with the definitions
above and one worked example per dimension. We score every public message across
all repetitions of the final dataset, yielding one $(\text{conviction},
\text{anchoring}, \text{dialogic})$ triple per (agent, round, repetition), which is
then aggregated to the repetition and cell levels for correlation with the
motif measures.

Two limitations bound the interpretation. First, the scores are \emph{ordinal}:
the levels are ranked but not equally spaced, so we treat them as ranks and rely on
rank-based statistics rather than on interval arithmetic. Second, and more
importantly, the judge is an instance of the \emph{same} model family used for the
debate agents. This makes the scorer not fully independent of the system it
measures: it may share stylistic priors with the agents, which can inflate apparent
consistency and could bias absolute levels. We therefore use the linguistic scores
only for \emph{relative} comparisons (across conditions, and in correlation with
motif measures) and never as an absolute ground truth of argument quality. An
independent-scorer replication is left to future work.
