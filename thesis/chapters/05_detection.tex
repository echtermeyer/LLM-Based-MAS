Each of the four motifs defined in this section is a hypothesis about the
underlying debate dynamics. If the system self-organizes in the way a
complex-systems reading predicts, then confidence should reinforce within a held
position, repeated repetitions should settle into a small number of
initial-condition-selected attractors, influence should concentrate on particular
agents, and in some configurations vote sequences may oscillate persistently
without converging. This section turns each hypothesis into a \emph{detector}, that is, a
statistic computed from the interaction log together with a principled null model
against which it is tested, so that a positive result reflects genuine structure
rather than the chance regularities of a small, discrete state space. Together the
four detectors form \suitename{}, the detector suite this thesis contributes.

Two properties are shared by all four detectors and therefore are worth stating up
front. Firstly, each detector reads only from ordinary interaction logs, the vote
and confidence channels defined in \autoref{sec:exp}, which is what later lets us
propose the same detectors as diagnostic tools. And secondly, each detects a
\emph{signature}, not a certified dynamical object. Thus, we add a
paragraph stating the limitations to each detector which makes explicit what the
corresponding detector can and cannot claim.

\subsection{Shared Measurement Conventions}
\label{sec:method:vocab}

Before defining each detector individually, it is worth fixing what they share.
Four things recur across every subsection below, namely the shape those subsections take,
the classification of the motifs themselves, the macrostate the system-level
detectors read from, and the nesting of the data that constrains every comparison.

The four subsections each follow the same five parts, in the same order.
\emph{Concept} states the motif in dynamical-systems terms and names the quantity
in a debate that plays the role of the state variable. \emph{Measurement} defines
the \emph{measurement primitive}, the lowest-level object the detector identifies, and
the statistic computed from it. \emph{Detection} specifies the null model
against which that statistic is tested, and the rule for flagging the motif. 
\emph{Worked example} runs the detector by hand on a small case, and \emph{Limitations} states
what the resulting statistic cannot claim. Furthermore, so that
the four worked examples can be read against one another, they share a single
running scenario, one task with $N = 4$ agents and $M = 4$ options labelled $A$ to
$D$ of which $A$ is correct, and they write the state of an agent as its option with its
confidence as a subscript, so that $D_8$ is a vote for $D$ with a confidence score of $8$.

The motifs those subsections detect are preselected rather than exhaustively
enumerated and each is motivated by a documented failure mode of \ac{LLM}-based debate.
\autoref{tab:method:motifs} lists them together with their classification and the
section in which each detector is defined. Further motifs that fit the same
framework but fall outside this study are shown in
Appendix~\ref{app:future_motifs}.

What the table calls the \emph{level} of a motif decides where its detector reads
from. Self-reinforcement, the only agent-level motif, reads directly from
per-agent vote and confidence trajectories. The three system-level readings
instead operate on the macrostate $s_t$ defined in \autoref{sec:setup:notation}.
It forgets which agent holds which vote but keeps how the votes are split across
the options. A $3$--$1$ split therefore differs from a $2$--$2$ split, and a vote
for the correct option differs from a vote for a wrong one. However, each of the three motifs 
takes a different slice of that record. Multistability reads only the locked-in endpoint, dominance reads the
emergent influence graph, which is built relationally and therefore needs
within-repetition agent identity, and the system-level limit-cycle test reads the
full sequence $s_0, \ldots, s_T$.

Whichever slice a detector takes, it inherits the same nesting of the data,
\begin{equation}
  \text{agent} \subset \text{repetition} \subset
  (\text{task} \times \text{config}) \subset \text{dataset},
\end{equation}
where tasks are crossed with configurations, $\text{config} = W \times \text{topology}$,
with $W \in \{1,2,5\}$, $\text{topology} \in \{\text{fc}, \text{star}\}$, $N = 4$,
and $\text{dataset} \in \{\text{GPQA}, \text{HiddenBench}\}$, and that nesting
constrains what may be compared with what. Because agents interact within a
repetition, their trajectories are not independent, so the repetition is the
independent unit of replication and agent-level trajectories are never pooled as
if they were separate observations. For the same reason GPQA
and HiddenBench are always analysed separately and never pooled, and memory-window
comparisons are made within a fixed topology and dataset, since cells from
different configs are not exchangeable.

All four detectors share the limitation that they are descriptive and not causal. 
They find a
\emph{signature} in the trajectories or influence graph, not a proven attractor,
cycle, or causal hub. Telling the two apart would need perturbation experiments,
which we leave to future work. Because the debates are cooperative and forced to
converge, we also expect several detectors to find little or nothing. This is why
each detection paragraph below compares its statistic against a named baseline
rather than against zero.

\subsection{Self-Reinforcement}
\label{sec:method:sr}

\paragraph{Concept.}
In complex systems, self-reinforcement is \emph{positive feedback} reinforcing itself. 
The current
level of a state variable raises the drive toward more of the same, so small changes
are amplified rather than damped \citep{arthur1989competing}. At the motif level
this is positive autoregulation, a node that augments its own production
\citep{alon2007network}. For us the state variable is the expressed
\emph{confidence} of an agent. Self-reinforcement is then the tendency of an agent
to push its own confidence upward while it holds a position, driven by
accumulating commitment rather than by new incoming information. The
pattern is already documented in multi-turn \ac{LLM} debate, where confidence tends to rise
across rounds even in the face of opposing arguments \citep{prasad2025certain}.

Rising confidence alone is not enough to show self-reinforcement, since rational
updating and peer conformity also raise confidence. We therefore measure two
further signatures. \emph{Directional drift} is confidence tending to rise rather
than fall while an agent holds its position. \emph{Strength scaling} is this drift
growing the more an agent is re-exposed to its own earlier commitments, which the
memory window $W$ controls directly. A third signature, \emph{endogeneity}, would
show that the rise comes from the commitment of the agent itself rather than from new
information, but trajectory data cannot separate it from peer effects.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
The primitive is the \emph{vote-stable segment} which is the maximal block of consecutive
rounds, from $t_s$ to $t_e$, in which agent $i$ keeps the same vote, so that
$v_i^t = v_i^{t_s}$ for all $t \in \{t_s, \ldots, t_e\}$.
With $L = t_e - t_s + 1$ as its length in rounds, we keep a segment only if
$L \geq 3$. Three rounds give two steps for the
least-squares fit to average over. An agent that flips can contribute several
segments, and one that locks in early contributes a single long one. Agents are
exchangeable, so we pool segments within a config and never match them across
repetitions.
Within each kept segment we fit the confidence on the round index by \ac{OLS},
\begin{equation}
  c_i^t = \beta_0 + \beta_{\mathrm{seg}}\, t + \varepsilon_t,
  \qquad t \in \{t_s, \ldots, t_e\},
\end{equation}
and keep the slope $\beta_{\mathrm{seg}}$, in confidence points per round. Its sign
enters the prevalence test below, and its value enters the mean slope $\bar{\beta}$
used in the $W$ comparison.

\paragraph{Detection.}
To detect self-reinforcement we ask whether segment slopes point upward more often than chance would allow.
We read them in two ways, both within a single config.
The first is a presence test for directional drift. Let $n_{\mathrm{seg}}$ be the
number of kept segments in a config-task pair. The share of upward segments is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{p}_{\mathrm{SR}}
  = \frac{\bigl|\{\text{seg}: \beta_{\mathrm{seg}} > 0\}\bigr|}{n_{\mathrm{seg}}},
\end{equation}
so flat segments ($\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} = 0$) count in the denominator but never in
the numerator. Under a no-feedback ``wobble'' null, an upward and a downward
segment are equally likely. With a fraction $f$ of flat segments this gives
$P(\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} > 0) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1-f) \leq 0.5$. We therefore take
$\hat{p}_{\mathrm{SR}} > 0.5$ as an indicator of self-reinforcement. Flat segments and
the confidence ceiling both make a positive result harder to get, because a
segment already at the top of the scale cannot drift further up.
The second reading uses the memory window to tell the mechanisms apart.
Let $\bar{\beta}$ be the mean segment slope in a config. An \emph{endogenous}
reading predicts that re-reading the own earlier messages over a longer
window strengthens the held position,
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\beta}(W{=}5) > \bar{\beta}(W{=}2) > \bar{\beta}(W{=}1),
\end{equation}
whereas a \emph{peer-conformity} reading expects a longer window to mainly add
peer content, so the ordering may be missing or reversed.

We report both readings per config-task and then summarise them across tasks, for
example ``above $0.5$ in $k$ of $50$ tasks''. This shows whether an effect is broad
or driven by a single task.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In the running scenario, suppose agent~$0$ holds option~$A$ for five rounds with
confidences $c = (5, 6, 6, 8, 9)$. This is one vote-stable segment of length
$L = 5$. Regressing confidence on the round index gives
$\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} = +1.0$ confidence points per round, so the segment is
upward and counts toward $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{SR}}$. Had the agent instead drifted
$c = (7, 7, 6, 6, 5)$, the slope would be $-0.5$ and the segment would count
against it. A segment that never moved, $c = (6,6,6,6,6)$, has
$\beta_{\mathrm{seg}} = 0$. It stays in the denominator but is not counted as
upward, which is what makes the $0.5$ reference conservative. Under the
strength-scaling reading, the average of such slopes should grow as the window
widens from $W{=}1$ to $W{=}5$.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Beyond the shared problem that the detectors are descriptive, two points limit the
detection.
First, \emph{endogeneity is not isolated} as an upward segment fits equally well with peer
copying and with a peer adding genuinely new information in a later round, and
rising confidence alone cannot tell these apart. The $W$ ordering narrows this
ambiguity but does not settle it, since a longer window also re-exposes more peer
content. Second, \emph{convergence is a confound} as on easier tasks, fast agreement can
itself raise confidence. This is why we report prevalence per task and not as a
single pooled figure.

\subsection{Multistability}
\label{sec:method:multistability}

\paragraph{Concept.}
In dynamical systems, \emph{multistability} is the coexistence of two or more
stable attractors for one fixed parameter set. Which attractor the system settles
into depends on the basin of attraction that contains the initial condition
\citep{feudel2008complex, pisarchik2014control}. At the motif level this is the
dynamical signature of positive feedback with a symmetry. The same interaction
rules allow several self-consistent outcomes.
For us the state variable is the macrostate $s_t$, and multistability is read off
the \emph{locked-in endpoint}. For a fixed task, the debate converges to different
consensus answers on different repetitions, and the initial vote configuration,
rather than the correct option, decides which one is reached. This is the
structural reading of unreliable correction. On a task where the model carries a
systematic bias, a wrong-consensus and a right-consensus attractor can coexist.
The fate of the group is then a competition between basins decided at round $0$, which
self-reinforcement amplifies and locks in. Seeing more than one final answer is
necessary but not sufficient, as a task may simply be noisy. Genuine multistability
additionally requires that the initial condition \emph{predicts} the endpoint
\citep{pisarchik2014control}.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
The primitive and independent unit is the repetition, described by its initial
vote composition $s_0 = (n_1^0,\ldots,n_M^0)$ and its final macrostate. A
repetition is converged if its final macrostate is unanimous, whether that was
reached by early stopping or at the round cap $T$. We analyse only converged
repetitions, because a stable non-unanimous endpoint means the group failed to
agree and is not a genuine attractor. Repetitions that end without unanimity are
excluded. The endpoint attractor $A^{(r)}$ of a converged repetition is the
winning option, so each task has at most $M$ distinct attractors.
We score each task-config cell separately, with $n_c$ the number of converged
repetitions in that cell. The \emph{dominant attractor} of a cell is its most
frequent endpoint option. The start of each repetition is summarised by
\begin{equation}
  g(s_0) \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N\},
\end{equation}
the number of agents whose round-$0$ vote is on the dominant option.
From these we compute the empirical basin distribution over endpoint attractors,
$\hat{P}(A = k) = \tfrac{1}{n_c}\sum_r \mathbf{1}[A^{(r)} = k]$. This is a
basin-stability estimate of relative basin volumes under the initial
distribution produced by the model itself \citep{menck2013basin}. The effective number of coexisting attractors
is the inverse Simpson (Hill) index \citep{hill1973diversity},
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_k \hat{P}(A = k)^2} \in [1, M].
\end{equation}
A value of $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 1$ means a single attractor, $\approx 2$ means
two coexisting attractors, and so on. Attractors are unanimous options, so
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is bounded by $M$. Since $M$ varies across tasks, we report
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ raw and compare it only within a fixed $M$. GPQA always has
$M = 4$, while HiddenBench mixes $M = 3$ and $M = 4$, so we report its
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ summaries separately by option count.

\paragraph{Detection.}
Coexistence alone does not establish multistability. The initial condition must
also \emph{select} the attractor \citep{feudel2008complex, pisarchik2014control},
and this association carries the inferential claim. We measure it between
$g(s_0)$ and the endpoint $A$ with Cram\'er's $V$
\citep{cramer1946mathematical} on their contingency table,
\begin{equation}
  V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2 / n_c}{\min(R_{\mathrm{tab}} - 1,\, C_{\mathrm{tab}} - 1)}} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2$ is the Pearson statistic and $R_{\mathrm{tab}}, C_{\mathrm{tab}}$
are the numbers of rows in the table (distinct $g(s_0)$, at most $N+1$) and columns
(distinct endpoints, at most $M$). A value of $V \approx 0$ means the start is
irrelevant, and $V$ near $1$ means the start fully determines the endpoint. The
null shuffles the pairing of $g(s_0)$ and $A$ across the $n_c$ repetitions. This
keeps both marginals but destroys the link between start and endpoint
\citep{theiler1992surrogate}. With $B = 1000$ surrogates, we flag basin selection
when the add-one one-sided $p$-value is below $0.05$.

The two readings combine into a single cell label,
\begin{equation}
  \text{label}(c) =
  \begin{cases}
    \text{monostable} & N_{\mathrm{eff}} < 1.5,\\[2pt]
    \text{multistable} & N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5 \ \wedge\ p < 0.05,\\[2pt]
    \text{stochastic (not multistable)} & N_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 1.5 \ \wedge\ p \geq 0.05,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and we read the degree of multistability from $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, capped at $M$.
The cutoff $1.5$ is a reporting threshold and not a test, so the inferential claim
rests on $p$. The third case is the essential guard, because coexistence without
basin selection is scatter and not multistability. Per-cell power is limited, so
we rely on the distribution of $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $V$ across tasks rather than
on any single cell.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In the running scenario, take a cell with $n_c = 30$ converged repetitions whose
dominant attractor is option~$A$. The coexistence reading depends only on the
endpoint distribution, and the label depends on whether the start predicts it.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Monostable.} Endpoints $28 \times A$, $2 \times B$ give
        $\hat{P} = (0.933, 0.067)$ and
        $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/(0.933^2 + 0.067^2) \approx 1.14 < 1.5$, so there is
        one valley.
  \item \textbf{Multistable.} Endpoints $15 \times A$, $15 \times B$ give
        $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 2.0$. If repetitions starting with more agents on $A$
        (high $g(s_0)$) end on $A$ and those starting with few end on $B$, then
        $V \approx 1$ and $p < 0.05$, so the start selects between two basins.
  \item \textbf{Tristable.} Endpoints $10 \times A$, $10 \times B$, $10 \times C$
        with the start predicting each give $N_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 3.0$ and
        $p < 0.05$, the maximal degree when three of the four options attract endpoints.
  \item \textbf{Stochastic.} Endpoints $15 \times A$, $15 \times B$ again give
        $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 2.0$. If $g(s_0)$ is unrelated to the endpoint, then
        $V \approx 0$ and $p \gg 0.05$. This is coexistence without basin
        selection and is correctly \emph{not} labelled multistable.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
First, basin stability is usually estimated with uniformly drawn initial
conditions \citep{menck2013basin}, whereas our starts come from the round-$0$
distribution of the model itself. $\hat{P}(A)$ therefore estimates the \emph{effective} basin volume
under that distribution and not the geometric volume. Second, $g(s_0)$ counts
agents already on the eventual dominant option, so a high $V$ fits both genuine
basin selection and ordinary persistence of the initial majority. The test detects
dependence of the endpoint on the start but cannot separate these two mechanisms.
We therefore treat the association as necessary but not decisive.

\subsection{Dominance}
\label{sec:method:dominance}

\paragraph{Concept.}
Dominance is the concentration of directed influence onto a single node, an
\emph{emergent hub}. It is the structural member of our four motifs, but not a
motif in the subgraph sense of \citet{milo2002network}, whose degree-preserving
null conserves hubs by construction. It is read at the system level instead, since
it is a property of the whole influence distribution. Its formal home is opinion dynamics. In the DeGroot model
\citep{degroot1974consensus} and its social-power extension, the influence of an agent
tracks its network centrality. The group is \emph{autocratic} when influence is
maximally uneven and \emph{democratic} when it is uniform \citep{jia2015opinion}.
Dominance is the autocratic side. For us the state variable is the distribution of
influence across the four agents, and dominance is the tendency of this
distribution to concentrate on one agent rather than spread evenly. When it does,
a single stubborn agent can pull the rest of the group toward its answer
\citep{chen2023consensus}, and agents conform more readily to peers who appear
confident \citep{cho2025herd}. Conformity of this kind can lower collective
accuracy even when stronger models outnumber weaker ones \citep{wynn2025talk}. Concentration can
also come from the wiring alone, so we measure influence for each topology
separately. The result is a concentration signature and not proof of causation,
since an adoption edge cannot distinguish genuine influence from coincidental
agreement.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
The primitive and independent unit is the per-repetition influence graph, which
requires agent identity within a repetition. Dominance is defined over agents and
their conversions, so it does not depend on the option count $M$. A
\emph{conversion} occurs when agent $j$ adopts, at round $t{+}1$, a vote that a
disagreeing neighbour $i$ held at round $t$. The directed edge $i \to j$ collects
the confidence-weighted conversions of $j$ onto $i$'s vote. Credit is split among
the neighbours that already held the target vote,
\begin{equation}
  w_{ij} = \sum_{t} \frac{1}{m_j^t}\,
           \mathbf{1}\!\left[v_j^{t+1} = v_i^{t} \neq v_j^{t}\right] c_j^{t},
  \qquad
  m_j^t = \bigl|\{k \in \mathcal{N}(j) : v_k^{t} = v_j^{t+1}\}\bigr|,
\end{equation}
where $c_j^t$ is the confidence of the converting agent before the switch, so that a switch
away from a confidently held vote counts as stronger evidence of influence than one
away from a hesitant vote, $\mathcal{N}(j)$ is its
neighbourhood in the topology, and the factor $1/m_j^t$ shares credit equally among
all eligible sources. Per-agent influence is the weighted out-strength
$\sigma_i = \sum_j w_{ij}$. We use out-strength rather than eigenvector centrality
\citep{bonacich1972factoring}, which is undefined or degenerate on
non-strongly-connected and acyclic graphs \citep{newman2010networks} and
concentrates on a few nodes \citep{martin2014localization}. Our graphs are tiny and
sparse, which makes out-strength the only node score that is always defined.

The statistic is the concentration of the influence-share vector
$p_i = \sigma_i / \sum_k \sigma_k$, measured by the normalised Herfindahl--Hirschman index
\citep{hirschman1964paternity},
\begin{equation}
  D = \frac{\sum_i p_i^2 - \tfrac{1}{N}}{1 - \tfrac{1}{N}} \in [0, 1].
\end{equation}
A value of $D = 0$ is democratic, with equal influence for all agents, and $D = 1$
is a single pure hub. $D$ is undefined when $\sum_k \sigma_k = 0$, meaning no
conversion occurs at all, and we exclude such repetitions.

\paragraph{Detection.}
A high $D$ in the star topology says little on its own. The centre is the only
agent a leaf can be converted by, so the wiring alone forces concentration. We
therefore key the null to the topology. It asks how concentrated $D$ would be if
influence were assigned at random on the same wiring and the same conversion
events. We use a constrained shuffle in the spirit of the random-shuffle surrogates of
\citet{theiler1992surrogate}. It keeps the converting agent, the round, the weight,
and the number of eligible sources fixed, and redraws only the source
\emph{identity}, uniformly among $\mathcal{N}(j)$.
In the fully-connected topology every agent has three neighbours, so the shuffle
can reassign any conversion to any of them and the null is symmetric. Any
concentration above it is therefore emergent. In the star topology, however, a
leaf has exactly one neighbour, so a leaf conversion has only one possible source
and the shuffle leaves it unchanged. When a repetition consists of such
conversions, every surrogate reproduces the observed graph and the test can never
flag. The star null is thus degenerate, and we identify emergent concentration only
in the fully-connected topology. We still report raw $D$ for both topologies, but
in the star it describes the wiring and not the behaviour.

A repetition is concentrated when its observed $D$ falls in the upper tail of its
own surrogate distribution. With $B = 1000$ surrogates the add-one one-sided
$p$-value is
\begin{equation}
  p = \frac{1 + \#\{\,b : D^{(b)} \geq D\,\}}{1 + B},
\end{equation}
and we flag the repetition when $p < 0.05$. For the fully-connected topology we
also report the standardised excess
$z_r = (D_r - \mu_{\mathrm{null}})/\sigma_{\mathrm{null}}$ and compare the flag
rate with the $5\,\%$ that the threshold produces by chance. We summarise each per
task and then count across tasks. As for self-reinforcement, an entrenching hub
predicts that concentration grows as the memory window widens at fixed topology.
We also report whether the vote of the hub matches the final consensus and how often the
hub capitulates.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In the running scenario with the fully-connected topology, agent~$0$ opens at
$D_8$ while agents~$1$, $2$, and $3$ open at $A_6$, $B_5$, and $C_5$
respectively. The vote sequence is
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
t & \text{agent}_0 & \text{agent}_1 & \text{agent}_2 & \text{agent}_3\\\hline
0 & D_8 & A_6 & B_5 & C_5\\
1 & D_9 & D_6 & B_5 & C_5\\
2 & D_9 & D_7 & D_5 & C_5\\
3 & D_9 & D_7 & D_6 & D_5
\end{array}
\]
Three conversions occur. At round~$1$, agent~$1$ flips to $D$. Only agent~$0$
held $D$ at round~$0$, so the full weight of agent~$1$'s confidence goes to
edge $0 \to 1$, giving $w_{01} = 6$. At round~$2$, agent~$2$ flips to $D$. Both
agents~$0$ and $1$ held $D$ at round~$1$, so credit splits equally, giving
$w_{02} = w_{12} = 2.5$. At round~$3$, agent~$3$ flips to $D$. All three of
agents~$0$, $1$, and $2$ held $D$ at round~$2$, so credit splits three ways,
giving $w_{03} = w_{13} = w_{23} = \tfrac{5}{3}$.
The out-strengths are therefore $\sigma = (\tfrac{61}{6},\, \tfrac{25}{6},\,
\tfrac{5}{3},\, 0)$, the influence shares are $p \approx (0.635,\, 0.260,\, 0.104,\,
0)$, and $D = (0.482 - 0.25)/0.75 \approx 0.31$. Agent~$0$ accounts for $64\,\%$
of influence, yet $D$ is well below $1$. The credit-splitting rule makes each early
adopter a co-source of all later conversions, so staggered convergence never
produces a pure hub. $D = 1$ would require every conversion to trace back to a
single agent, which in the fully-connected topology with at least two conversions typically
requires every other agent to flip simultaneously.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
First, with four agents the four shares and a discrete null limit how small $p$ can
get. Per-repetition power is therefore low and the signal lives in the aggregate.
Second, a repetition with a single conversion from a single source gives $D = 1$ trivially. We handle
this through aggregation and the topology null and do not discard such
repetitions. Third, as noted above, the star null is degenerate, so the test for
emergent excess is identified only in the fully-connected topology. Any claim about
emergent concentration in the star rests on raw $D$ and its $W$ ordering and not on
the null comparison.

\subsection{Limit Cycles}
\label{sec:method:lc}

\paragraph{Concept.}
In dynamical systems, a limit cycle is an isolated closed orbit, a self-sustained
periodic oscillation that needs no external forcing
\citep{strogatz2015nonlinear}. At the motif level, sustained oscillation is the
dynamical signature of composite negative feedback loops, the complement of the positive
autoregulation behind self-reinforcement \citep{alon2007network}.
This is the only one of our motifs that lives at both agent and system level, so
it has two state variables. At the agent level the state variable is the
vote $v_i^t \in \{1,\ldots,M\}$ of the agent, and a limit cycle is an agent that keeps cycling
through votes (for example $A, B, A, B, \ldots$) without ever settling. At the
system level it is the macrostate $s_t$, and a limit cycle is a collective
configuration that cycles without settling. Neither implies the other, so we detect
and report both. This matters because convergence to consensus is one possible
fate of the system, while sustained oscillation is a different one, a periodic
disagreement that never resolves. Detecting it tests whether cooperative debate can
become trapped in a cycle and not at a point. With only a few symbols, states repeat
by chance, and a transient fluctuation that later converges is not a cycle. The
definition therefore requires two things. The oscillation must be
\emph{self-sustained}, meaning the sequence does not converge, and its periodic
structure must \emph{beat a chance baseline}. We measure both. The isolation and
stability of a true limit cycle cannot be certified from one observed trajectory,
and we do not claim them.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
The primitive depends on the level. At the agent level it is the per-agent vote
sequence within a repetition, $v = (v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_T)$, using the committed
\updatephase{} vote at each round. At the system level it is the per-repetition
composition sequence $s = (s_0, s_1, \ldots, s_T)$, so the repetition is both the
measurement primitive and the independent unit. Everything that follows applies
unchanged to either level, so we write $x = (x_0, \ldots, x_{L-1})$ for whichever
symbolic sequence is being scored.

A sequence that converges is not a limit cycle, so we exclude converged sequences
before computing anything. A sequence counts as converged when it ends in a
constant run of at least $u = 3$ symbols, the settling criterion already used in
\autoref{sec:method:multistability}. We judge this on the sequence itself and not on
the debate, for two reasons. An agent can settle while the debate around it
continues. A debate that deadlocks at a $3$--$1$ or $2$--$2$ split holds that
composition until the cap $T$, which is a fixed point even though no consensus was
reached. Every remaining sequence is a limit-cycle candidate, provided it passes a
guard $L \geq 4$ that leaves room for one repeat of the shortest possible cycle,
which has period~2. We drop converged sequences whole instead of trimming them,
since a trailing constant run cannot be separated into cycle-internal repeats and
genuine convergence.

We quantify periodicity in the remaining candidates with categorical \ac{RQA} \citep{cocodale2014crqa}. For sequence positions $a, b$ the
recurrence matrix \citep{eckmann1987recurrence} records where the sequence revisits a symbol,
\begin{equation}
  R(a,b) = \mathbb{1}[\,x_a = x_b\,], \qquad a \neq b,
\end{equation}
and excludes the main diagonal, which is trivially all ones
\citep{marwan2007recurrence}. The pattern of ones tells the cases apart. A
periodic orbit produces diagonal lines parallel to the main diagonal, spaced equally
by the period. A fixed point produces a solid block, and scattered isolated points indicate no
sustained recurrence \citep{marwan2007recurrence}. Two quantities summarise this.
\emph{Determinism} is the fraction of recurrence points on diagonal lines of length
$l \geq l_{\min} = 2$ \citep{zbilut1992embeddings, webber1994dynamical},
\begin{equation}
  DET = \frac{\sum_{l \geq l_{\min}} l\,P(l)}{\sum_{l \geq 1} l\,P(l)},
\end{equation}
where $P(l)$ is the histogram of diagonal-line lengths. The \emph{dominant period}
is the lag at which recurrence is densest,
\begin{equation}
  RR(\tau) = \frac{1}{L-\tau}\sum_{a=0}^{L-1-\tau} R(a, a+\tau),
  \qquad \hat{P} = \arg\max_{\tau \geq 1} RR(\tau).
\end{equation}
Ties go to the smallest lag.
We need both, because $DET$ alone cannot separate persistence from periodicity. A
solid block and equally spaced diagonals raise it equally, so a single stray symbol
in an otherwise constant sequence can reach a high $DET$ without oscillating at
all. We therefore require $\hat{P} \geq 2$ and cap it at
$\hat{P} \leq \lfloor L/2 \rfloor$, since a longer period cannot recur twice within
the window.

\paragraph{Detection.}
The null uses random-shuffle surrogates, which are random permutations of the
symbols of the sequence itself \citep{theiler1992surrogate}. Shuffling destroys temporal
order but keeps the symbol frequencies, so the chance-match rate implied by the
alphabet is built into the null. For $B = 1000$ surrogates we compute $DET^{(b)}$
and the add-one one-sided $p$-value \citep{phipson2010permutation}
\begin{equation}
  p = \frac{1 + \#\{\,b : DET^{(b)} \geq DET\,\}}{1 + B},
\end{equation}
and flag a limit cycle when
\begin{equation}
  p < 0.05 \quad\text{and}\quad 2 \leq \hat{P} \leq \lfloor L/2 \rfloor .
\end{equation}
We report a binary flag per candidate, the prevalence per config-task as the
flagged share of eligible candidates, and summaries across tasks per topology and
$W$.

The two levels share this machinery and differ only in power. The macrostate space
has $\binom{N + M - 1}{M - 1}$ compositions, $15$ for $M = 3$ and $35$ for
$M = 4$, against only $M$ raw vote symbols. Chance recurrence is therefore lower
and $DET$ cleaner at the system level. On the other hand, there is one sequence per
repetition and not one per agent, so there are far fewer candidates.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
In the running scenario, suppose agent~$2$ fails to settle and cycles
$v = (A, B, A, B, A, B)$ across $L = 6$ rounds. It never ends in a constant run, so
it is a candidate, and $L \geq 4$. The recurrence matrix (main diagonal excluded)
is
\[
  R = \begin{pmatrix}
    \cdot & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0\\
    0 & \cdot & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1\\
    1 & 0 & \cdot & 0 & 1 & 0\\
    0 & 1 & 0 & \cdot & 0 & 1\\
    1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdot & 0\\
    0 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdot
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The ones lie on diagonals spaced by $2$, giving $RR(1) = 0$, $RR(2) = 1$
and $RR(3) = 0$, so $\hat{P} = 2$, which satisfies the guard
$2 \leq \hat{P} \leq \lfloor 6/2 \rfloor = 3$. The offset-2 diagonal carries a line
of length $4$ and the offset-4 diagonal a line of length $2$, so
$DET = (4 + 2)/(4 + 2) = 1.0$.
The null shows why length matters. The multiset $\{3A, 3B\}$ has
$\binom{6}{3} = 20$ arrangements, and only the two perfectly alternating ones reach
$DET = 1$, so $p = 2/20 = 0.10$. At $L = 6$ even a perfect period-2 cycle cannot
clear $0.05$. The same pattern over a full-length candidate ($L \approx 16$) is
flagged decisively, so only long, non-converged sequences are informative. Two
exclusions make this concrete. The sequence $(A,B,A,B,B,B,B)$ ends in a constant
run and is dropped as a fixed point. The sequence $(A,A,A,A,A,A,B)$ does not end in a
constant run of three, reaches a high $DET$, but has $\hat{P} = 1$, so the period guard rejects it as persistence and not
oscillation.
The same scenario shows why we report the two levels separately. Suppose agents~$0$
and $1$ swap $A \leftrightarrow B$ each round while agents~$2$ and $3$ hold their
votes. Every $s_t$ is then identical. The agent-level test flags two period-2
cycles, while the system-level test sees a fixed point and excludes the repetition
entirely. Conversely, a rotating majority can cycle $s_t$ with no single agent
being periodic.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
First, the alphabet is small at the agent level, which inflates the chance
recurrence rate, and at both levels the sequence length caps the detectable period
at $\lfloor L/2 \rfloor$. Second, the shuffle null accounts for chance recurrence,
but the period guard makes the test conservative, so a small nonzero flag rate
remains under a chance baseline. We compare observed prevalence against this floor
and not against zero.
