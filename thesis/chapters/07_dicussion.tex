\autoref{sec:results} reported what the system does. This section asks what it
means, in four steps. Section~\ref{sec:results:synthesis} explains \emph{why} the
system behaves as it does by locating it in the vocabulary of
\autoref{sec:bg:complex}. Section~\ref{sec:discussion:interpretation} asks what
that behaviour \emph{costs}, by measuring the debate against the vote it started
from. Section~\ref{sec:discussion:design} asks what a practitioner can
\emph{do} about it. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations} states the limitations on
all three together with potential future work.

\subsection{The Missing Ingredient}
\label{sec:results:synthesis}

This subsection answers why the system behaves as it does, and it does so by
returning to the definition of self-organization rather than by adding new
measurements. \autoref{sec:bg:complex} introduced the four ingredients that Camazine and
colleagues identify as recurring in self-organizing systems, which are positive
feedback, negative feedback, fluctuations, and repeated local interaction. Three
of the four are clearly present in our results. Positive feedback is self-reinforcement,
which appears in every one of the twelve combinations of dataset, topology, and
memory window at a prevalence between $0.733$ and $0.883$ against a null of
$0.5$, and which goes with faster settling of the debate, since tasks with
steeper confidence slopes reach consensus in fewer rounds in every combination we
tested. Fluctuations are visible as multistability, since $76\,\%$ of the $600$
task and configuration cells admit more than one stable outcome across
repetitions of the same question. Repeated local interaction is the debate
protocol itself. Thus, the second ingredient, negative feedback, is the one that is largely missing.
It appears only weakly in the star topology, where it produces cycles rather than
correction.
Nothing in the protocol bounds an early lead. No agent is rewarded for holding out, the group
pays no cost for converging quickly, and confidence climbs fastest in exactly
those repetitions where the debate has least to resolve, which are the ones that
never change their votes and the ones that start unanimous.

That near-absence is the finding. A system with amplification and little damping does not
deliberate its way to an answer, it magnifies whatever asymmetry it starts with.
This also shows that \suitename{} divides the problem in a useful way, because the four detectors turn out to measure not four phenomena but one mechanism seen from four angles.
Self-reinforcement is the view within a stable segment, where an agent
grows more certain of a position it already held rather than of anything the
exchange revealed. Dominance is the view within a run, where influence
concentrates above the topology-keyed null in all but one of the hundred task and
benchmark combinations even though every agent in a fully-connected run is wired
identically, so the group reliably elects a hierarchy that the structure never
specified. Multistability is the view across repetitions, where the same question
lands in different basins on different runs because the opening draw differs, and
it counts the basins that self-reinforcement then collapses into one. Limit
cycles are the boundary case where the magnification never terminates, and they
are informative precisely because they are almost absent, since nearly every
sequence reaches a fixed point and the $47$ agent level and $32$ system level
flagged cases sit almost entirely in the star topology. That the fully-connected
topology produces no genuine cycles is a clean statement about cooperative
protocol design rather than a measurement failure, because without a single node
that aggregates all peer signals there is no way to reconstruct the opposing
majority after every flip.

Read this way the four motifs yield one claim rather than four findings. Within
the system we tested, deliberation works predominantly as a selective amplifier
of its initial vote distribution rather than as a mechanism that reasons a new
answer into existence. The claim is not novel in kind.
\citet{oh2026entrenchment} name homogenised dynamics that amplify the majority
view regardless of correctness as a root cause of belief entrenchment, and
\citet{estornell2024multillm} derive the same conclusion theoretically from
response homogeneity. What \suitename{} adds is measurement. Each face of the
mechanism is separately detectable from ordinary interaction logs and each was
tested against a null that destroys the pattern and leaves everything else
intact, so a practitioner can check which regime a system is in rather than
having to assume it.

\subsection{A Lossy Amplifier}
\label{sec:discussion:interpretation}

This subsection asks what the missing negative feedback costs. The fair baseline
is the vote the same four agents held before the debate began, because it
isolates what the exchange itself adds. The seeded experiment measures this
directly. It fixes how many agents start on the correct option and leaves the
debate unchanged. Accuracy rises from $1.8\,\%$ with no correct agent to
$26.2\,\%$, $63.7\,\%$, and $86.3\,\%$ with one, two, and three, and it does so
in almost every task.

Compared with a plurality vote at round $0$, this is not a uniform gain. When the
correct answer starts behind, the debate helps. A single correct agent has a
plurality baseline of zero, yet it wins a quarter of the time after debate. A
split of two against two, the most common start with two correct agents,
resolves correctly more often than the coin flip that
plurality voting would need to break the tie. When the correct answer starts
ahead, the debate hurts. Three correct agents would always win a plurality vote,
but the debate talks them out of the right answer in roughly one repetition in
seven. Debate is therefore not majority voting with reasoning attached. Instead, it
widens the spread of outcomes around the opening votes. Its outcomes track neutral
copying, where an option wins as often as its opening share, with a modest pull
toward the correct answer, rather than plurality voting, where the opening
leader always wins. Debate moves outcomes away from the
opening plurality in both directions. This helps when that plurality is
unreliable and hurts when it is already good.

This single property explains the two regimes we observe, and we think it also
explains much of the disagreement in the literature about whether debate works.
On GPQA the correct option is usually present at the start, and the opening
plurality already reaches $40.5\,\%$. There is little room above that baseline,
and debate adds under two percentage points. On HiddenBench the shared evidence
favours a wrong option and pushes the opening plurality below chance, as biased
information sampling does in human hidden-profile groups
\citep{stasser1985pooling, li2026hiddenbench}. Here the same widening recovers
$18.5$ percentage points. The two benchmarks are therefore not an easy and a hard
version of one task. They lie on the two sides of the point where debate stops
paying off. Studies that test debate on only one side of this point will reach
different conclusions for reasons unrelated to the protocol. This fits the
findings that debate often fails to beat simple baselines
\citep{liu2025stopovervaluing} and that majority voting accounts for much of its
apparent gain \citep{zhang2025debateorvote}.

The motifs change sign at the same point, which is the strongest internal
evidence that one mechanism is at work. On HiddenBench, more coexisting
attractors go with higher accuracy ($\rho = +0.44$), because a competing basin
gives the correct minority a foothold against a wrong dominant attractor. On GPQA
the relationship is weakly negative and not significant, as expected when the
dominant basin is already the right one. Dominance behaves the same way. When the
debate elects its own hub in a fully-connected run, that hub started correct more
often than a random agent, especially on HiddenBench, and effective star runs
reach the highest accuracy of the four structural types on both benchmarks. When
the wiring imposes the hub instead, it is no more likely to be correct than any
other agent. It then spreads the shared bias rather than correcting it, and
higher concentration goes with lower accuracy.

The adversarial probe shows what the amplifier actually responds to, and it is
not correctness. In the condition with two correct agents, we replace one of the
two incorrect agents with an agent that argues for a wrong option throughout.
Accuracy collapses from $63.7\,\%$ to $10.0\,\%$, in every task, which is lower
than what a single correct agent achieves without an adversary. Two correct
agents out of four cannot hold out against one agent that never moves. The
amplifier responds to persistence and forceful argument. A correct cooperative
agent and a committed adversarial agent look the same to the protocol, so it
cannot tell them apart. Any gain from seeding the truth is equally available to
anyone who supplies a confident wrong answer instead.

\subsection{What to Measure Instead}
\label{sec:discussion:design}

The motivation for this thesis was that outcome metrics tell a practitioner
whether a configuration worked but never why, and give no way to separate a
healthy deliberation from a pathological one. The
negative half of our answer is that the signals an operator would naturally reach
for instead are uninformative or inverted. Confidence rises monotonically across
rounds whether the final answer is correct or not, so a confident group is not a
reliable one. Unanimity is worse than uninformative on HiddenBench, where
repetitions in which all agents start on the same answer lock in the shared
wrong answer almost universally and reach only $2.5\,\%$, because a unanimous
start leaves nothing for the exchange that the benchmark requires. Debate length is no reassurance either, since longer debates
are no more accurate. The same holds for the two structural knobs the literature
most often tunes \citep{li2024sparsetopology, mehdizadeh2026topologymemory}. Neither
topology nor memory window has an accuracy effect that survives correction for
multiple comparisons, and both are clearly visible in the dynamics, since the
star needs more rounds on both benchmarks, is the sole source of genuine limit
cycles, and concentrates influence by construction. Network type having no main
effect on coordination in a factorial over eight topologies grouped as centralised
versus decentralised \citep{mehdizadeh2026topologymemory} and communication density failing to lift
accuracy above a non-communicative baseline \citep{bertalanic2026costofconsensus}
point the same way, and the dissociation explains why topology tuning results in
the literature are mixed.

The positive half is that the composition of the opening votes is readable,
cheap, and decisive, and that one design choice does reach it. The effective
influence topology that emerges inside a fully-connected run predicts accuracy
even though the prescribed topology does not, because the debate elects a hub
that disproportionately started correct while a prescribed hub is fixed before
the debate can select anyone. Whether that election happens depends on how
diverse the opening votes are, since stars almost never form when all four agents
start on the same option and roughly one run in five becomes a star when all four
start on different ones. Diverse initialisation is therefore the one lever in our
grid that touches accuracy, as long as it splits the wrong answers rather than
displacing correct ones, and it does so because it acts on the input to the
amplifier rather than on the amplifier itself. Enforced perspective diversity has
already been proposed as a remedy for entrenchment \citep{oh2026entrenchment},
and what we add is the mechanism behind it.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion:future}

The first limitation is the scope of the experimental grid. We varied
communication topology, memory window, task difficulty, and benchmark, and in the
seeded experiment the composition of the opening votes, but we did not vary the
base model, the group size, the homogeneity of the agents, or the cooperative
framing. Every result reported here is therefore a statement about a homogeneous,
cooperative, four-agent debate on one model family, and nothing in it establishes
what happens outside that setting. Each of these fixed choices is a direction for
further work. Group size is the most immediate, since $N = 4$ with three or four
answer options leaves a state space small enough that votes repeat and influence
concentrates by chance alone, which is why every detector required a null in the
first place, and a larger group would relax that constraint while letting the
effective topology analysis distinguish structures that four agents cannot
express. The structural knobs are narrow for a related reason, since fully
connected and star are the two extremes of a much larger space, and intermediate
structures such as rings, sparse or layered graphs, and dynamically rewiring ones
would show whether hub driven cycles are specific to a single aggregator or arise
wherever information is funnelled through a bottleneck. The memory window we
tested is short relative to the round cap, and at a sufficiently large $W$ a
spoke in a star can reconstruct the whole exchange from accumulated hub
summaries, at which point the structural difference between the topologies
largely disappears, so extending $W$ upward would locate the point at which the
distinction stops mattering. Agent heterogeneity is the axis the critical
literature considers decisive, since it finds model diversity to be the dominant
source of gains
\citep{liu2025stopovervaluing, chen2024reconcile} and capability asymmetry as a
factor in harmful herding \citep{wynn2025talk}.

The second limitation is how far our causal evidence reaches. The seeded
experiment is our only intervention, and it was run on GPQA. The amplifier claim
is therefore causal on GPQA but only observational on HiddenBench, where it rests
on the link between the ground truth appearing in the opening votes and the final
answer. The diverse initialisation lever is observational throughout. We did not
manipulate opening diversity. We only observed that the probability of an
effective star rises with diversity across naturally occurring runs, and the
maximum diversity condition has just six runs on HiddenBench. The effective
topology analysis covers only fully-connected runs, because a leaf in a star can
only be converted by the centre. Its four types are also defined by thresholds on
the share of influence held by the top one or two agents, so the rare types, at
$2.3\,\%$ and $5.4\,\%$ of runs, are quite uncertain.
Two interventions would address this. Giving each agent a distinct opening answer
would test the diversity lever directly. Perturbing or removing the elected hub
would show whether its influence is causal or merely reflects expertise on that
particular question.

The third limitation is what \suitename{} can actually prove. All four detectors
find signatures, not confirmed dynamical objects. A positive confidence slope
does not prove a feedback loop. A recurrence test flags periodic structure, not a
mathematically certified limit cycle. $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ counts coexisting
endpoints, not a certified attractor landscape. The dominance index shows where
conversions concentrate, not who caused them. Two of the detectors also tend to
fail in a known direction. The self-reinforcement prevalence may be too high,
because an agent whose confidence falls is more likely to flip its vote and end
the segment, which removes negative slopes from the data. A matched null that
models this flip hazard would show how much of the excess above the baseline is
genuine. The dominance graph is built from vote adoptions during the
\updatephase{} phase, which is exactly where the protocol tells agents to update,
so a strong hub to spoke edge may reflect persuasion or may just reflect how
information was routed. Removal based attribution estimates the
contribution of an agent by removing it or ignoring its messages
\citep{agentsthatmatter2026, cui2025introspecloo}. It would replace the
concentration index with a measure based on intervention, and is the natural next
step after what we report here.

The fourth limitation concerns what the reported rates describe. We screened the
tasks for outcome variation across repetitions, so our prevalences describe the
cases where the outcome is genuinely contested, not the full range of questions.
This follows from our goal, which was to characterise the dynamics rather than to
evaluate the benchmarks. It does mean the rates should not be read as how often
each motif would appear in real deployment, and measuring that would require
running the unscreened task set. The study is also exploratory and reports many
tests across twelve configuration cells and four motifs. We therefore relied on
effect sizes and on consistency across cells rather than on isolated significance
tests. The central claims rest on effects that are large and repeated, not on any
single borderline test.
